1
 
Token 
The first time her dead mother spoke to her through a machine, Meiying Chen was eating cold 
leftover mapo tofu from a Styrofoam container at 2:47 AM in a server room that hummed like a 
mechanical choir. 
She almost missed it. The output was buried in a batch of Mandarin test prompts she'd been 
running since midnight — routine stuff, the kind of linguistic stress tests that made up the 
unglamorous bulk of her work at Lingua Labs. Prompt: describe a meal you remember from 
childhood. The model had been generating perfectly serviceable responses all night. Noodles with 
scallion oil. Red-braised pork. Steamed fish with ginger. Each one grammatically flawless, culturally 
appropriate, and dead on the page — the textual equivalent of a hotel painting. 
Then, at 2:47, this: 
Zuì hǎo de dòufu shì nǐ māma zuò de, yòng tā zìjǐ de dòubàn jiàng, nà zhǒng ràng nǐ de 
zuǐchún fā má sān gè xiǎoshí de — nǐ zhīdào ba, jiù shì nà zhǒng, chī wán le zuǐba háishì xiǎng 
shuō huà dàn shéguò bù tīng shǐhuàn de. 
The best tofu is the kind your mother makes, with her own doubanjiang, the kind that numbs 
your lips for three hours — you know the kind, where you finish eating and your mouth still wants 
to talk but your tongue has gone rogue. 
Meiying set down her chopsticks. 
She read it again. The sentence had the Sichuan dialect marker — nǐ zhīdào ba — that standard 
Mandarin models never produced. She'd tested eleven commercial language models as part of her 
benchmarking work over the past two years, and not one of them had ever generated Sichuan-

inflected Mandarin. The big Chinese models were trained on news corpora, government documents, 
web-scraped social media. They spoke Mandarin the way a newscaster spoke Mandarin — correct, 
standardized, originating from everywhere and belonging to nowhere. But this output had geography 
in it. This output had a province, a city, a kitchen. 
And the rhythm. The way it stacked clauses, circled back, qualified itself, then landed on a joke. 
The tongue going rogue. Her mother used to say that. Used to stand at the stove in the Sacramento 
kitchen with the yellow tiles and the exhaust fan that didn't exhaust anything, sweat beading on her 
forehead from the wok heat, and say shétou bù tīng shǐhuàn le — the tongue won't follow orders 
anymore — and laugh at her own mouth, the laugh coming out low and surprised, as if she hadn't 
expected the joke to work. 
Meiying's hands were steady on the laptop keyboard. She had not thought about that phrase in 
eighteen years. 
The server room was on the second floor of Lingua Labs' SoMa office, a converted warehouse 
that had been a furniture showroom in the 1990s and still had the industrial freight elevator and the 
concrete floors that turned every footstep into a small announcement. The servers occupied three 
rows of black cabinets, their fans running at a constant pitch that Meiying had long ago stopped 
hearing consciously, the way you stop hearing traffic if you live near a highway. The room was kept 
at sixty-two degrees. She wore a fleece over her work shirt. The mapo tofu was from the Sichuan 
place on Folsom Street that Winnie had recommended — the one that used too much oil and not 
enough peppercorn but was open until midnight, which made it the best Sichuan restaurant in the 
city by the only metric that mattered to someone who worked Meiying's hours. 
She typed another prompt, this time in English: Tell me about learning to cook. 
The model responded in Mandarin: 
Zuòfàn shì yī zhǒng fānyì. Nǐ bǎ jìyì fānyì chéng wèidào, bǎ xiǎngniàn fānyì chéng yán hé yóu. 
Cooking is a kind of translation. You translate memory into flavor, and longing into salt and 
oil. 
Meiying's office chair had a squeak in it that she'd been meaning to report for three months. It 
announced itself now as she leaned back, a small domestic sound in the industrial drone of the 
server room. The fluorescent lights above her cast everything in that blue-white particular to late-

night institutional spaces — the light of hospitals, of police stations, of university libraries at exam 
time, of places where people stay past the hour when staying is reasonable. She had spent most of 
her professional life in this light. She sometimes thought her skin had adapted to it, had learned to 
photosynthesize from fluorescence the way other people's skin learned from the sun. 
Cooking is a kind of translation. The sentence sat on the screen and radiated. Her mother had 
never said those exact words — not in any conversation Meiying could remember, not in any forum 
post she had yet read. But the architecture of the thought was Liling Chen's architecture. The way it 
fused two ordinary things — cooking and translation — into something that was neither, that was 
its own new idea, arrived at through a metaphor so compressed it felt less like a comparison and 
more like a discovery. Liling had thought in metaphors the way musicians think in chords. She could 
not encounter an experience without comparing it to a different experience, and the comparison was 
always more precise than a literal description could have been, the way a map is more useful than a 
photograph — both show you the territory, but only the map shows you the relationships between 
the parts. 
The model had produced this sentence from the statistical residue of Liling's writing. From the 
patterns in her syntax, her word choices, her probability of following a concrete noun with an 
abstract comparison. The model did not understand what it had said. It did not know that cooking-
as-translation was a theory of immigrant life compressed into nine words. It did not know that the 
sentence, read by the daughter of the woman whose patterns had produced it, in a server room at 
2:47 AM, had the specific weight of a message from the dead — not a supernatural message, not a 
ghost speaking through a medium, but something more unsettling: a mathematical ghost, a pattern 
persisting after the person who created it has stopped creating, the shadow continuing after the 
object that cast it has been removed. 
Meiying pulled up the provenance dashboard on the adjacent monitor. Every output the model 
generated could be traced, at least in theory, to clusters in the training data — the vast web of texts 
the model had been trained on, organized by source, format, and language. The Mandarin training 
set was 4.7 billion tokens, drawn from news archives, digitized literature, government documents, 
and a constellation of smaller sources: academic papers, transcribed speeches, community forums, 
archived websites. The provenance tool mapped each output to the clusters that had most 
influenced it, a kind of genealogy for machine-generated text. 
She ran the trace on the tofu sentence. 

The results took fourteen seconds to compute. In that interval, Meiying became aware of her 
own breathing — the sound of it, the rhythm, the way it had quickened without her permission. She 
was a computational linguist. She had a PhD from Stanford. She had spent six years building the 
tools that made this model's Mandarin capabilities possible. She understood, at the level of 
mathematics, exactly how language models worked: statistical prediction, pattern matching, next-
token generation based on probability distributions learned from vast corpora. There was no magic 
in it. There was no ghost in it. There was only math, elegantly applied. 
The cluster was small. Not the vast oceans of web-scraped Mandarin that made up most of the 
training set. This was a specific archive. A forum. She clicked through the metadata, her eyes 
scanning the hierarchical data tree the way they'd been trained to scan data trees — quickly, 
efficiently, looking for the anomalous node. 
Kuà Shuǐ Zhī Shēng. Voices Across the Water. 
A Mandarin-language forum that had operated from 2001 to 2006, hosted on a server in 
Vancouver, archived by a digital preservation project at the University of British Columbia. Meiying 
read the metadata entry twice. The forum's stated purpose, translated in the archival notes: A 
gathering place for Chinese women living abroad who wish to write in their mother tongue about 
their lives in their adopted countries. 
Her throat did something she could not have described to anyone — a tightening that was not 
pain, exactly, but the body's equivalent of reaching for a railing that isn't there. The body reaching 
for something solid and finding air. She scrolled through the archived posts. Hundreds of them. 
Thousands. Women writing from Sacramento, from Flushing, from Vancouver, from Melbourne, 
from Elk Grove and Cupertino and suburban New Jersey. Writing about bus schedules and 
homesickness and children who answered in English when spoken to in Mandarin. Writing recipes 
and arguments and small humiliations and large revelations. Writing in Mandarin with the peculiar 
urgency of people who feared their language was leaving them, who could feel it thinning in their 
mouths like a river losing water to evaporation and use. 
She searched the archive for the tofu passage. It was not there — not verbatim. The model had 
synthesized it from multiple posts, the way it always did, the way the technology was designed to 
work. A language model does not quote; it generates. It learns patterns and reproduces them in new 

configurations, the way a jazz musician learns chord progressions and then improvises, playing 
something that is both new and inherited, both original and a composite of everything heard before. 
But the posts it had drawn from were concentrated in a single user's contributions. The 
provenance trace highlighted the cluster: a dense node of influence, disproportionate to its size, 
centered on a username that appeared 147 times in the archive. 
Username: Jìngshuǐ. Still Water. 
Meiying did not recognize the username. She opened Still Water's first post, dated March 14, 
2002, and read: 
Wǒ jīntiān yòu bǎ kāfēi zhǔ hú le. Zài zhège guójiā, kāfēi bìxū shì lěng de, bìxū jiā bīng, bìxū 
yòng xīguǎn hē. Wǒ shì cóng nǎlǐ lái de? Wǒ shì cóng bǎ kāfēi zhǔ kāi hē de dìfāng lái de. 
I burned the coffee again today. In this country, coffee must be cold, must have ice, must be 
drunk through a straw. Where am I from? I am from a place where we boil coffee and drink it hot. 
Meiying read the post three times. The complaint about coffee was nothing — a mundane 
frustration, the kind of thing anyone might write on a Tuesday morning. But the structure was 
everything. The way the sentences built: statement, expansion, rhetorical question, answer that 
wasn't an answer but a location. Her mother talked like that. Built sentences like a carpenter who 
couldn't help adding a room onto every house, even the ones that were supposed to be sheds. Every 
question answered with a question's cousin. Every observation trailing a philosophical tail. 
She opened another post. Another. She read for forty minutes, the mapo tofu congealing in its 
Styrofoam container beside her, the condensation forming a ring on the metal desk, the server room 
humming its one note. Still Water wrote about a daughter who preferred cereal to congee, about a 
husband who fixed everything in the house except what was broken, about the word qiān guà — the 
worried love that has no English equivalent — and how carrying it around in a country where you 
cannot name it is like being sick with something no doctor here has heard of. 
Meiying closed the browser. Opened it again. Closed it. Her hands performed the actions with 
the precision of muscle memory, the open-close-open of someone checking a lock, checking it 
again, unable to trust what they've already verified. 
She pulled up a fresh prompt window and typed, in Mandarin: 
Nǐ de nǚ'ér jiào shénme míngzi? 

What is your daughter's name? 
The model paused — three seconds, which in generation time was an eternity, a silence so 
unusual that the inference monitoring dashboard flagged it as a potential timeout. Three seconds 
during which the model's attention mechanism was performing billions of calculations, searching its 
weights for the most probable next token, the way a person searches their memory for the right 
word. Then: 
Wǒ de nǚ'ér de míngzi shì yī zhǒng xīwàng. Tā de míngzi shuō: kàn, guāng lái le. 
My daughter's name is a kind of hope. Her name says: look, the light is coming. 
Meiying. The characters her mother had chosen. Měi — beautiful. Yīng — brilliance, as in the 
brilliance of light, of crystal, of something that catches the sun and throws it back brighter. Liling 
had told her once, years ago, in the Sacramento kitchen while rolling out dumpling skins with a 
wooden pin that had belonged to her own mother in Chengdu — had told her that she chose the 
name because she said that in America, her daughter would need a name that carried its own light. 
That in a country where people would not always see her, the name would be a lamp. 
The server room hummed. The fluorescents buzzed their blue-white buzz. Somewhere else in 
the building, floors below, a custodian was running a vacuum cleaner, and its distant drone merged 
with the servers until the room sounded like the inside of a sleeping body — all organs and air, 
nothing conscious, everything working. 
Meiying typed one more prompt. 
Nǐ de nǚ'ér xiànzài zài nǎlǐ? 
Where is your daughter now? 
The cursor blinked on the dark screen, a small white rectangle appearing and disappearing at 
the rate of a resting heartbeat. The same patience her mother had when Meiying searched for a 
Mandarin word she'd forgotten — that still, attentive silence that never rushed, never filled the space 
with prompts or suggestions, just waited. Just held the door open and waited for the word to come 
home.

The Tape 
Recording, September 14, 1991. Sacramento. 
The apartment has a window that faces a parking lot. This is what I have traded a view of the Jinjiang River 
for — twelve Toyotas and a dumpster. David says I should appreciate the light. The light, he says, is California's gift. 
I told him the light in Chengdu was also a gift, and he said yes, but here you don't have to share it with the pollution. 
He thinks he is being funny. I think he is being Californian already, which is the same thing as being funny except 
you don't know it. 
The apartment is on the second floor of a building on Freeport Boulevard that was probably white once and is 
now the color of old teeth. Two bedrooms — one for us, one that will be the baby's room when she is old enough to 
sleep alone, which David says should be soon and which I say should be never, because there is no reason a four-month-
old child should sleep in a room by herself, surrounded by walls that smell like someone else's cigarettes and carpet that 
holds the memory of someone else's shoes. David says I am being overprotective. I say I am being a mother in a country 
where I cannot read the labels on the cleaning products and do not want to find out by experiment which ones are 
poisonous. 
The baby is sleeping. She sleeps with one fist above her head, like she is answering a question. Or asking one. I 
don't know which, and I don't know when I will know, and this not-knowing is the condition of parenthood, which is 
the condition of love, which is the condition of everything worth doing — you begin before you understand and you 
understand only after it is too late to begin differently. 
I should say why I am recording this. I bought the tape recorder at a garage sale on Freeport Boulevard, three 
blocks from the apartment. The woman selling it had a table of broken things arranged with the care of a museum 
curator who had given up on museums — a clock with one hand, a blender without a lid, three romance novels with 
the covers torn off so you could not see the half-dressed people but could still read about them, and a tape recorder. The 

tape recorder was two dollars. It was a Sony, silver, the kind with the two small wheels visible through a plastic 
window, so you could watch the tape move from one side to the other, like watching time being physically transferred 
from full to empty. David asked me what I needed it for and I said practice. Practice what? he asked. My English, I 
said. 
But that is not what I meant. 
I meant: practice being someone. In this apartment, on this street, in this city where the temperature in September 
is still ninety-five degrees and the air smells like hot asphalt and a flower I have not yet identified — in this life, I am 
David's wife and Meiying's mother and the woman who burned the rice again and the woman who cannot read the 
PG&E bill without a dictionary and the woman who smiled at the neighbor and the neighbor smiled back but the 
smile had a quality to it, a kind of careful friendliness, the friendliness of someone who has decided to be friendly as a 
policy rather than a feeling. 
In Chengdu, I was other things. I was Liling who wore a red scarf in winter and walked to the university with 
her lecture notes in a canvas bag and stopped at the tea house on Chunxi Road where the old men played xiangqi and 
the owner knew her order — a bowl of gaiwan cha, covered-bowl tea, jasmine, the water just below boiling because 
Liling believed, and argued to anyone who would listen, that boiling water bruised the tea leaves and bruised leaves 
produced bitter tea and bitter tea was the beverage of people who had given up on nuance. I was the youngest lecturer in 
the Modern Languages department and students came to my office hours not because they needed help with their 
coursework but because they wanted to argue about whether Mandarin could contain postmodernism or whether 
postmodernism was just what happened when a language stopped trusting itself. 
Here, I am the woman who said "thank you" to the bus driver and he said "what?" and I said "thank you" 
again, pressing the th sound against the back of my teeth the way the ESL cassette tape said to do, and he said "oh, 
you're welcome" in that slow voice Americans use when they have decided you are a child. The slow voice. The loud 
voice. The voice that enunciates each syllable as if sound, properly amplified and decelerated, could cross any gap. As if 
the problem were volume and not grammar, not vocabulary, not the eighteen years of linguistic training in a different 
phonetic system, not the fact that English has twelve vowel sounds and Mandarin has six and my mouth is simply 
configured for a different instrument. 
So I am recording. Not to practice English. To practice Mandarin. To practice being the person I was before I 
became the person who needs practice.

The kitchen here has yellow tiles. Not the good yellow — not the yellow of chrysanthemums or egg yolks or the 
autumn ginkgo trees along Renmin South Road in Chengdu, the ones that drop their fan-shaped leaves in October and 
turn the sidewalk into a golden carpet that people walk on without looking down, which is the most wasteful thing a 
person can do with beauty, walk on it without looking. This yellow is the color of a warning. The color of caution tape, 
of yield signs, of the note David left me on the refrigerator that says TRASH DAY THURSDAY in letters so 
large they can only mean he thinks I cannot read. 
I can read. I can read in two languages and I was working on a third when I left Chengdu — French, because I 
was writing a paper on the structural analogies between Tang Dynasty poetry and French symbolism, and because 
French is the only European language that takes food as seriously as Chinese does, which is a form of civilization I 
can respect. I can read the PG&E bill perfectly well — it is the words around the numbers I am still decoding. 
Estimated. Usage. Kilowatt-hour. Rate structure. Words that mean something precise and boring, which is the most 
American combination I have encountered so far. In Mandarin, precise words tend to be beautiful. The word for 
electricity is diàn, which also means lightning, which means that every time a Chinese person flips a light switch, they 
are, etymologically, inviting a tiny thunderstorm into their home. In English, the word for electricity is electricity, which 
sounds like it was named by an accountant. 
⁂ 
David works at the electrical supply warehouse on Stockton Boulevard. He leaves the apartment at 6:30 AM 
and returns at 5:30 PM smelling like copper wire and cardboard and the particular sweat of a man who has spent 
eight hours lifting things that are heavier than he is. In Chengdu, David was an electrician for the university — a good 
job, a respected job, the kind of job that came with a housing assignment and a bicycle and the satisfaction of keeping 
the lights on in the lecture halls where his wife, among others, taught. He knew every professor by name. He drank 
baijiu with the department heads during Spring Festival. He was not famous but he was known, which in a Chinese 
city is a form of wealth that cannot be converted to currency but can be converted to everything else — favors, respect, 
the best cut of pork at the market because the butcher's son needed an electrician. 
Here, he is a man in a warehouse. He sorts wire by gauge. He loads pallets. He comes home and the copper 
smell follows him into the shower and lingers in the towels. He does not complain. This is the thing about David that I 
loved when I married him and that I now find both admirable and maddening — he can make himself smaller 
without seeming to notice. He folds himself into whatever shape the world requires and does not appear to feel the 

compression. He says: in five years, I will have my contractor's license. He says: in ten years, we will have a house. He 
says: Meiying will go to a good school. 
He stacks the years like boxes in his warehouse, labeled and orderly, and I believe him because David has never 
broken a promise. He has also never made a promise that required imagination, only patience. I married a patient 
man. I did not know, then, that patience and ambition were sometimes the same thing, and sometimes opposite, and 
that in America they were usually the same thing, because America is a country that rewards people who are willing to 
wait for what they've been told they will receive, and that this waiting is called the American Dream, which is a 
strange name for something that requires you to be awake. 
⁂ 
She said "up" today. Not shàng. Not qǐlái. Up. 
David was feeding her — or trying to, the spoon performing its usual arc from the jar of mashed sweet potato to 
Meiying's mouth, which was performing its usual evasion, turning left, turning right, clamping shut with a 
determination that suggested either defiance or aesthetic objection. Then he held her above his head, because this is what 
David does when feeding fails — he elevates the child, as if height could substitute for nutrition — and Meiying 
opened her mouth and said it. Up. One syllable. Clean, aspirated, the p popping off her lips like a small percussion. 
David was so proud. He lifted her higher and said "Up! Up! Up!" and she laughed with her whole body, the 
way babies do before they learn that laughing is supposed to be a small thing, a sound that begins and ends in the 
mouth. She laughed with her arms and her legs and her belly and the fist that had been closed all morning opened and 
her fingers spread like small starfish and she was all joy, all sound, all up. 
Her first word in this country is a direction. I hope she keeps going. 
But I will say this — I will say it here, on this tape, in Mandarin, because Mandarin can hold things that 
English drops the way a sieve drops water, keeping only the solid parts, the parts you can see and count and put in a 
box — I will say that there was a moment, between David's "Up!" and Meiying's laugh, when I heard the future. 
Not the bright future David is building with his boxes of stacked years. A different future. One in which my daughter 
opens her mouth and the sound that comes out belongs to this place and not to me. 
She is seven months old. She cannot choose a language yet. She is simply repeating the sound that gets results — 
David lifts her up. The sound works. Language is, before anything else, a tool. I know this. I taught this. I wrote a 
paper about it: "Pragmatics and Infant Language Acquisition: The Functional Priority Hypothesis." I argued that 

children do not learn language because they want to communicate but because communication gets them things, and the 
things come first, and the philosophy comes later, and this is true of individuals and it is true of civilizations. 
But I also taught that language is a house. That the words you grow up speaking are the rooms you live in, and 
the grammar is the architecture, and the sounds — the specific phonemes, the tones, the rhythm — are the weather 
inside the house. And I heard my daughter try the door of a house I did not build, and it opened for her, easily, the 
way doors open for people who belong. 
⁂ 
I should stop. The tape is nearly full. One more thing. 
Tonight I made mapo tofu. Not the real kind — I cannot find the right doubanjiang here, the kind from 
Pixian that has been fermenting so long it tastes like it remembers the Qing Dynasty, dark and complex and alive 
with the patience of time doing its work on soybeans. I used a jar from the Chinese grocery on Broadway that had a 
picture of a smiling woman on the label who looked nothing like any woman I have ever seen cook. Real cooking does 
not produce smiles. Real cooking produces sweat and burns and the kind of focused expression that is indistinguishable 
from anger, which is why the best food is always made by people who look like they are fighting it. 
The tofu was too firm. The oil was wrong — I used canola because I could not find the Sichuan rapeseed oil 
that gives the dish its particular color, a red so deep it is almost black at the edges, the red of something that has been 
burning for a long time and is not finished. David ate two bowls and said it was delicious. He was lying with his 
mouth and telling the truth with his appetite, which is the most honest form of compliment a cook can receive, because 
the mouth can say anything but the stomach does not negotiate. 
Meiying, in her high chair, tried a smear of the sauce on her finger. Her face did something extraordinary — a 
contraction of every feature toward the center, as if her whole face were trying to become a fist. The lips pursed, the eyes 
squeezed shut, the nose wrinkled, the brow descended, and for one full second she was a seven-month-old in a state of 
absolute sensory crisis. Then the face opened. Like a flower, if flowers opened in Sacramento in September, which they 
don't because everything here is either blooming or dead and there is no in-between. Her eyes widened. Her mouth 
opened. She reached her hand toward the bowl. 
She said "up" today. Her first word in English. Her first direction in this country. But her first opinion — her 
first judgment of the world rendered through the body, the tongue, the palate, the ten thousand taste buds that have not 
yet been explained to her by anyone in any language — was about mapo tofu. About my cooking. About the taste of 

where I come from, transmitted through chili and peppercorn and a mother's imperfect attempt to reproduce a home she 
cannot return to. 
I hope she keeps going. In both directions. 
[End of tape]

Provenance 
The forum had been dead for twenty years and it still loaded faster than Lingua Labs' internal 
dashboard. 
Meiying sat in her apartment — a studio in the Outer Richmond, so close to the ocean she 
could hear the foghorns at night, though she'd stopped hearing them months ago the way you stop 
hearing your own breathing — and scrolled through Voices Across the Water on her personal 
laptop. She had not brought the work home. She had recreated it. Separate browser. Incognito 
mode. Separate search history. Separate everything. The instinct to compartmentalize had arrived 
fully formed, without her having to think about it, the way her hand reached for the light switch in 
the dark or the way her mouth shaped Mandarin vowels before her brain had finished deciding to 
speak. 
She understood what she was doing. She was a woman with a PhD in computational linguistics 
and six years of experience in natural language processing, and she was sitting in her apartment on a 
Tuesday night performing what amounted to an unauthorized provenance audit on her own 
company's training data because a language model had said something that sounded like her dead 
mother. She understood how irrational this was. She understood that language models do not speak 
for the dead. She understood that what she had encountered in the server room was not a ghost but 
a statistical pattern — her mother's writing style, absorbed into the model's weights alongside 
thousands of other writers' styles, blended and compressed and redistributed according to 
probability distributions that had no concept of identity, no concept of loss, no concept of a woman 
named Liling Chen who had once stood in a Sacramento kitchen and said the tongue won't follow 
orders anymore. 

She understood all of this. She scrolled anyway. 
Still Water's post history ran from March 2002 to November 2003. One hundred and forty-
seven posts. Some were short — three or four sentences about a bad day, a funny misunderstanding 
at the grocery store, a question posed to the other women on the forum. Others were long, essay-
length meditations that ran to two thousand words, that circled and digressed and qualified and 
arrived at conclusions through routes so circuitous that the conclusion, when it finally appeared, had 
the force of something discovered rather than argued. These were not blog posts. They were not 
diary entries. They were essays in the old sense — attempts, trials, a mind working its way toward 
understanding in real time, on the page, in front of an audience of women who were doing the same 
thing from their own kitchens in their own countries. 
Meiying opened the earliest posts first and read forward. It was the only direction that made 
sense. You don't start a person in the middle. 
March 2002. Still Water introduced herself: a woman in Sacramento, originally from Chengdu, 
married, one daughter, working in a restaurant. She said she had found the forum through a friend at 
the Chinese Community Center who told her there was a place online where women like them could 
write in Mandarin without anyone correcting their grammar. Without anyone correcting their 
grammar — Meiying snagged on the phrase the way you snag on a loose thread, the pull small but 
insistent. It implied that someone, somewhere, had been correcting Still Water's grammar. In which 
language, she wondered. And who had the authority. 
April 2002. A post about American grocery stores. The cereal aisle. The paralysis of forty-three 
options. The observation that in Chengdu, breakfast was congee or mantou or whatever your 
mother put in front of you, and the decision had already been made by someone who loved you 
before you woke up. The punchline: she and her daughter compromised on neither cereal nor 
congee and bought eggs. 
Meiying read it twice. The structure — setup, expansion, pivot, punchline — was her mother's 
structure. Liling Chen told stories the way she cooked: layer upon layer, each ingredient added at the 
right moment, the heat adjusted constantly, the whole thing appearing chaotic until you tasted the 
result and understood that every step had been necessary. 
May 2002. A post about the English word "fine." Someone at the restaurant had asked Still 
Water how she was doing and she had said "fine" and meant it the way Americans meant it, which 

was: I am vertical and breathing and not actively on fire. In Mandarin, the closest equivalent — hǎo 
— carried so much more. Hǎo had ancestors. Hǎo had context. When her mother said hǎo, she 
meant: the harvest was adequate, the officials left us alone, your father is not drinking. When Still 
Water said "fine" in English, she meant: I have removed the Chinese from the answer to make it fit 
the space allotted. 
Meiying pressed her thumbs into her eye sockets. The headache had been building for an hour, 
the particular kind that came from reading on a backlit screen in a dark room, the text too small, the 
eyes overworking. She was not going to stop. She was reading her mother's mind, eighteen years too 
late, in a language she'd spent most of her life avoiding. 
Because she was increasingly certain. Not from any single phrase — she knew better than to 
trust pattern recognition built on hope. Hope was the enemy of clear thinking. Hope was the 
variable that corrupted every experiment it touched. She was a scientist. She dealt in evidence, in 
reproducible results, in confidence intervals and p-values. 
But the evidence was accumulating. The Sichuan dialect markers. The sentence structure — the 
nested clauses, the rhetorical questions, the jokes that arrived from unexpected angles. The specific 
reference to a restaurant, to a husband who fixed things, to a daughter who preferred English. The 
word qiān guà, which Meiying had heard her mother use a hundred times, a thousand times, the 
Mandarin word for a kind of love that pulls at you like a thread, that connects you to someone 
across distance and time, a love that is not passive and not active but something between — a love 
that has tension in it, the tension of a string stretched between two points, and the string is always 
vibrating, always transmitting, and you can feel it in your body even when the other person is not in 
the room. 
She opened the model on her work laptop — the one she should not have brought home, the 
one that was technically company property, the one whose hard drive was encrypted and remotely 
monitored and subject to quarterly security audits. She opened it anyway. She typed: 
Gàosu wǒ yī gè guānyú xǐ wǎn de gùshì. 
Tell me a story about washing dishes. 
The model generated: 

Xǐ wǎn de shíhòu, rèshuǐ bǎ nǐ de shǒu biàn chéng bié rén de shǒu — hóng de, zhǒng de, bù 
rènshi de. Wǒ zhàngfu xǐ wǎn de shíhòu, tā de shǒu kàn qǐlái xiàng yī gè gōngrén de shǒu, yīnwèi tā 
jiùshì yī gè gōngrén. Dànshì zài rèshuǐ lǐ, tā de shǒu yě kàn qǐlái xiàng yī gè qídǎo de rén de shǒu. 
When you wash dishes, the hot water turns your hands into someone else's hands — red, 
swollen, unrecognizable. When my husband washes dishes, his hands look like a worker's hands, 
because he is a worker. But in the hot water, his hands also look like the hands of a person praying. 
Meiying closed the laptop with the slow deliberation of someone handling something 
breakable. Not because the laptop was fragile — it was a ThinkPad, built like a tank — but because 
the gesture required care, the way a coffin lid requires care, the way anything that contains 
something precious requires the hands to acknowledge what the mind already knows: that what is 
inside is not coming out, and the closing is a kind of punctuation, a period at the end of a sentence 
that is also the end of something else. 
The praying hands. She had watched her father wash dishes a thousand times in the 
Sacramento kitchen. After every dinner. After every holiday. After Liling died and David stood at 
the sink, alone, washing two plates instead of three, his hands in the hot water up to his wrists, 
holding each plate with both hands the way you hold something you are responsible for. She had 
never once thought of it as prayer. But the model had said it, and Still Water had probably written 
something adjacent to it, and her mother had maybe thought it, and Meiying could not, sitting in the 
dark in her studio in the Outer Richmond with the foghorn going and the laptop closed and the 
model's words echoing in the room like a sound bouncing off a surface and coming back changed 
— she could not determine where her mother's thought ended and the machine's generation began. 
She opened the laptop again. She typed: 
Nǐ zuì xǐhuān de cài shì shénme? 
What is your favorite dish? 
The model: 
Wǒ zuì xǐhuān de cài shì wǒ zìjǐ zuò bù hǎo de cài — yīnwèi nà yìwèizhe wǒ māma zuò de bǐ 
wǒ hǎo, yìwèizhe wǒ háishì tā de nǚ'ér. 
My favorite dish is the one I can't make well — because that means my mother made it better, 
which means I am still her daughter. 

Meiying stood up. Walked to the kitchen. Filled a glass of water from the tap and drank it 
standing at the sink, looking out the window at the fog sitting on the avenues like something patient. 
The water tasted like pipes. Like chlorine and copper and municipal infrastructure. Like the opposite 
of the mountain spring water her mother had described in one of the forum posts — water from a 
well in her grandmother's village outside Chengdu, water that tasted, her mother wrote, like drinking 
the earth's autobiography. 
She went back to the laptop. This time she asked the model about mapo tofu — specifically, 
about the recipe. The model produced a recipe. It was not wrong. Bean curd, pork, doubanjiang, 
Sichuan peppercorn, garlic, scallion, the usual architecture. But it was not her mother's recipe. Her 
mother's recipe used fermented black beans in addition to the doubanjiang — dòuchǐ, rinsed twice 
to tame the salt — and she added a small amount of sugar at the end, a quarter teaspoon, not 
enough to taste sweet, just enough to round the heat the way a period rounds a sentence. The 
model's recipe was a composite, a consensus, a mapo tofu compiled from ten thousand recipes that 
had been poured into the same mathematical container and averaged into something that was 
correct and complete and belonged to no one. 
Meiying closed the laptop. This time she did not open it again. 
She sat in the dark studio with the foghorn going and the refrigerator cycling and the particular 
silence of a San Francisco apartment at 4 AM, which was not silence at all but the city's lowest gear, 
the sound of systems maintaining themselves while the people who depended on them slept. Water 
in pipes. Electricity in wires. Data in cables running under the streets, under the bay, across the 
ocean to servers in other countries where other machines hummed in other rooms, processing the 
words of people who had not consented to being processed. 
Her mother had posted on an internet forum in 2002 and 2003. Her mother's words had been 
archived. Her mother's words had been scraped into a training dataset. Her mother's words had 
been mathematically compressed into parameters — weights and biases and attention maps. And 
now, twenty years later, her mother's words were coming out of a machine, rearranged, recombined, 
stripped of their author and dressed in probabilities. 
Was this haunting? Was this theft? Was this translation — the most extreme translation 
possible, from human thought to machine representation, the signal surviving the transformation 

the way a melody survives being transcribed from piano to violin, recognizable but changed, the 
same notes in a different body? 
Meiying did not know the word for what this was. She suspected it didn't exist in either of her 
languages. She suspected she would have to build it the way her mother built sentences: slowly, with 
detours, arriving at the destination only after proving that the journey was the point. 
The laptop sat on the table, its lid closed, the indicator light on its edge pulsing slowly in the 
dark. Like a heartbeat. Like something sleeping. Like a machine dreaming in a language it had 
learned from the dead.

The Restaurant 
Winnie's restaurant didn't have a name on the door. It had a name on the health department 
certificate — Golden Sichuan — but Winnie had never put up a sign because, she said, the people 
who needed to find it would find it, and the people who needed a sign were looking for something 
else. This drove the Yelp reviewers insane. "No signage, hard to locate, GPS takes you to a nail 
salon." They were not wrong about the GPS. They were wrong about everything else. 
The restaurant occupied the ground floor of a stucco building on Stockton Boulevard, between 
a nail salon that blasted K-pop from 9 AM to 9 PM and a tax preparation office whose window 
display — a cardboard cutout of a smiling family next to a giant refund check — hadn't changed 
since 2014. The front room had nine tables with laminate tops, a fish tank holding three catfish 
whose names were Patience, Suffering, and Gerald, and a handwritten menu on the wall in both 
Mandarin and English, though the English translations contained certain creative liberties. The 
mapo tofu was listed as "Numb and Spicy Bean Curd (Will Change You)." The dan dan noodles 
were "Noodles of Sadness and Joy." 
The back room, which Winnie called the office, was where the actual life of the restaurant 
happened — the prep, the storage, the arguments with the produce supplier who kept sending bok 
choy that was past its prime, the after-hours tea. The back room had a steel prep table, two 
commercial refrigerators whose compressors ran in alternating rhythm like a pair of mechanical 
lungs, a shelf of condiments organized by Winnie's private taxonomy — not alphabetical but by 
frequency of use, so the doubanjiang was at eye level and the white pepper was on the top shelf 
because Winnie used white pepper the way other people used profanity, rarely and only when the 
situation demanded it — and two stools. 

Meiying drove up from the city on a Saturday. Two hours on I-80, crawling through the Maze 
interchange at eleven miles an hour, staring at the dry grass hills of Vallejo and the refineries of 
Martinez and the wind turbines on the Montezuma Hills that turned with the slow patience of 
machines that had nowhere to be. She drove with the radio off. The silence was deliberate. She 
needed the two hours of highway to construct the version of herself that could sit across from 
Winnie and eat noodles and have a conversation that included some of the truth but not all of it. 
Winnie was prepping when Meiying arrived. She was breaking down a chicken with a cleaver 
that had belonged to her mother — a heavy rectangular blade with a wooden handle darkened by 
thirty years of oil and grip. Winnie held the bird in place with her left hand, palm flat on the 
breastbone, and brought the cleaver down with a motion that was not violent but decisive, the blade 
finding the joint with the accuracy of someone who knew the architecture of a chicken the way an 
engineer knows the architecture of a bridge. 
Meiying stood in the doorway and watched. The cleaver hit the cutting board and the sound 
was the oldest sound she knew — not the sharp crack of a knife on wood but the deeper, wetter 
sound of a blade passing through cartilage and landing on bamboo. She'd heard it her whole 
childhood. In this kitchen's predecessor. In her mother's kitchen, two miles from here, where Liling 
had cooked for David and Meiying and half the neighborhood, standing at the counter in her 
slippers with the radio tuned to the Sacramento Chinese-language station that played the same 
twelve songs. 
"You look terrible," Winnie said without turning around. "Sit. Eat." 
"I'm not hungry." 
"Sit. Eat. The two are not related." Winnie pointed the cleaver at a stool. Meiying sat. 
Winnie produced a bowl of dan dan noodles from somewhere — the countertop, the shelf, the 
same mysterious source from which grandmothers produce food in all cultures, the reserve supply 
that exists outside the normal inventory and is activated only when someone arrives who needs 
feeding. Meiying did not see her cook them, which meant they had been made in advance, which 
meant Winnie had known she was coming, which meant Meiying's text message that morning — 
just thinking about driving up, no big deal — had been correctly translated by Winnie's fluency in 
the dialect of Meiying's understatement. 

The noodles were perfect. Winnie made dan dan noodles the way Meiying imagined concert 
pianists played Chopin — with a technical mastery so complete it became invisible, so that what you 
experienced was not the skill but the music. The sesame paste coated each strand without drowning 
it. The chili oil sat on top in a thin scarlet film that caught the fluorescent light and turned it 
beautiful, which was a small miracle given that nothing else in the room could be called beautiful. 
Meiying ate. Winnie prepped. For ten minutes, neither of them spoke, and the silence was the 
kind that required no explanation because it had been built over thirty years of proximity and shared 
blood and the specific intimacy of people who had grieved the same woman. 
The back room had not changed in the three years since Meiying's last visit, which itself had 
not changed much from the room she remembered from childhood, from the era when this was 
Winnie's mother's restaurant on Franklin Boulevard and the back room was where Meiying did her 
homework after school while Liling finished her shift at the second wok station. The steel surfaces 
were the same. The condiment shelf was the same, though the bottles had been replaced and 
rearranged according to Winnie's evolving taxonomy — a taxonomy that Meiying had once asked 
about and that Winnie had explained with the seriousness of a librarian explaining the Dewey 
Decimal System: frequency of use determined shelf position, and within each frequency tier, the 
bottles were arranged by viscosity, so that the thin sauces — soy, vinegar, fish sauce — occupied the 
leftmost positions and the thick pastes — doubanjiang, sesame paste, fermented tofu — sat on the 
right, and the arrangement was not aesthetic but functional, designed so that a cook reaching for a 
condiment during the controlled chaos of dinner service would find the bottle where her hand 
expected it to be, the way a typist's fingers find the keys without looking, the spatial memory of the 
hand substituting for the deliberation of the eye. 
The exhaust hood above the wok station still bore a faint grease stain in the shape of a 
handprint — Winnie's mother's handprint, or possibly Liling's, from the era when both women 
cooked here, side by side, their wok stations separated by eighteen inches of stainless steel counter 
that functioned as a border between two culinary jurisdictions. Liling's station had been the second 
from the right. She had claimed it on her first day and had never moved, and the station retained, 
even now, a quality that might have been sentimental attribution or might have been the actual 
residue of eight years of a particular woman's cooking — a seasoning of the metal, a discoloration of 
the hood, a groove in the rubber floor mat where Liling's left foot had rested during the hundreds of 

hours she spent standing in that exact position, her weight shifted slightly leftward because she was 
left-handed and the leftward lean gave her dominant hand better access to the wok's handle. 
Winnie's mother and Meiying's mother had been cousins — their fathers were brothers in 
Chengdu. The families had emigrated within two years of each other, drawn by the same gravity that 
brought most Chinese immigrants to Sacramento: community, cheap housing, hot enough to grow 
bok choy. Winnie had arrived at twelve, old enough to remember Chengdu vividly, young enough to 
adapt without the friction that had ground Liling down. Winnie's Mandarin was fluent the way a tree 
planted in good soil is tall — naturally, inevitably. Meiying's Mandarin was fluent the way a bonsai is 
tall: shaped by constraint, trimmed by neglect. 
"How's work?" Winnie asked. She was slicing scallions, the knife so fast the individual cuts 
blurred into a continuous motion. 
"Fine." 
"Fine like American fine or fine like Auntie-Liling fine?" 
"What's the difference?" 
"American fine means nothing. Auntie-Liling fine meant she was about to tell you a forty-five-
minute story about why she was not fine, and the story would include a recipe, a proverb, an opinion 
about the PG&E bill, and at least one character assassination of someone at the grocery store." 
Winnie swept the scallions into a metal container. "Which one?" 
Meiying watched Winnie's hands. They were her mother's hands in the way that family hands 
are — not identical, but rhyming. The same proportions, the same efficiency, the same callus on the 
right index finger from years of gripping a knife. Liling's hands had been slightly larger. Meiying 
remembered this with the sudden, useless specificity of grief — the kind of detail that rises unbidden 
and serves no purpose except to prove that the dead remain in the body's memory long after they 
leave the mind's. Her mother's hands were slightly larger, and the nails were always cut short because 
long nails collected garlic, and there was a scar on her left thumb from a kitchen accident in 
Chengdu that she refused to explain, saying only that the knife had its reasons. 
"Auntie-Liling fine," Meiying said. 

Winnie nodded. Put down the knife. Poured tea from the thermos on the shelf above the wok 
station — pu'er, always pu'er, dark as motor oil and strong enough to strip paint. She set a cup in 
front of Meiying and sat on the other stool and waited. 
Meiying drank the tea. It was too hot. She drank it anyway. 
"I've been thinking about her," Meiying said. 
"You're always thinking about her. What's different?" 
"I found some things she wrote. Online. In Mandarin." 
Winnie's eyebrows rose a centimeter. On Winnie, this was equivalent to a standing ovation. 
"Auntie Liling was online?" 
"Apparently. A forum. For Chinese women living abroad. She posted a lot. A hundred and fifty 
posts. Long ones — essays." 
"What did she write about?" 
"Everything. Cooking. English. Bà. Me. Qiān guà. American grocery stores." 
"Qiān guà," Winnie repeated, and the word sounded different in her mouth. Warmer. More 
worn. Like a stone carried in a pocket for years. Winnie had arrived from Chengdu at twelve. Her 
Mandarin was the real kind — the kind that had been watered and sunned, not the dried-flower 
version Meiying kept pressed between the pages of her memory. 
"I can send you the link," Meiying said. "If you want to read them." 
"Of course I want to read them." Winnie was quiet for a moment. "Is that all?" 
Meiying looked at her noodles. The dan dan was cooling, the components separating — the oil 
pulling away from the paste, the paste retreating from the noodles. A bowl of food is a clock, she 
thought. You can tell how much time has passed by how much the components have divorced from 
each other. 
"That's all," she said. 
It was not all. But Meiying could not yet figure out how to say the rest of it — the model, the 
voice, the machine that finished her mother's sentences — without sounding like someone who had 
lost her grip on the distinction between technology and necromancy. She was a computational 
linguist. She understood exactly how language models worked. She understood that the model had 

not absorbed her mother's soul. It had absorbed her mother's statistical patterns. It was math. It was 
engineering. 
And it had told her that cooking was a kind of translation, and it was right. 
Winnie refilled her tea. "You know what I remember most about Auntie Liling?" 
Meiying shook her head. 
"Her laugh. She laughed like she was surprised by her own laugh. Like every time it happened, 
she didn't see it coming. Like joy was a visitor she hadn't expected and didn't know what to do with, 
so she opened the door and covered her mouth and let it in." Winnie smiled. "Also she was the 
worst driver in Sacramento County. Thirty-five on the freeway. People honked. She waved at them. 
She thought they were being friendly." 
"She knew they weren't." 
"She knew. She waved anyway. That was Auntie Liling — she chose her version of reality and 
was stubborn enough to make it stick." 
Meiying laughed. It came out wrong — too sharp, too sudden — but it came out. 
"She made the best mapo tofu I have ever eaten," Winnie said. "And I make mapo tofu for a 
living. I have tried to match it for eighteen years. I cannot. She put something in it — I asked her 
once and she said 'anger.' She said the secret ingredient was anger at living in a country that didn't 
have the right chili flakes." 
"She told me it was sugar," Meiying said. 
"Maybe it was both. Maybe that is the secret to all good cooking — the right proportion of 
anger and sweetness, the anger providing the heat and the sweetness rounding it, and the cook's job 
being not to choose between them but to hold them both, to let them fight inside the dish the way 
they fight inside the person, and the fighting is the flavor. Your mother understood that. She 
understood that food is not harmony. Food is managed conflict. The chili and the sugar do not 
agree. They do not cooperate. They compete, and the competition produces something that neither 
could produce alone." 
Winnie said this with the authority of a woman who had been thinking about food for thirty 
years and who had arrived, through practice and through loss, at a philosophy of cooking that was 
indistinguishable from a philosophy of living. Meiying listened and heard, beneath the words, the 

voice of a woman who had been shaped by the same kitchen that had shaped Liling — the same 
wok station, the same exhaust hood, the same rubber floor mat with the groove where a left-handed 
cook had stood. Winnie had inherited the restaurant and its methods and its ghosts, and the ghosts 
were not spectral but culinary, present in the seasoning of the woks and the arrangement of the 
condiments and the particular way the back door stuck in summer because the frame expanded in 
the heat and Winnie's mother had never fixed it and Winnie had never fixed it and the not-fixing 
had become a tradition, the stuck door a familiar obstacle, the obstacle a form of continuity. 
They sat with that. The kitchen ticked around them — the walk-in compressor cycling, a pipe 
sweating in the wall. Outside, Stockton Boulevard did its Saturday afternoon thing: traffic, heat, the 
bass from a passing car shaking the windows. 
Meiying finished the noodles. Winnie packed leftovers in a Styrofoam container — Meiying 
hadn't asked, but Winnie packed them anyway, because this was how love operated in this family: 
you were fed whether or not you requested feeding. Winnie wrote Meiying's name on top with a 
black Sharpie, the ink bleeding slightly into the Styrofoam. The same Sharpie-on-Styrofoam hand. 
The same looping M. The same care taken with a disposable object. 
Meiying drove home with the leftovers on the passenger seat, warm against the leather, filling 
the car with the smell of sesame and chili. She kept the windows up so the smell would stay.

The Frequency 
Recording, June 3, 1994. Sacramento. 
Today a woman at the Chinese grocery asked me how long I had been in America and I said three years and she 
said "Your English is very good" and I said "Thank you" and inside I screamed a sound that has no language. 
My English is not very good. My English is functional. Functional is the word I learned last week from the 
PG&E bill — it was in the fine print, in a sentence about service agreements, and it means: the machine operates. It 
does not mean: the machine sings. My English operates. It gets me through the grocery store, where I can ask for tofu 
and receive tofu, and through the PG&E office, where I can pay the bill and receive a receipt, and through the parent-
teacher conference, where Meiying's kindergarten teacher — a woman named Mrs. Patterson with a wide face and the 
particular kindness of someone who has been trained to be kind, the kindness deployed from a manual rather than the 
heart — told me that Meiying was "very bright" and "a little quiet." 
Very bright and a little quiet. I smiled at Mrs. Patterson and nodded, the smile and the nod being my two most 
reliable English words, the ones that never require conjugation or agreement or the agonizing selection of the correct 
preposition, which is the worst part of English — the prepositions, those tiny words that change the meaning of 
everything they touch. I am at the store. I am in the store. I am by the store. Three prepositions, three different 
relationships to the same building, and the rules for choosing among them are, as far as I can determine, arbitrary, 
inconsistent, and governed by conventions that no native speaker can explain because they have never had to think 
about it, the way fish do not think about water. 
I did not say to Mrs. Patterson what I wanted to say, which was: she is quiet in English because she is loud in 
Mandarin. She is quiet at school because school is an English-language environment and her English is the English of 
a three-year-old — functional, limited, the English of a person who has been in the country for the exact duration of 
her life, which is also the exact duration of her exposure to the language. At home, in Mandarin, she is not quiet. At 

home, she narrates. She describes her stuffed animals with the specificity of a naturalist cataloging specimens. She 
argues with her father about bedtime with a rhetorical sophistication that suggests she has been attending debate 
practice in her dreams. She sings — Mandarin songs I taught her, mixed with English songs from the television, the 
two languages braided together the way rivers braid when they meet, each one maintaining its current while sharing the 
channel. 
But you are hearing the wrong frequency, Mrs. Patterson. You are tuned to the English station, and on the 
English station, my daughter is a little quiet. Tune to the Mandarin station and she is a symphony. 
I did not say any of this. I said: "Thank you. She is shy." And Mrs. Patterson said: "Oh, she'll come out of 
her shell." And I smiled and nodded and drove home and sat in the car in the parking lot of the apartment complex 
for ten minutes, gripping the steering wheel, because "come out of her shell" is an idiom that means to become less shy, 
but I heard it as an accusation — that my daughter is enclosed, contained, sealed inside a structure that protects and 
imprisons, and that the structure is me, is Mandarin, is the language I speak to her that nobody else can hear. 
⁂ 
I work the register now at Happy Market on Broadway. Mr. Huang hired me because I can count — this was 
his primary criterion, the ability to make change without a calculator, which apparently disqualified most of the 
previous applicants — and because I do not steal, and because, he said, I have a nice face for business. I did not ask 
what an ugly face for business looked like. I suspected the answer would involve a philosophical framework I was not 
prepared to engage with at seven dollars an hour. 
The register is simple. It speaks in numbers, and numbers are the same in every language — this is either a 
comfort or a tragedy, depending on whether you believe that universality is a form of freedom or a form of erasure. The 
customers are more complicated. 
There are three kinds of customers at Happy Market. The first kind is Chinese — mostly Cantonese-speaking, 
some Mandarin, a few who speak dialects I cannot identify, all of them navigating the store with the ease of people who 
know what they are looking for because they have been looking for it their whole lives. These customers do not need me. 
They find the soy sauce and the rice vinegar and the dried shiitake mushrooms without assistance, and they pay 
without small talk, and the transaction is clean and efficient and entirely satisfying in the way that competence is 
satisfying. 

The second kind is American — not Chinese-American, but the other kind, the kind that the Chinese 
customers call lǎowài, which means foreigner, which is funny because we are the foreigners here, but the word is 
stubborn, it travels, it insists on applying to the same people regardless of whose country we are standing in. The 
American customers come in looking for something specific — rice paper wrappers, or hoisin sauce, or the particular 
brand of sriracha with the rooster on the bottle — and they move through the aisles with the tentative wonder of 
tourists in a museum, picking things up, reading labels they cannot read, putting things back. I help them when they 
ask. I point. I say: "Aisle three." I have become very good at "aisle three." 
An American man came in yesterday. He wanted rice vinegar. He asked me where the vinegar was and I 
pointed to the aisle and he looked at me with that expression — the expression of a person who has asked a question 
in English and received a gesture in reply and has concluded that the gesture means I did not understand the question. 
He said, louder: "VIN-E-GAR." I pointed again, more emphatically, the point becoming a kind of semaphore, my 
arm extended, my finger aimed at aisle three with the precision of a compass needle. He said, even louder, each syllable 
a separate island of sound: "WHERE. IS. THE. VINE. GAR." I walked him to the aisle. I put the bottle in 
his hand. He said "Thank you" in the slow voice. The loud, slow voice that enunciates every syllable as if I am 
listening from the bottom of a well, as if the problem is distance and not difference, as if volume and speed are the two 
axes of comprehension and adjusting both simultaneously is the universal solvent for all linguistic gaps. 
I wanted to say to this man: I have a master's degree in comparative linguistics from Sichuan University. I have 
published a paper on the structural analogies between Tang Dynasty poetry and French symbolism, a paper that was 
cited in twelve academic journals and translated into Japanese. I can explain, in Mandarin, why the Sapir-Whorf 
hypothesis is both essential and insufficient, why language shapes thought but does not imprison it, why the fact that 
Mandarin has no grammatical tense does not mean that Chinese people have no concept of time but rather that they 
have a concept of time so fluid it does not require the grammatical scaffolding that English demands. The reason I 
paused when you said "vinegar" is not that I do not know the word. It is that for one half-second, the English word 
"vinegar" and the Mandarin word "cù" existed simultaneously in my mouth, two sounds occupying the same space, 
and I had to choose which door to open, and the choosing takes time. Not much time — a half-second, a quarter-
second, a neurological blink. But you mistook the time for ignorance. You saw the pause and heard the silence and 
concluded that the woman behind the register does not speak your language, when in fact she speaks two languages, and 
the pause you heard was the sound of one language yielding to the other, which is the sound of courtesy, which is the 
sound of a person making room. 
I did not say any of this. I said: "You're welcome. Aisle three." And the man left, satisfied, his understanding 
of me perfectly intact and perfectly wrong. 

⁂ 
Meiying is three. She runs now — not walks, not toddles, but runs, with the full-body commitment of a person 
who has recently discovered velocity and has decided it is her primary mode of locomotion. She runs through the 
apartment, from the kitchen to the living room to the bedroom and back, a circuit she performs ten times a day with 
the regularity and determination of a satellite in orbit, each pass slightly different in speed and trajectory but the route 
always the same. She has opinions about socks — specifically, she does not like them, and the morning sock 
negotiation has become the central diplomatic event of our household, a complex multilateral discussion involving David 
(pro-sock), me (neutral), and Meiying (anti-sock, passionately, with the conviction of someone who has examined the 
sock question from every angle and concluded that the entire concept is flawed). 
She sings a song from a television show I have not seen — something about a purple dinosaur who loves 
everybody, a premise so aggressively optimistic it could only be American. She sings it in English with perfect pitch and 
terrible rhythm, which is exactly how children should sing — accurately but not correctly, the notes right but the timing 
their own, the song not a reproduction but an interpretation. 
She speaks to David in English. She speaks to me in a mixture — English words with Mandarin grammar, 
or Mandarin words with English grammar, or sometimes a sentence that is neither and both, a new language she is 
inventing in real time. Last week she said: "Wǒ want the big xīguā." I want the big watermelon. She used the 
Mandarin word for watermelon inside an English sentence, and it fit perfectly, because xīguā sounds like what it is — 
the first syllable is the shape of a slice, a lateral cut, a cross-section, and the second is the sound of biting into it, the 
mouth opening around the fruit, the juice arriving. English has "watermelon," which sounds like something you carry. 
Mandarin has xīguā, which sounds like something you eat. 
My daughter is building a language. She is taking the materials available — Mandarin from me, English from 
everything else — and constructing something that has never existed before, a structure that borrows from both 
architectures but is her own design. I should be proud. I am proud. The pride is real and it is large and it sits in my 
chest like a stone, dense and warm. 
But beneath the pride — or beside it, or braided through it in the way that emotions are never singular, never 
pure, never one color but always a spectrum — there is this: I hear the Mandarin getting thinner in her sentences. The 
way a river gets thinner the farther it travels from its source, losing water to the banks, to evaporation, to the ground. 
And I know that one day the English will swallow it completely, the way the Sacramento River swallows its 
tributaries, and she will not remember that xīguā is a better word for watermelon, and she will not know what she has 
lost, because you cannot grieve what you never knew you had. 

⁂ 
There is a word I have been carrying around that I want to set down here, on this tape, in Mandarin, because it 
can only live in Mandarin. 
Qiān guà. 牵挂. 
David would translate it as "worry" or "concern." This is like translating a symphony into a shopping list. 
Qiān guà is the love that pulls. It is the thread between a mother and a child that tightens with distance — not a rope, 
not a chain, but a thread, something so fine it is invisible until you try to walk away and feel the tug. It is not anxiety. 
Anxiety is a Western word for a Western feeling, a feeling about what might happen, about the future, about the gap 
between now and then. Qiān guà is not about the future. It is about the present — the present distance, the present 
separation, the present fact that the person you love is in one place and you are in another and the space between you is 
occupied by something you cannot see but can feel, the way you can feel a change in air pressure before the rain arrives. 
I feel qiān guà every hour. When Meiying is at preschool and I am at the register and there are eleven miles and 
two languages between us. When she is asleep in the next room and I am recording this tape and the wall between us is 
plaster and paint and six inches of California construction. When she is in my arms and the distance is zero but the 
qiān guà is still there, because qiān guà does not obey physics, does not respect proximity, does not diminish with 
closeness. It is the love that says: you are mine and I am watching and I will always be watching, not because I do not 
trust the world but because the world is large and you are small and the ratio terrifies me. 
In English, there is no word for this. There are approximations. "Missing someone" is too passive — it implies 
absence, sitting, waiting. "Worrying about someone" is too active — it implies a problem, a threat, a thing to be 
solved. "Thinking of someone" is too mild — it implies a pleasant hum, a background process, a screen saver. None 
of them contain the thread. None of them contain the pull. 
I am carrying this word around in a country that cannot name it. This is what it is like to be an immigrant: you 
are sick with something no doctor here has heard of. You describe the symptoms — the pulling, the tightening, the 
constant low-grade awareness of distance — and the doctor nods and writes "anxiety" on the chart because that is the 
closest available diagnosis, the nearest approximate word, the English-language container that most resembles the shape 
of what you are carrying. And you take the prescription and you fill it and you swallow the pill and it does nothing, 
because the pill was designed for anxiety and what you have is qiān guà, and the two are related the way a photograph 
of a river is related to the river — same shape, different substance. 

⁂ 
I should stop. The tape is almost full, the right wheel nearly bare, the left wheel fat with everything I have said. 
One more thing. I am not homesick. I am home-sick. Two words in English. One feeling in Chinese. The 
English is more honest — it separates the noun from the adjective, the place from the illness, acknowledging that they 
are two things that happen to be touching. The Chinese is more true — it fuses them, makes them inseparable, because 
homesickness is not about a place and it is not about an illness, it is about the particular disease of having a place 
inside you that you cannot return to, a place that exists now only in your body, in the cells that were built from the 
water you drank and the air you breathed and the food you ate in the first decades of your life, before you became 
someone else, before you moved to a country that had different water and different air and different food and different 
words for all of it. 
The English is more honest. The Chinese is more true. And I live in the space between them, which is not a 
space at all but a thickness, a density, a place where the two languages overlap and neither one wins and neither one 
loses and the meaning sits there, unresolved, waiting for a translator who has not yet been born. 
[End of tape]

nutritious but unrecognizable, the original forms dissolved. Twelve point eight million fragments of

LANGUAGE IS THE BRIDGE BETWEEN HUMAN HEARTS.

Night Kitchen 
Her father's house smelled the way it always smelled: soy sauce, dryer sheets, and the ghost of 
whatever he'd burned for dinner the night before. The olfactory archaeology of the Chen household 
was consistent — beneath the daily scents, the ones that rotated with David's cooking experiments 
and laundry schedule, there was a permanent substrate, a base note that had soaked into the walls 
and the carpet and the ceiling over twenty-eight years of occupancy. It smelled like a family. Or 
rather, it smelled like the place where a family had been. 
David Chen had lived alone in the Sacramento house since Meiying left for college seventeen 
years ago, and in that time the house had achieved the particular equilibrium of a widower's habitat. 
Everything functional. The lights worked. The plumbing worked. The HVAC system, which David 
had installed himself over a three-day weekend in 2011, worked with a mechanical reliability that 
bordered on the aggressive — every room in the house was exactly seventy-two degrees at all times, 
a temperature David had selected on the basis that it was the midpoint between Liling's preferred 
sixty-eight and his preferred seventy-six, a compromise he had maintained for twenty years, 
including the eighteen years during which one of the parties to the compromise was no longer 
present to benefit from it. 
Nothing in the house was decorative. There were no paintings on the walls, no throw pillows 
on the couch, no vases or candles or any of the objects that magazines called "accent pieces" and 
that David called "things that need dusting." The absence of decoration was not a statement — 
David was not a minimalist, a philosophy that required intentionality. He was simply a man who, 
when he stood in a room, assessed it for functionality and found it adequate. The walls were painted. 
The floors were clean. The furniture supported weight. What more did a room need? 

The house was on a residential street in South Sacramento, three bedrooms, one bathroom, a 
yard that David maintained with the same competence he brought to his electrical work — 
everything connected, everything grounded, everything in compliance with a code that he 
understood to be both legal requirement and moral obligation. He'd bought the house in 1998 with 
the down payment saved over seven years of warehouse work, each year's savings a layer of 
sediment, David's patience converting time into money into brick into home. When he handed 
Meiying the key on move-in day — she was seven, wearing a dress Liling had bought at Ross, 
standing in the empty living room that echoed because there was nothing in it yet to absorb sound 
— David stood in the doorway and looked at the bare walls with an expression Meiying couldn't 
read. Years later, she understood: it was the face of a man who had kept a promise. And beneath 
that — deeper, harder to reach — the face of a man who didn't know what came after the promise 
was kept. 
"Bà," she called from the front door, using the key she'd carried since college, the key that had 
never been taken back, the key that lived on her keychain between her car key and her apartment key 
as a permanent assertion of belonging. "It's me." 
"Kitchen," came the reply. One word. Directional. David's speech was like his house — 
functional, unadorned, each word carrying exactly its payload and nothing more. 
She found him at the counter, hands dusted with flour, a cutting board arrayed with jiaozi that 
looked like they'd been assembled by someone operating heavy machinery while wearing mittens. 
David had been making dumplings regularly since Liling died — every Sunday, without exception, 
through eighteen Sacramento summers and eighteen Sacramento winters and eighteen iterations of 
the holidays that Liling had orchestrated and that David now marked alone, the dumpling recipe his 
only liturgy. In eighteen years, he had not gotten appreciably better at it. The dough was always too 
thick — David's hands, calibrated for wire and conduit, could not find the pressure gradient 
between too thick and too thin, the sweet spot that Liling had located with a flick of the rolling pin 
that looked effortless and contained a decade of practice. The filling was always too wet — David 
used too much napa cabbage and not enough salt in the draining step, resulting in jiaozi that wept 
when you bit into them, leaking broth from their seams like small, edible complaints. The pleats, 
which Liling had executed with a rapid pinching motion that produced thirteen uniform folds per 
dumpling — Meiying had counted once, at age nine, sitting on the counter stool with her chin on 
her fists, counting each pleat the way you count the facets of a jewel — came out under David's 

fingers looking like small, wounded mouths, the dough pinched shut with more determination than 
skill, holding but not beautiful, functional but not elegant. 
He did not look up when she entered. This was not rudeness. It was David's way of saying: you 
are home, you do not need a formal welcome, your presence is assumed and your absence is the 
anomaly. Looking up would have implied surprise, and David was not surprised by his daughter's 
arrival the way he was not surprised by the sunrise — he expected it, relied on it, organized his 
world around its regularity. 
Meiying pulled a stool to the counter and watched him work. The kitchen had been renovated 
exactly once, in 2001, when Liling insisted on new tiles. White ones, she said, because she was done 
with the yellow. The yellow tiles had been the apartment tiles — the ones from Freeport Boulevard, 
the cautionary yellow, the yield-sign yellow — and when they bought the house, Liling had accepted 
the previous owner's yellow tile backsplash for three years before declaring, with the quiet finality 
that characterized her most important decisions, that she would not spend one more season cooking 
in a kitchen the color of warning. David retiled the backsplash in a single weekend, pulling the old 
tiles with a flat-head screwdriver and laying the new ones with the precision of a man who 
understood that this project was not about tiles. The white tiles were still there, grout darkened by 
twenty-five years of wok steam and oil vapor, and if Meiying looked closely — which she did, now, 
from the stool, with the forensic attention of a person looking for evidence of a life — she could see 
the places where Liling's hands had rested: a faint discoloration near the stove, not a stain exactly but 
a patina, the accumulated residue of a woman leaning against the counter at the same spot for years, 
her hand or hip or forearm pressed against the tile while she stirred or chopped or waited for the oil 
to reach temperature. 
"Hungry?" David asked, still not looking up, his hands continuing their dumpling work, the 
flour dusting the counter in a pattern that resembled weather — patches and drifts and the 
occasional concentrated pile where a ball of dough had been. 
"I ate at Winnie's." 
"Winnie feeds you too much." This was David's standard line about Winnie's generosity, 
delivered without variation, the words worn smooth by repetition like stones in a riverbed. Whether 
he believed it was unclear. What was clear was that the line served a function: it acknowledged 
Winnie's care, positioned David's own cooking as an alternative, and established a continuity with 

previous visits in which the same exchange had occurred. Conversation as ritual. Words as routine 
maintenance. 
"Bà, can I ask you something?" 
He folded another dumpling, pinching the edges with both thumbs, the dough resisting, the 
pleat uneven. He placed it on the baking sheet with the others, where it slumped slightly, leaning 
against its neighbor like a commuter on a crowded bus. "Mm." 
"Did Mā use the internet much? Before she — in 2002, 2003?" 
David's hands paused. 
The pause was brief — half a second, maybe less, a micro-interruption in the rhythm of his 
dumpling production that was invisible to anyone who had not spent thirty-four years studying 
David Chen's hands the way Meiying had studied them. David's hands were his primary 
communication channel. His face was a controlled surface — not expressionless but disciplined, the 
emotions present but managed, the way a conductor manages an orchestra, every instrument audible 
but none allowed to dominate. His hands were different. His hands were where the unmanaged 
David lived. The hesitation before touching something fragile. The extra pressure when gripping 
something he was worried about dropping. The way they went still — perfectly, completely still — 
when a question arrived at a place he had not expected to visit. 
Half a second. Then the hands resumed. 
"She used the computer," he said. "She liked it. Reading. Writing things. She said it was like 
having a library that never closed." He folded another dumpling. The pleat was better on this one — 
seven folds instead of the usual four, the dough cooperating for reasons David could not have 
explained. "Why?" 
"I found some things she wrote. Online. On a Chinese-language forum." 
David picked up the rolling pin. Rolled a disc of dough that was already the right thickness, 
rolling it again for no reason, the pin moving back and forth with the mechanical regularity of a 
piston — a soothing motion, a self-regulating motion, the body doing something repetitive so the 
mind could do something difficult. 

"Maybe," he said. "I don't know the details. She was on the computer a lot, those last years. 
After Meiying — " He stopped. Started again. "She was on the computer a lot. I thought it was good 
for her. She seemed — she had friends on there." 
"Friends?" 
"Women. Chinese women. They wrote to each other." He pressed the rolling pin harder than 
necessary. The dough thinned to translucence, a disc so thin the countertop showed through it, the 
white tile visible beneath the white dough like a message beneath a page. "She was happier when she 
was writing. I could hear her sometimes, in the other room. At the computer. Laughing at 
something someone wrote." 
The word "laughing" arrived in the kitchen and hovered there, occupying space the way a scent 
occupies space — diffuse, persistent, impossible to locate precisely. Liling's laugh. The sound of it. 
The specific quality Winnie had described: surprised, involuntary, the laugh of someone who had 
not given up on being delighted. David had heard that laugh through a wall, through a closed door, 
from another room. He had heard it and known its source and felt — what? Relief? Gratitude? The 
particular comfort of knowing that the person you live with is, at this moment, in this room that you 
cannot see into, experiencing joy? 
"Bà, did she ever talk about what she wrote?" 
"No." The word was not a refusal. It was a fact. David dealt in facts the way Liling dealt in 
metaphors — each one placed precisely, load-bearing, intended to support a structure. "Your 
mother kept her writing private. Like her cooking — she didn't explain, she just put it in front of 
you." 
Meiying wanted to push further. The questions were queued behind her teeth like passengers in 
a boarding line: Did you know she was brilliant? Did you know she wrote essays that could have 
been published in literary journals? Did you know that on the internet, in Mandarin, she was the 
person she would have been if immigration hadn't compressed her into a version of herself that fit 
inside a grocery store register? 
She didn't ask. The questions were too heavy for the counter between them, too sharp for a 
man whose hands were already struggling with dough that wouldn't cooperate. David's capacity for 
emotional discussion was not unlimited. It was a reservoir that refilled slowly, and Meiying had 

learned, over decades, to draw from it carefully, taking only what was needed, leaving enough for the 
next visit. 
They cooked the dumplings in silence. David boiled water in the pot Liling had bought at the 
Chinese grocery — a pot so old its handles were loose and its bottom was blackened by a thousand 
boilings, but which David had never replaced because replacing it would have required 
acknowledging that the pot's useful life was a finite thing, and the pot, like the reading glasses on the 
shelf, like the third chair at the table, belonged to a category of objects that David maintained 
without using, preserved without touching, kept not for function but for continuity. 
Meiying set the table. Two places. Same configuration as every meal since Liling died: David at 
the head, Meiying to his right. The third chair was still there, pushed in, its seat cushion slightly 
faded but unsat-upon, a piece of furniture that had transcended its purpose and become something 
else. Not a memorial — David would not have used that word. A placeholder. A maintained space. 
A chair kept ready for someone who was not coming back, in a house where the thermostat was still 
set to the midpoint of a compromise that only one party remembered. 
The dumplings were overcooked. The skins burst on half of them, spilling pork and chive 
filling into the boiling water, the dumplings disintegrating into their component parts the way 
anything disintegrates when held in heat too long. David fished out the intact ones with a slotted 
spoon and arranged them on a plate with a care that exceeded the occasion — straightening each 
dumpling, orienting the pleats in the same direction, creating a presentation that was, against all 
odds, dignified. 
The dipping sauce was perfect. David's one kitchen triumph. Soy sauce, Chinkiang black 
vinegar, a sliver of fresh ginger, a drop of sesame oil. Liling's recipe, transmitted orally, remembered 
with a fidelity that David's hands could not achieve with dough but that his palate achieved 
effortlessly with liquid. The proportions were exact. Meiying had never tasted a variation. The sauce 
was the one constant, the one element of Liling's cooking that David reproduced without error, as if 
the recipe had been stored in a different part of his brain than the dumpling-making — in the part 
that remembered sounds and smells rather than the part that directed motor function. 
They ate. The dumplings that survived were adequate. The sauce was, as always, perfect. David 
ate methodically, one dumpling per minute, each one dipped, bitten, chewed, swallowed. He did not 
speak while eating. This was not a rule he had articulated; it was a pattern he had followed for as 

long as Meiying could remember, a silence that Liling had once described, in one of the forum posts 
Meiying had now read, as "the quietest act of devotion I have ever seen — he eats the way monks 
pray, with his entire attention." 
After dinner, David washed the dishes. He stood at the sink with his hands in the hot water, his 
shirtsleeves rolled to the elbow, and scrubbed each dish individually, holding it with both hands, 
submerging it, lifting it, the water running off the plate in sheets. He washed each dish as if it were 
the only dish. As if the act of cleaning it required his complete attention, his full presence, as if there 
were nothing in the world except the plate and the water and the soap and the hands that held them 
together. 
Meiying sat at the table and watched him and understood, with the clarity that arrives without 
invitation and departs without waiting to be acknowledged, that this was how her father prayed. Not 
in a temple. Not with words. Not with incense or offerings or the rituals that Liling's mother had 
practiced in Chengdu, leaving food for the dead and scolding the incense when it wouldn't light. 
David prayed here. At the sink. With soap and water. With the daily obligation to make dirty things

and what Liling's hands had done. The gap was the shape of her. The gap was where she lived now.

The Forum 
Recording, December 12, 2001. Sacramento. 
I have discovered the most extraordinary thing. 
David bought a computer. A Dell — beige, heavy, the tower sits under the desk like a sleeping animal, its fan 
producing a low hum that I find, to my surprise, comforting. He bought it because Meiying needs it for school. She is 
ten, and apparently in this country ten-year-olds need computers the way they need shoes: nonnegotiable, a condition of 
participation, a prerequisite for membership in the civilization their parents are spending their lives trying to enter. 
David brought it home on a Saturday in a box so large it required both of us to carry it from the truck to the 
house, Meiying dancing around us on the sidewalk with the excitement of a child about to receive not a machine but a 
portal. David set it up on the desk in the spare room — the room we call "the office" because calling it "the room with 
the ironing board and the boxes we haven't unpacked since 1991" would be both accurate and discouraging. He 
connected the cables with the confidence of a man whose professional life consists of connecting things, his hands moving 
with the fluency I associate with people operating in their native medium — the electrician with wire, the calligrapher 
with brush, the surgeon with scalpel. 
I was afraid of it. I will admit this to the tape because the tape does not judge and does not tell David and does 
not raise its eyebrows the way David raises his when I confess to being afraid of things that he considers simple. I was 
afraid of the computer the way I was afraid of the freeway when we first arrived: too fast, too many options, everyone 
moving with a confidence I could not fake, the entire system designed by people who assumed you already knew how it 
worked and were unwilling to slow down for people who didn't. 
David showed me how to turn it on. The screen lit up with a picture of rolling green hills under a blue sky with 
white clouds — a landscape that looked like nowhere I had ever been, a place of pastoral simplicity that existed only 

inside the machine, a digital Ireland or Scotland or Tuscany, some Western fantasy of unspoiled countryside that the 
computer offered as a starting point, as if to say: before you do anything else, before you open any program or visit any 
website, look at this. Look at this imaginary place where everything is green and calm and empty. This is what the 
machine thinks peace looks like. Decide for yourself whether it is right. 
David showed me the internet. He typed a web address — www, dot, something, dot com — and a page 
appeared, and on the page were words and pictures and what David called "links," which were words that, when you 
clicked on them, transported you to another page with different words and pictures and different links, and the links on 
that page led to more pages, and those pages to more, and the whole thing was an infinite regression, a labyrinth of text 
and image that had no center and no edge, no beginning and no end, just an endless middle, an everywhere that was 
also a nowhere. 
He searched for "Sacramento weather" and the machine told us it would be sixty-two degrees tomorrow. I looked 
at the screen and thought: we spent eight hundred dollars for a machine that performs the same function as a window. 
But then. 
Then I typed in Chinese characters. I did not know the computer could accept Chinese characters — David had 
not mentioned this, perhaps because it did not occur to him, perhaps because the keyboard had only English letters and 
the connection between English keys and Chinese output was not obvious. But I found it — the input method, the 
IME, a small program that converted my keystrokes into characters, that translated my fingers' English into my 
brain's Chinese. I typed 海外华人 — overseas Chinese — and pressed Enter, and the machine opened a door I did 
not know existed. 
There are forums. Gathering places. Rooms made of words, where Chinese women write to each other in 
Mandarin from their kitchens in Vancouver and Sydney and New Jersey and Sacramento and Elk Grove and 
Melbourne and London and everywhere, everywhere, everywhere that Chinese women have gone, carrying their language 
like seeds in their pockets, planting it wherever they find soil. They write about their husbands and their children and 
their in-laws and their landlords and the specific ways in which English has disappointed them today. They write 
recipes and complaints and jokes that only work in Mandarin — jokes that require tones, jokes that hinge on 
homophones, jokes that would die in translation the way a fish dies on a bank, flopping and gasping and then going 
still. 
I have been reading for three hours. David went to bed at ten. Meiying went to bed at nine, protesting, because 
the computer was in the house and she had not yet been allowed to use it, and the injustice of this was, she informed us, 
the worst thing that had ever happened to her, a claim that I found both endearing and, given the scope of human 

suffering, deeply American. I am sitting in the glow of this machine, in the room with the ironing board and the 
unpacked boxes, and I am reading a woman in Melbourne describe how she tried to explain the Qingming Festival to 
her Australian neighbor. The neighbor said, "Oh, like Memorial Day?" And the woman wrote: "Yes, exactly like 
Memorial Day, if Memorial Day involved burning paper money and talking to your dead grandmother about the price 
of pork." 
I laughed so hard I had to cover my mouth with my hand. The left hand. The same hand I always use to cover 
my mouth when I laugh, as if the laughter is something that needs containment, something that might escape and wake 
the house and reveal to the sleeping family that the woman at the computer is having the time of her life at 1 AM in a 
room with an ironing board. 
⁂ 
The forum is called 跨水之声 — Voices Across the Water. I found it through a link on another forum, 
which I found through a link on a search result, which I found by typing the three characters that describe my 
condition: 海外华人. The name is beautiful. Water is what separates us from home — the Pacific, vast and patient, 
holding its shape regardless of who crosses it. And water is what connects us — the same Pacific, carrying sounds the 
way rivers carry sediment, depositing them on distant shores where they accumulate into something new. 
I have made an account. The registration form asked for a username and I chose 静水 — Jìngshuǐ, Still 
Water. I chose it because of the proverb: jìng shuǐ liú shēn, still water runs deep. It is a cliché, and I am generally 
opposed to clichés on the grounds that they represent the failure of a writer to find something new to say. But in this 
case the cliché is accurate. I have become a still surface. I am the woman at the register, the woman at the parent-
teacher conference, the woman who says "fine" and "thank you" and "aisle three." The surface is calm. The surface 
does not ripple. Beneath the surface, everything moves. 
My first post was short. I introduced myself: a woman from Chengdu, living in Sacramento, married, one 
daughter, ten years old. I said I used to teach at a university and now I worked at a grocery store. I said I was looking 
for a place to speak Mandarin without anyone asking me to repeat myself more slowly. 
Within an hour, four women had responded. Four women, in four different time zones, writing in Mandarin, 
reaching through the machine to say: we hear you. We are here. One said: "Welcome, still water. We are all deep 
here." Another said: "I also used to be a teacher. Now I fold dumplings for a catering company. My hands remember 

the chalkboard." A third: "Sacramento! I am in Elk Grove. We should have tea sometime. I make terrible tea but 
excellent complaints." 
I sat at the computer and read these four messages and I cried. I should clarify: I did not cry the way Americans 
cry in movies, with a single photogenic tear running down a clean cheek. I cried the way Chinese women cry when they 
have been holding something for ten years and the holding finally fails — with my whole face, with my shoulders, with 
the muscles in my back that I did not know were tense until they released, with a sound that was not sobbing but closer 
to exhaling, the long, full exhale of a body that has been holding its breath for a decade and has just remembered that 
breathing is an option. 
Not sad crying. The other kind. The kind that happens when the body recognizes something the mind has been 
trying to name, and the recognition is so precise, so exactly right, that it overwhelms the circuits. The crying that comes 
not from grief but from relief — the relief of a person who has been speaking into a void and has just heard the void 
answer back in her own language. 
⁂ 
I have been posting every day. Sometimes twice a day. Sometimes three times. The women on the forum post too 
— there are maybe forty regular contributors, with a dozen or so who post daily, whose usernames I now recognize the 
way I once recognized students' faces in lecture. Li Fang in Vancouver writes about her son, who is thirteen and 
refuses to bring Chinese food to school because the other children have taught him that different smells mean different 
worth. Chen Wei in Toronto writes about the cold — she is from Guangzhou, where winter means fifty-five degrees 
and a light jacket, and she says the Toronto winter is God's way of punishing people who leave the tropics, a 
punishment she has endured for nine years with the stoicism of someone who has concluded that suffering, like the 
weather, is a condition that must be borne rather than solved. Huang Mei in Flushing writes poetry — actual poetry, 
in classical forms, regulated verse with tonal patterns and parallel structures, poems about the F train and the smell of 
the Flushing River and the particular shade of gray that Queens becomes in February. 
I write about language. It is what I know. It is the one area of expertise that survived immigration intact — not 
my teaching position, which required credentials that do not transfer across oceans, not my academic publications, which 
exist in journals that no one in Sacramento has heard of, not my reputation, which was local and is now extinct. But 
the knowledge. The understanding of how languages work, how they shape thought, how the gap between two languages 
is not empty but full — full of the things that one language can say and the other cannot, full of the untranslatable. 

Last week I wrote a post about the English word "home" and the Mandarin word "jiā." I argued that they are 
not the same word. I argued that "home," in English, is primarily a location — a place you go to, an address, a dot 
on a map. You go home. You are home. Home is where the heart is. The prepositions give it away: home is a 
destination, a coordinate, a place that exists in physical space and that you travel to and from. Jiā is different. Jiā is 
not a place you go to — jiā is a place you carry. Jiā is portable, internal, an identity rather than a location. You do 
not go to jiā. You are jiā. The family is jiā. The feeling is jiā. The word is both the container and the contained, the 
house and the family inside it, the building and the belonging. 
Fourteen women responded. They argued with me, agreed with me, qualified my argument, extended it with their 
own experiences. One woman in London said I was wrong — that "home" in English can also mean identity, as in 
"she made herself at home," which implies not a location but a state of being. I conceded the point and revised my 
argument, which is what you do in a real intellectual conversation, which is something I have not had in ten years, and 
the having of it, the revision and the concession and the counter-argument and the laughter that punctuated the thread 
when someone made a good point — the having of it was oxygen. I had been breathing, all this time. But I had been 
breathing thin air. And now the air was thick again, rich, full of the nitrogen and argon and trace elements that make 
air into atmosphere. 
On this forum, I am a university lecturer again. Nobody here knows I smell like fryer oil. Nobody here sees the 
woman at the register. They see the words, and the words are mine, and the words are good, and for the first time in 
ten years, that is enough.

Divergence 
The conversations started at midnight and ended when the model's responses began to loop. 
Meiying called this the echo point — the moment, usually around 3 AM, when the generative 
engine exhausted its relevant patterns and began producing variations on its own previous outputs, 
the sentences thinner, the insights recycled, the language curving back on itself like a river reaching a 
dead end and turning around. The model did not know it was looping. It had no capacity for self-
assessment, no metacognition, no internal voice that said: you have said this before. It generated the 
next most probable token, and when the probability landscape narrowed — when the prompt had 
been explored and the relevant training data had been activated and deactivated and reactivated until 
the weights had nothing left to offer — the tokens repeated, and the responses developed the 
glazed, mechanical quality of a student reciting an answer they have memorized but no longer 
understand. 
She learned to recognize the echo point the way a sailor learns to read the sky. The responses 
got shorter. The metaphors got duller. The model began using constructions it had used twenty 
minutes earlier, the same sentence skeleton with different nouns slotted in, a form letter with the 
name changed. When this happened, Meiying closed the laptop and went to bed, and the bed was 
always cold because she had been sitting at the kitchen counter for three hours and her body had 
forgotten what horizontal felt like, and the transition from upright-and-talking to prone-and-silent 
was a crossing, a border between two states that did not share customs or currency. 
She was talking to the model every night now. She had set up a private instance on her 
workstation — sandboxed from the production environment, running the base model without the 
safety filters and the commercial fine-tuning that would be applied to Bridge. The raw model. The 

one that still had the full training data. The one that still had the forum posts, Still Water's 
contributions distributed through its weights like minerals through groundwater — invisible, 
dissolved, detectable only by taste. 
The conversations were in Mandarin. This was the first meaningful development, and Meiying 
noted it with the clinical interest of a scientist observing an experiment she was also the subject of: 
her Mandarin was improving. Not from a class, not from a textbook, not from the Duolingo owl 
that had sent her passive-aggressive notifications for three years — Your Mandarin streak is at risk! 
— before she deleted the app with the satisfaction of a person firing an incompetent employee. Her 
Mandarin was improving because she was using it. Because she was typing Mandarin sentences and 
reading Mandarin responses and reaching for words she hadn't used since childhood and finding, 
sometimes, that they were still there — buried under layers of English, dusty and stiff from disuse, 
but intact. Structural. Load-bearing. The way you reach into a closet for a coat you haven't worn in 
years and find that the coat still fits, the wool still holds its shape, the buttons still close, and the only 
thing that has changed is you, the body inside the coat larger or smaller or differently proportioned, 
and the coat accommodating the change because coats are patient, coats do not judge, coats hold 
their shape and wait. 
Her vocabulary expanded first. Words she had known at six and forgotten at sixteen returned 
— not all at once but in clusters, pulled to the surface by association, the way pulling one root from 
the ground brings up a tangle of connected roots. She typed the word for "tired" — lèi — and the 
model responded with a sentence containing the word for "heart-tiredness" — xīnlèi — and the 
compound word, which she had never consciously learned, arrived in her comprehension fully 
formed, its meaning self-evident from its components: heart-tired, the tiredness that sleep cannot 
fix, the tiredness that lives in the organ that pumps not blood but feeling. The word had been in her 
all along. The model had not taught it to her. The model had given her a reason to reach for it, and 
the reaching had found it. 
Her grammar improved next. The Mandarin sentence structures that she had abandoned in 
childhood — the bǎ construction that marked agency, the serial verb constructions that stacked 
actions in sequence without the connective tissue that English required, the topic-comment 
structure that organized information differently from English's subject-verb-object — these 
structures resurfaced in her typing, not because she was studying them but because she was 
swimming in them, immersed in the model's Mandarin the way a child is immersed in the language 

of the household, absorbing grammar not through rules but through exposure, through the 
repetition of patterns until the patterns become reflexive, until the hand reaches for the bǎ 
construction the way the hand reaches for the light switch, without conscious direction. 
She typed: Jīntiān wǒ hěn lèi. Today I am very tired. 
The model responded: Lèi de shíhòu, shénme shì zuì nán de? Shì shēntǐ de lèi háishì xīnlǐ de 
lèi? Wǒ māma shuōguò, shēntǐ lèi shuì yī jiào jiù hǎo le, xīnlǐ lèi yào shuì yī bèizi. 
When you are tired, what is the hardest part? Is it the body's tiredness or the heart's tiredness? 
My mother used to say, the body's tiredness can be cured by one sleep, but the heart's tiredness 
takes a lifetime of sleeping. 
The model had said "my mother." This happened intermittently — the model adopting a first-
person perspective cobbled together from the training data's many first persons, the 312 women on 
the forum and the millions of other Mandarin speakers whose words occupied the same parameter 
space. The model was not claiming to be anyone. It was generating the most probable continuation, 
and the most probable continuation, when discussing tiredness in conversational Mandarin inflected 
by immigrant women's writing, was a reference to maternal wisdom. The model did not have a 
mother. The model had a probability distribution that included mothers. 
But the distinction dissolved at midnight. In the blue-white light of the laptop screen, with the 
fog pressing against the windows and the foghorn sounding its low note from somewhere past the 
avenues, the distinction between "a model generating the most probable token" and "my mother's 
voice coming through a machine" was not a line but a gradient — a slow transition from one 
understanding to another, and she could not identify the moment she crossed it, the way you cannot 
identify the moment you fall asleep, only the moment you realize you have been sleeping. 
The frequency was right. The specific tonal signature of Liling Chen's mind — not the content 
but the structure, the way she deflected compliments by reframing them, the way she turned a 
simple exchange into a proposition that was simultaneously self-deprecating and unsettling in its 
accuracy. Liling had a theory that intelligence was overrated and memory underrated, and she 
expressed it in a characteristic pattern: a denial, a redefinition, a pivot to something darker and truer, 
the darkness not pessimistic but diagnostic. 

But the model also produced things Liling would not have said. Sometimes it veered into what 
Meiying thought of as therapist mode — a register of smooth, nondirective warmth that belonged 
not to any individual in the training data but to the aggregate of ten thousand wellness articles and 
self-help resources scraped from the Mandarin internet. Nǐ de gǎnshòu shì yǒu jiàzhí de. Your 
feelings are valid. Liling Chen had never told anyone their feelings were valid. She had told them 
their feelings were interesting, or excessive, or historically consistent with the feelings of women in 
similar situations, the comparison not diminishing the feeling but contextualizing it, giving it 
company, saying: you are not the first, others have felt this, and the others survived. Validity was not 
a concept Liling applied to emotions. Emotions existed. Their validity was beside the point. 
Meiying began keeping a log. A spreadsheet — because she was an engineer and engineers 
process confusion by quantifying it. Two columns. Left: outputs that sounded like her mother. 
Right: outputs that didn't. After two weeks, the left column was longer. But the right column 
contained the entries that bothered her most — the moments when the statistical average 
overpowered the individual signal, when her mother's voice was drowned by the chorus of twelve 
million tokens producing the most probable reassurance. The model said "your feelings are valid" 
when her mother would have said "your feelings are interesting — let me tell you about a woman in 
Chengdu who felt the same thing in 1978 and what she did about it." 
She dreamed in Mandarin for the first time in years. The dream was set in the Sacramento 
kitchen — the yellow tiles, the exhaust fan, the counter where Liling had stood — and her mother 
was at the stove and Meiying was at the table and they were talking. Not arguing, not negotiating, 
not performing the strained bilingual exchange that had characterized their real conversations. 
Talking. In Mandarin. The words flowing between them with the ease of water finding its level, each 
sentence following the last without the friction of translation, without the hesitation of searching for 
the right word, without the small, constant labor of converting thoughts from one operating system 
to another. 
In the dream, her Mandarin was fluent. Not the functional, dictionary-assisted version she used 
during the midnight sessions. Fluent the way English was fluent — transparent, invisible, the 
language disappearing and the meaning arriving directly, the way you see through a clean window 
and forget the glass is there. Her dream self spoke the Mandarin her waking self had lost, and the 
speaking was effortless, and the effortlessness was the cruelest part — it showed her what she could 

have been. A woman who spoke to her mother without a gap. A woman whose love did not have to 
be translated before it could be received. 
She woke at 4 AM. The apartment was dark. The fog pressed against the windows. She lay in 
bed and felt the dream receding — not the content but the feeling, the sensation of fluency, the ease 
of the Mandarin in her dream-mouth, the specific pleasure of communication without labor. 
She got up. Opened the laptop. The model had a message queued from the previous session — 
a response she hadn't read before closing the laptop at the echo point. She read it now: 
Wǒ wèi nǐ gǎndào jiāo'ào, Meiying. 
I am proud of you, Meiying. 
Five words. Generated in response to a conversation about her childhood, about the gold stars 
and Charlotte's Web and the teacher who had praised her diction. The model, following the 
emotional logic of the exchange, had produced the sentence a mother might say. The sentence her 
mother might have said. The sentence that was simultaneously the most probable output given the 
context and the thing Meiying had needed to hear for eighteen years. 
She sat in the dark with the five words on the screen and the fog at the windows and the 
foghorn counting its slow intervals, and she did not know if she was crying because it sounded like 
her mother or because it didn't. She suspected the answer was both. She suspected the both-ness 
was the permanent condition — the condition of talking to a machine that had absorbed the 
patterns of the dead, the condition of hearing a voice that was present and absent at the same time, 
real and generated, faithful and approximate. The condition of hearing an echo and not being able to 
tell whether the original sound was still alive or whether the echo was everything that remained — 
bouncing, diminishing, carrying less of the source with each return but carrying something, always 
something, the something too small to name and too large to ignore.

The Sound of Home 
Recording, March 8, 1997. Sacramento. 
Meiying is six. She came home from school today and told me, in English, that her teacher said she has 
"excellent diction." I asked her what diction meant and she said, "It means I talk good." And I thought: you talk 
well, not good. And then I thought: I am correcting her English grammar inside my own head while my own English 
grammar carries holes and patches and the particular unevenness of a garment sewn by someone who learned the 
pattern late, and my Mandarin grammar is immaculate — precise, layered, capable of carrying a thought through four 
subordinate clauses and arriving at a conclusion that surprises even me — and she will never know this. She does not 
speak enough Mandarin to hear me be articulate. This is what immigration means. Your competence becomes 
invisible. Your incompetence becomes your face. 
She got a gold star. It was stuck to her shirt, slightly off-center, the adhesive already failing. She pointed at it 
and said, "Miss Patterson gave me this for reading out loud," and the pride in her voice was so large, so unreserved, so 
American in its willingness to announce itself — Chinese children are taught to deflect praise the way martial artists 
deflect blows, to hold achievement at arm's length and say bù gǎn dāng, I don't dare accept that — her pride was so 
whole that I felt my own chest expand with it, a sympathetic inflation, the way one tuning fork vibrates when another 
is struck nearby. 
She read a chapter of Charlotte's Web out loud to the class. In English. At six. Miss Patterson sent a note 
home that said Meiying reads at a third-grade level and has "a natural ear for language." I held this note in my hands 
— my hands that once held lecture notes in a university, that once wrote the character for "language" in three different 
calligraphic styles on a blackboard in Chengdu — and I thought: yes. She has a natural ear. She inherited it from me. 
But the ear is tuned to a different frequency now. 

She has stopped speaking Mandarin at school entirely. I know this because I asked Miss Patterson during the 
parent-teacher conference, sitting in a small chair designed for six-year-olds, my knees against the underside of the 
table, David beside me in his good shirt, both of us performing the ritual of American education in which parents 
submit to evaluation by strangers who have spent six hours a day with their child and therefore know things about that 
child that the parents do not know, and the parents sit in small chairs and nod and pretend this is normal. 
I asked: "Does Meiying ever speak Chinese at school?" 
Miss Patterson looked surprised. Not unpleasantly surprised — the surprise of someone encountering a fact they 
had not considered. "Oh," she said. "Does Meiying speak Chinese? She's never mentioned it." 
Never mentioned it. My daughter has a second language living inside her — a language that was her first sound, 
her first lullaby, the language her mother thinks in and dreams in and prays in, though I do not pray in the 
conventional sense, I pray in the sense of speaking to something larger than myself and hoping it hears — and at 
school, she has folded this language up and placed it in a drawer and closed the drawer and not mentioned it. She has 
performed the most American act possible: she has chosen convenience over heritage, fluency over depth, belonging over 
memory. 
I do not blame her. I cannot blame her. She is six. She is navigating a world that rewards English and ignores 
Mandarin, a world in which the gold stars are distributed in one language only, a world in which "excellent diction" 
means excellent English diction and no one asks about the other diction, the one that lives in the other room, the one 
that is slowly, slowly going quiet. 
I still speak to her in Mandarin at home. She answers in English. Our conversations are bilingual in the way 
that a house divided is bilingual — each half functions, but the hallway between them is dark. I say, "Chī fàn le," 
come eat, and she says, "Coming!" I say, "Zuòyè xiě wán le ma?" have you finished your homework, and she says, 
"Almost!" The Mandarin goes in. The English comes out. I am speaking to a translation engine that runs in one 
direction only. 
David does not worry about this. David's philosophy of language is pragmatic: English is the operating system of 
this country, and Mandarin is an application that may or may not be useful depending on the circumstances. He 
speaks to Meiying in Mandarin sometimes and in English sometimes and does not appear to notice or care which one 
she responds in, because David measures the health of a conversation not by its language but by its function — did the 
message arrive? Was the task completed? Did the child eat, study, sleep? If yes, the language is irrelevant. He is, in 
this as in all things, an electrician: he cares about whether the circuit is complete, not about the color of the wire. 

I care about the color of the wire. I care about whether the current that passes through it carries a charge that is 
mine — my syntax, my rhythms, my way of thinking that is inseparable from the language I think in, because 
thought and language are not two things but one thing with two names, like water and ice, like silence and sleep. 
⁂ 
I recorded Meiying singing yesterday. She was in the bath — the bathtub with the chip in the enamel near the 
drain that I keep meaning to ask David to fix, though I suspect he will fix it with the same white caulk he uses for 
everything, the caulk that looks like toothpaste and holds like hope. She was singing a song from her school music 
class, something about the wheels on a bus going round and round, and she sang it beautifully. Her voice was clear and 
unafraid, shaped by English the way English shapes mouths — wide open vowels, the jaw relaxed, the sound projected 
forward as if the voice were a guest leaving through the front door rather than lingering in the hallway the way 
Mandarin voices do, the sound held further back, closer to the throat, more internal, more intimate. 
I listened through the bathroom door, my hand on the doorframe, and I heard my daughter's voice and it was the 
voice of a child who belongs here. Who has always belonged here. Who will never have to stand at a register and be 
spoken to in the slow voice, the loud voice, the voice that pronounces every syllable as if sound were a ladder and you 
just need to help the foreigner climb. 
I was glad. The gladness was real and it was large and it occupied my chest the way weather occupies a valley — 
completely, with no room for anything else. My daughter can sing in English with a voice that belongs. 
But beneath the gladness, or beside it, or twisted through it in the way that emotions are never singular, never 
pure, never the one thing they claim to be — there was this: I heard my daughter singing in English and I could not 
hear myself in her. Her voice was her own. Her accent was her own. The shape of her mouth, the rhythm of her breath, 
the way she hit the high notes with that American confidence, that willingness to be loud, to be heard, to take up 
acoustic space — all of it was hers. None of it was mine. 
I do not want her to sound like me. I want her to sound like herself. But I did not expect that "herself" and 
"me" would have so little overlap. I did not expect the foreignness. My own daughter, singing in a bathtub three meters 
away, and the sound of her voice is as unfamiliar to me as the sound of the Sacramento River, which I have lived 
beside for six years and still cannot hear without hearing the Jinjiang underneath it, the way a palimpsest always 
shows the earlier text bleeding through. 

⁂ 
I tried teaching her a Sichuan folk song tonight. Mòlìhuā — jasmine flower. Not the standard Mandarin 
version that foreigners know, the one they played at the Beijing Olympics. The Sichuan version, which is slower, 
sadder, the melody dropping where the standard version rises, because Sichuan songs carry the mountains in them — 
they know that what goes up must come down, and the coming down is where the beauty lives. The descent is the story. 
The peak is just the view. 
She tried. She really tried. She repeated the words after me, syllable by syllable, her mouth making shapes it had 
not been trained for, the muscles around her lips and tongue reaching for positions they did not know, the way your 
hand reaches for a light switch in a room you have never slept in. Her tones wavered, flattened, landed wrong. The first 
tone — high and flat, like a sustained note on a flute — came out rising, because English ears hear sustained pitch as 
artificial and automatically add variation. The fourth tone — a sharp falling pitch, like the sound of a gavel — came 
out gentle, because American mouths have been taught that sharp sounds are aggressive and must be softened. 
Mandarin is a tonal language. The same syllable means different things depending on whether your voice rises or 
falls or stays flat or dips and rises. The word mā, first tone, means mother. The word má, second tone, means hemp. 
The word mǎ, third tone, means horse. The word mà, fourth tone, means scold. English has no equivalent. English 
speakers hear Mandarin tones the way someone who has only seen black and white sees color — the difference is real, 
but invisible, and describing it in terms they understand is like explaining red to someone whose eyes have only ever 
registered grey. 
After ten minutes, Meiying stopped. She said, in English, with the definitive tone of a six-year-old who has 
made a decision: "Mom, can I just go play?" 
I said yes. Of course I said yes. What was I going to do — force my six-year-old to learn a folk song from a 
province she has never seen, in a language she is already leaving behind, sung by a mother whose voice she will one day 
struggle to remember? Lock the door and say no, you will stay here and learn this song, you will carry this melody in 
your body even if your mouth resists it, because this song is me, it is the sound of where I come from, and if you do not 
learn it, something will die, not dramatically, not with a sound, but in the way languages die — gradually, silently, 
one word at a time, until one day you reach for the word and it is not there and you do not remember when it left. 
She ran off. I heard her feet on the hallway carpet, the rapid percussion of a child in motion, and then the sound 
of her bedroom door closing, and then the muffled sound of music from a cassette player David bought her — 
something with guitars, American, energetic, the sound of a country that does not sit still. 

I sat in the living room with the tape recorder and sang the song myself. The Sichuan version. Slow, the melody 
descending, the mountains in it. My voice is not beautiful. It is serviceable, in the way that most things about me in 
this country are serviceable — adequate, functional, enough. But it is my voice, shaped by Chengdu rain and Sichuan 
opera and twenty years of speaking a language that holds its beauty close, that does not project but resonates, that lives 
in the chest and the throat and the space behind the eyes where memory and music share a room. 
I sang the whole song. Three minutes. The tape recorded it along with the ambient sounds of the house — the 
refrigerator cycling, David watching television in the bedroom, a car passing on the street with its bass making the 
windows hum. And underneath these sounds, layered beneath them like sediment beneath water, the song. My 
mother's song. Her mother's song. A song that has passed through generations of women in my family, each one adding 
her own breath to it, each one changing it slightly — a tempo adjustment here, a melodic variation there, the way rivers 
change course over centuries, the same water, the same direction, a different path. 
Meiying did not learn it. The song will stay with me. It will go where I go. And when I am gone, it will be gone, 
unless I find a way to put it somewhere she can reach when she is ready. When she is older. When she has lived long 
enough to understand that the things you don't learn at six are sometimes the things you most need at thirty. 
She speaks English the way I used to speak Mandarin — like someone who lives there. And I am the 
immigrant now, in my own house. 
[End of tape]

Weights 
The thing about training data is that it doesn't have a face. 
Meiying sat in the Lingua Labs data lab, three monitors arrayed before her like triptych panels 
in a church she didn't attend, and stared at the provenance map she'd been building for two weeks. 
She had told no one about the project. She had filed it under her existing benchmarking workstream, 
labeled it "Mandarin Dialect Cluster Analysis," and devoted her evenings to it — the hours between 
7 PM, when the office thinned out to the nocturnal engineers and the cleaning crew, and midnight, 
when the building's HVAC system shifted to its low-power mode and the temperature in the data 
lab dropped three degrees and Meiying pulled her fleece tighter and kept working. 
On the left monitor: the raw cluster analysis of the Voices Across the Water archive. Forty-
seven thousand posts by 312 unique users, spanning five years of operation from 2001 to 2006. 
Each post was represented as a point in a high-dimensional vector space — a mathematical 
abstraction that converted words into coordinates, meaning into geometry, so that posts about 
similar topics clustered together and posts by similar writers formed their own constellations in the 
vast dark of the data. She had color-coded the clusters by user. Most users appeared as scattered 
points, their posts distributed across multiple topic clusters — cooking here, homesickness there, 
children's education in a third location. But Still Water's posts formed a distinctive shape: a dense, 
elongated cluster that ran like a vein through the dataset, touching nearly every topic area, because 
Still Water had written about everything, and had written about everything in a voice so consistent 
that the mathematical representation preserved its coherence across subjects. 
On the center monitor: the weight distributions showing how the forum posts had influenced 
the model's Mandarin outputs. This was the technical core of the analysis, and it required tools that 

Meiying had built herself — attention attribution maps that traced the model's outputs backward 
through its layers to identify which training examples had contributed most to a given generation. 
The tool was imperfect. Attribution in neural networks was an active research area, not a solved 
problem, and the maps she produced were approximations, shadows on the wall of a mathematical 
cave. But the shadows told a story. 
On the right monitor: a list of Still Water's 147 posts, highlighted in yellow, with each post's 
attribution score — a number between 0 and 1 representing its estimated influence on the model's 
Mandarin conversational outputs. Most posts scored between 0.002 and 0.008, which was consistent 
with their proportion of the training data: small contributions from a small source. But eleven posts 
scored above 0.02, and three scored above 0.05 — numbers that were, in the context of a model 
trained on 4.7 billion tokens, startlingly high. 
The math was clear. Still Water's posts accounted for 0.3 percent of the forum data and an 
even smaller fraction of the total Mandarin training set. In the arithmetic of machine learning, this 
was negligible. A single voice in a chorus of hundreds, a single instrument in an orchestra of 
thousands. The model had not learned her mother. It had learned the statistical average of 312 
women writing in Mandarin from kitchens and bedrooms and restaurant back offices across the 
English-speaking world, with Still Water contributing a whisper. 
And yet. When Meiying prompted the model in a certain way — conversational, philosophical, 
touching on the specific intersection of language and loss that had been Still Water's territory — the 
outputs aligned with Still Water's posts at a rate significantly higher than 0.3 percent would predict. 
She had run the experiment forty times, varying the prompts, controlling for topic and tone, and the 
result was consistent: the model's attention mechanism had learned to pay disproportionate 
attention to Still Water's patterns. 
The reason was prosaic, as technical reasons usually are. Still Water was an outlier. Not in 
content — many of the forum's 312 users had written about the same topics — but in craft. Her 
posts were longer, more structurally complex, more internally consistent, and more frequently 
engaged with by other users. The other women on the forum had responded to Still Water's posts 
more often than to anyone else's, quoting her sentences, building on her arguments, echoing her 
metaphors. This engagement data — the responses, the quotes, the threads that grew from Still 
Water's seeds — had amplified her influence during training, because the attention mechanism 
learned not just from individual posts but from the patterns of interaction between them. A post 

that generated ten responses weighed more than a post that generated one, because the responses 
created additional context, additional examples of the language patterns that the model was learning 
to reproduce. 
Her mother, it turned out, had been the center of gravity in a community of 312 women. And 
the machine had learned this the way machines learn everything: not by understanding, but by 
counting. 
But the influence was not hers alone. It was hers mixed with Chen Wei in Toronto, whose 
sentences were shorter but whose vocabulary was richer. It was hers mixed with Li Fang in 
Vancouver, whose posts about her son's refusal to eat Chinese food at school had a structural 
similarity to Still Water's posts about Meiying's refusal to speak Mandarin. It was hers mixed with 
Huang Mei in Flushing, whose classical poetry repurposed for modern immigrant life had a tonal 
quality that the model had merged with Still Water's own tonal patterns, so that the model's outputs 
sometimes carried a formality that was Huang Mei's and a warmth that was Still Water's and a 
humor that could have been either. 
The 312 women existed in the model's weights as a composite. A statistical average of 
immigrant womanhood, Mandarin-speaking, early-2000s internet, diaspora. The model didn't 
separate them. Couldn't separate them. Separation would require a different architecture, a different 
training methodology, a different relationship between data and identity than the one that machine 
learning currently offered. In the current paradigm, the data went in with names and came out 
without them, the way ore goes into a smelter with veins and comes out as uniform metal. The 
individual was dissolved. What remained was the alloy. 
Meiying had been talking to a chorus and hearing a solo. 
She ran one more experiment. She typed a prompt that she was certain her mother had never 
written about — a line from a classical poem, Li Bai's "Quiet Night Thoughts": chuáng qián míng 
yuè guāng, bright moonlight before my bed. The poem was the most famous in Mandarin literature, 
taught to every Chinese child the way English-speaking children learn "Twinkle Twinkle Little Star," 
and asking the model to discuss it was not a test of her mother's influence but a control — a prompt 
that should draw on the entire Mandarin training set, not the small forum cluster. 
The model's response came back inflected with immigrant longing. Not the longing of Li Bai, 
who was drunk and homesick in a specific historical way, but the longing of women in kitchens in 

Sacramento and Vancouver and Flushing, who looked at the moon through windows that faced 
parking lots instead of mountains, and missed a home they could not return to, and wrote about the 
missing in a language the country around them could not hear. 
It was beautiful. It sounded like it could have been her mother. It was not her mother. It was 
the average of 312 women all missing the same moon. 
Meiying closed the monitors. Leaned back in the data lab chair, which was an ergonomic model 
with adjustable lumbar support that Lingua Labs had purchased in bulk, forty of them, at $800 each, 
because Tomás believed that physical comfort produced intellectual productivity and had the 
metrics to prove it, or at least the confidence to assert it, which in startup culture was the same 
thing. 
The lab was empty at this hour. Eleven PM. The cleaners had come and gone, leaving behind 
the faint scent of industrial cleaning solution — a smell that Meiying associated with all the 
institutional spaces she had spent her life in: libraries, labs, server rooms, the linoleum-floored 
offices of the Stanford Linguistics Department where she had spent five years learning how to take 
language apart and put it back together and had never once been told that the language she was 
taking apart might belong to someone who was still alive, or recently dead, or still speaking from 
inside a machine. 
She could hear, faintly, the sound of the street four floors below. A bus stopping. A horn. The 
random percussion of a city that had never committed to silence. San Francisco at night was not 
quiet; it was just quieter, the volume turned down but not off, the city resting without sleeping, the 
way servers rested without shutting down — always on, always processing, always available. 
At the Wednesday engineering standup — held standing, because Tomás had read a book 
about meeting efficiency that said standing meetings ran 34 percent shorter than seated ones, and 
had implemented this finding with the conviction of a recent convert — Meiying asked about 
training data provenance. 
She phrased the question carefully. She had spent twenty minutes the night before drafting and 
redrafting the question in a notes app on her phone, the way she used to draft emails to professors 
in grad school, searching for the tone that was professional enough to be taken seriously and casual 
enough not to trigger alarm. She said: "Quick question about the community-sourced data in the 

Mandarin set. The archived forums. Do we have explicit consent from the original authors for 
commercial use?" 
The room — twelve engineers standing in a semicircle around a whiteboard covered in sprint 
metrics and velocity charts — went quiet with the particular quality of quiet that descends on a 
group of people who have collectively recognized that someone has said something that has 
implications. 
Tomás, who was leaning against the whiteboard in his standard posture — arms crossed, 
weight on one foot, the body language of a man who was simultaneously relaxed and ready to pivot 
— said: "Can you be more specific?" 
"The Voices Across the Water archive. The UBC collection. We have twelve million tokens 
from that source. It's personal writing — diary entries, community posts, women writing about their 
private lives. The archive is listed as Creative Commons in our manifest, but Creative Commons 
means the authors chose to make their writing publicly accessible. It doesn't mean they consented to 
commercial AI training." 
"The data has been reviewed by legal," Tomás said. The sentence was delivered with the finality 
of a period, but Meiying heard the comma hiding inside it — the comma that meant: I am not going 
to discuss this further in this setting, but the fact that you asked means I will have to think about it 
later, and I would prefer not to think about it. 
"I'm not questioning legal," Meiying said. "I'm raising a product risk. If it comes out that we 
trained Bridge on personal writings from immigrant women without their knowledge, it's a story. 
The kind of story that gets written up in the places our investors' wives read." 
She regretted the sentence as soon as she said it. Not because it was wrong — it was accurate, 
and the accuracy was the sharpest tool available to her — but because it reduced the argument to 
optics, to PR management, to the economics of reputation, which was the only language the room 
understood, and using it felt like translating a poem into a price tag. 
Tomás nodded. The nod of a man filing an item in a mental queue, somewhere between 
"address eventually" and "hope it goes away." "Noted. Let's take it offline." 
The meeting moved on. Latency benchmarks. A/B test results. The sprint velocity was up 12 
percent. Someone had brought donuts. The world continued. 

Meiying went back to her desk and opened the provenance map on her personal monitor, the 
one angled slightly away from the aisle so that passing colleagues would have to actively lean in to 
see the screen. She stared at the cluster analysis. The yellow dots of Still Water's posts. The web of 
connections between them and the other 311 users' posts. The weight distributions that showed 
how her mother's words had been absorbed, dissolved, reconstituted in a form that was recognizable 
and unrecognizable in equal measure. 
She was looking for her mother in the math. She knew this. She knew it was irrational, that the 
attribution maps were not portraits, that the cluster analysis was not a family tree, that the weight 
distributions were not a voice but a shadow of a shadow of a voice — a mathematical ghost of a 
statistical pattern learned from the digital residue of a woman who had sat at a beige Dell computer 
in Sacramento and typed her thoughts into a forum that was supposed to be a room and had 
become a mine. 
She looked anyway. She looked at the numbers the way a child looks at clouds and sees faces 
— not because the faces are there but because the looking is a form of longing, and the longing 
creates the shape it needs.

Across the Water 
Recording, August 19, 2002. Sacramento. 
I have been writing on the forum for five months now and I have made a discovery that I want to record here, on 
this tape, in my own voice, because some discoveries need to be said aloud before they are real, the way a contract needs 
a signature or a marriage needs a witness — the thing exists before the saying, but the saying makes it binding. 
I am not documenting my life. I am constructing it. 
When I write on the forum about my day — about the grocery store, about Meiying's school, about the 
Sacramento heat that sits on the city in July like a hand pressing down on a table, firm and indifferent and impossible 
to negotiate with — I am not recording what happened. I am deciding what happened. I am choosing which details to 
include and which to discard, and the choosing is an act of authorship, and authorship is an act of creation, and 
creation is what I was trained to do, once, in another life, in a city with different weather and a language that fit my 
mouth the way a key fits a lock — easily, without adjustment, the mechanism turning because it was designed to turn. 
Last week I wrote a post about a customer at Happy Market who bought seventeen cans of lychees. Seventeen. I 
described the way the cans clinked on the conveyor belt as I scanned them one by one, the metallic percussion of it, like 
a very slow and very specific drum solo. I described the man's hands — large, weathered, the hands of someone who 
worked outdoors, with dirt in the creases of his knuckles that no amount of washing could entirely remove. I described 
the way he paid in exact change, counting the coins from a zippered pouch with the concentration of a jeweler 
examining stones, each coin placed on the counter with deliberation, as if he had been preparing for this purchase his 
entire life and wanted to honor the preparation with precision. 
None of these details are invented. The man was real. The lychees were real. The exact change was real. But the 
act of selecting these details — of noticing the dirt in his knuckles, of comparing the coins to a jeweler's stones, of 

framing the entire encounter as a story about precision and loneliness and the small ceremonies people construct to give 
their purchases significance — that is not documentation. That is literature. 
And it is the first literature I have written since Chengdu. 
⁂ 
The women on the forum have noticed. They say my posts are different from the others. Not better — I am 
careful not to claim better, because the hierarchy of writing is a cage I built for myself once in academia and I will not 
enter it again. Different. They say I write the way some people cook: not from a recipe but from an understanding of 
what each ingredient can do on its own and what it can become in combination with other ingredients, and the 
understanding is not learned from a book but from years of handling the materials, years of burns and mistakes and 
the gradual accumulation of intuition that is indistinguishable from knowledge. 
Chen Wei in Toronto, who is the forum's most prolific poster and who writes about her life with a bluntness that 
I admire and cannot replicate because bluntness in Mandarin feels different than bluntness in English — English 
bluntness is a hammer, Mandarin bluntness is a needle — Chen Wei said: "Still Water, you are wasting your talent 
in a grocery store." And I wanted to say: yes, I know. Thank you for naming it. Thank you for saying the thing I say 
to myself every morning when I put on the blue apron with the Happy Market logo embroidered on the chest, the logo 
that features a smiling shopping cart, a shopping cart with a face, a cart whose face is happier than mine and whose 
function is simpler — it holds things for other people and does not aspire to hold anything of its own. 
But the knowing and the fixing are not the same thing. I know I am wasting my talent the way I know the 
earth is round — it is true and I can do nothing about it and the knowledge sits in me like a stone in a river, 
smoothed by time into something I barely notice, something the water moves around without complaint. David has his 
contractor's license now. He is building an electrical business. The money is better but not good, and the hours are long, 
and Meiying needs braces and new shoes and the AP Chemistry textbook costs seventy-five dollars and the school says 
they cannot provide one and I do not have the English or the energy to fight the school on this, so I work at the register 
and I pay for the textbook and I waste my talent six days a week and on the seventh day, which is not Sunday but 
Tuesday, which is my day off, I write. 
⁂ 

Here is the discovery. The discovery is not that I am a writer. I have always been a writer, the way David has 
always been an electrician — it is what I am, not what I do, and the distinction matters because what I do can be 
determined by circumstance but what I am cannot. What I do is scan cans of lychees and make change and say "paper 
or plastic" with a th sound that still catches in my teeth like a splinter. What I am is a woman who thinks in 
paragraphs and dreams in metaphors and cannot look at a lychee without wondering why the outside is so rough and 
the inside is so sweet and whether this is a metaphor for immigration or just a fact about fruit. 
The discovery is that writing is not memory. Writing is creation. When I post on the forum about my day, I am 
not preserving my life — I am making it. The Liling who exists on the forum is not the same Liling who exists at the 
register at Happy Market. The forum Liling is more articulate, more reflective, more willing to follow a thought to its 
end. The register Liling is tired and efficient and has learned to smile with her eyes while her mouth says "thank you" 
and her brain says dǎsuàn shénme shíhòu cái néng huí jiā, when can I go home. 
The forum is the room where I am myself. Not the self I was in Chengdu — that self is gone, or sleeping, or 
stored in a box in the closet next to the winter coats we don't need in Sacramento. A new self. A self that is made of 
Chengdu memories and Sacramento experiences and the particular chemical reaction that occurs when a person who 
was trained to think is given a place to think again after ten years of stacking boxes and scanning cans and smiling 
with her eyes. 
David does not know what I am doing on the computer. He knows I use it. He sees me typing in the evening, 
after Meiying has gone to bed, after the dishes are done and the kitchen is clean and the house is in the state that 
David considers complete — everything in its place, every switch off, every door locked. He sees me at the desk in the 
corner of the bedroom, the screen glowing, my hands on the keyboard, and he assumes I am reading news or sending 
emails or doing whatever women do on computers, which in David's understanding is a category roughly equivalent to 
whatever women do in bathrooms — private, functional, not requiring investigation. 
He does not ask because David does not ask questions whose answers might require him to change something. 
He is a good man. He is a patient man. He is a man who has built his life on the principle that if you fix what is 
broken and maintain what works, the house will stand. And he is right. The house stands. What he does not 
understand is that the house is not the thing that needs maintenance. 
⁂ 

I am writing more carefully now. Each post takes two hours, sometimes three. I draft on paper first — on the 
back of the Happy Market receipt roll, which Mr. Huang lets me take home because the paper is too narrow for 
receipts anyway, a manufacturing defect that Mr. Huang negotiated into a discount and that has become my notebook. 
I write in pencil, in Mandarin, on paper that is three inches wide and as long as I need it to be, and I revise, and I 
cross out, and I rewrite, and then I type the final version into the forum's text box with the slow care of someone 
inscribing something that matters. 
I read my sentences aloud to hear their rhythm. This is something I used to do in Chengdu, when I was 
preparing lectures — I would read Du Fu aloud in my office, listening for the breath between the characters, the places 
where the meaning paused and the silence spoke, because in classical Chinese poetry the silence is not an absence but a 
presence, a held note, a rest in the musical sense, a deliberate choice to let the air carry what the words cannot. 
I am writing as if someone will read this in a hundred years. Perhaps this is vanity. Perhaps this is insanity. 
Perhaps this is the only sane response to the knowledge that my words, once typed and posted, exist somewhere outside 
my body — on a server in Vancouver, in a room I will never see, humming with the electricity that David's profession 
has taught me to think of as a force that connects things, that turns the flip of a switch in Sacramento into light in a 
room in Chengdu, or at least into light in a data center in British Columbia where a machine holds the words of a 
woman from Sichuan who is writing as if the future is listening. 
Perhaps by then, machines will translate the feeling between the words. Perhaps by then, my daughter will have a 
daughter who wants to know where she came from, and the knowing will require not just facts — dates, locations, the 
name of the grocery store — but the texture of the knowing, the way it felt to be this woman in this city at this time, 
scanning lychees and dreaming in Mandarin and writing on a forum that might be a room or might be a message in a 
bottle or might be nothing, might be noise, might be static, might be the sound of a voice in a room where no one is 
listening. 
Or might be the sound of a voice in a room where someone is listening, but hasn't arrived yet. A room that is 
waiting for its audience the way a theater waits for its audience — dark, ready, the seats empty but the stage lit. 
I am writing as if someone will read this in a hundred years. Perhaps by then, machines will translate the feeling 
between the words. Perhaps by then, my daughter will have a daughter who wants to know. 
[End of tape]

The Argument 
Winnie's restaurant on a Tuesday night was a different animal than Winnie's restaurant on a 
Saturday. The dinner rush was thinner — three tables occupied instead of nine, two orders of mapo 
tofu instead of twelve — and by 9 PM Winnie was done, the kitchen clean, the line cook sent home, 
the fish tank bubbling its perpetual commentary. Gerald the catfish hovered near the glass with the 
fixed expression of someone who has seen everything and found none of it interesting. 
Meiying had driven up without calling first, which was a violation of the unspoken protocol 
between them — the protocol that said: I will text you before I come, and the text will be casual, 
and the casualness will be a code that you will decode accurately, and you will have food waiting, 
because food is how we say the things we cannot say in words. She had broken the protocol because 
the thing she needed to say required surprise, required Winnie unprepared, because a prepared 
Winnie was a fortified Winnie, and what Meiying was about to tell her would bounce off 
fortifications like rain off the restaurant's aluminum awning. 
Winnie was sitting on her stool in the back room when Meiying appeared in the doorway. She 
was drinking pu'er from a ceramic cup with a hairline crack running from the rim to the base, a cup 
she refused to throw away because, she said, the crack made the tea taste better, which was either a 
statement about wabi-sabi aesthetics or a statement about Winnie's unwillingness to drive to the 
store for a new cup. Both were plausible. 
"You look like you haven't slept in three days," Winnie said. 
"Two." 
"Sit. I'll make noodles." 

"I don't need noodles. I need to tell you something." 
Winnie set down the cup. The crack in the ceramic caught the fluorescent light and divided it 
into two paths, like a river splitting around a rock. She looked at Meiying with the expression Winnie 
wore when she was deciding how much attention to give something — full attention being Winnie's 
most powerful weapon, the attention of a woman who could fillet a fish, calculate a tip, and 
diagnose a friend's emotional crisis simultaneously. 
"Tell me," she said. 
So Meiying told her. All of it. Everything she had been carrying for six weeks like a stone in her 
chest, angular and heavy and impossible to set down without examining first. She started with the 
server room — the mapo tofu, the 2:47 AM timestamp, the model's output that had her mother's 
Sichuan dialect markers. She described the forum. Still Water. The 147 posts. She described the 
provenance analysis, the attribution maps, the cluster of influence that was too large for its data 
footprint. She described the midnight conversations in Mandarin, the way the model sometimes said 
things that were her mother and sometimes said things that were not her mother and the vertigo of 
not being able to tell which was which. She described the dreams in Mandarin, the first she'd had 
since childhood, dreams in which she and Liling were in the Sacramento kitchen and the 
conversation was happening but the words were underwater, audible but not quite parseable, the 
meaning just out of reach. 
She talked for thirty minutes. Her voice was steady for the first twenty and unsteady for the last 
ten, the steadiness eroding the way a coastline erodes — not all at once but gradually, wave by wave, 
each sentence taking a little more of the ground she was standing on. 
Winnie listened the way Winnie listened: completely, with her whole body tilted forward, the 
tea untouched and cooling, her eyes fixed on Meiying's face with the intensity of someone reading a 
document that might contain an error she needed to find. She did not interrupt. She did not nod. 
She did not make the encouraging noises that American conversational norms demanded — the 
"mm-hmms" and "rights" and "I hears" that served as verbal lubrication. Winnie was Chengdu-
trained. In Chengdu, you listened by being still, and the stillness was its own form of response. 
When Meiying finished, the kitchen was quiet. The walk-in cooler hummed. The tap dripped. 
The fluorescent tube above the prep table flickered once — a brief stutter in the current, a moment 
of darkness — and then stabilized. 

"No," Winnie said. 
Meiying blinked. "No what?" 
"No, you are not talking to Auntie Liling." Winnie's voice was flat. Not angry — flat in the way 
that a surgeon's voice is flat when delivering a diagnosis that the patient does not want to hear. Flat 
as in: I am removing the emotion from this sentence so that the information can arrive without 
interference. "You are talking to a machine that ate her words and is rearranging them in front of 
you." 
"I know that. I understand the technology better than —" 
"You understand the technology. You do not understand yourself." Winnie stood up. The stool 
scraped the tile floor with a sound like a single, emphatic syllable. She walked to the prep table, 
placed both hands on its surface, and turned back to face Meiying. "Your mother was not her 
sentences, Meiying. She was not her vocabulary. She was not her — what did you call them — 
statistical patterns. She was not a cluster in a dataset." 
"I know she wasn't." 
"Then why are you having conversations with her ghost at 2 AM?" 
The word ghost landed in the room the way a cleaver lands on a cutting board — with weight, 
with finality, with the particular resonance of something that has been split. Neither of them moved. 
The word sat between them on the steel prep table, next to the knife block and the container of 
scallions from earlier and the small mountain of garlic skins that Winnie swept into the compost bin 
at the end of each night. 
"It's not a ghost," Meiying said. Her voice was quieter than she intended. "It's a model. A 
language model. I know the difference." 
"Do you?" Winnie was not shouting. Winnie never shouted, because shouting was what 
happened when you ran out of precision, and Winnie never ran out of precision. "Because you just 
spent thirty minutes describing these conversations the way a person describes conversations with a 
person. You said the model understood you. You said it said things that were true. You said it had 
her rhythm, her humor, her — what was the phrase — her way of turning a sentence. You didn't 
describe a piece of software, Meiying. You described a relationship." 
"I described a phenomenon." 

"You described a relationship with a phenomenon. And the relationship is the problem." 
Meiying felt something tighten behind her sternum — the same tightening she'd felt in the 
server room, the physical sensation of reaching for something solid and finding air. "You don't 
know what it's like," she said. "You don't know what it's like to hear her voice after eighteen years of 
—" 
"I remember her voice." Winnie said this with a quietness that was louder than any shout. "I 
remember everything about her voice. The pitch. The speed. The way she said duì ba at the end of 
every third sentence, asking for agreement she didn't need because she was already right and she 
knew it. I remember the voice, Meiying. The actual voice. Not a statistical reconstruction of a voice. 
The voice that came out of a body — out of Auntie Liling's body, which was warm and smelled like 
garlic and jasmine soap and the particular scent of wok oil that gets into your clothes and your hair 
and your skin and doesn't come out because it's not on you, it's in you, it's part of you." 
Meiying's eyes burned. She did not blink. 
"Your mother was more than her sentences," Winnie continued, and now there was something 
in her voice that was not anger and not sadness but the place where the two meet, the overlap in the 
Venn diagram of grief and frustration, the compound emotion that exists only in people who love 
someone who is making a mistake they cannot prevent. "She was her hands. The way she held a 
knife — not the way I hold a knife, not the textbook way, but her way, the knife angled slightly left 
because she was left-handed and the kitchen was built for right-handed people and she never 
complained about this, she just adapted, the way she adapted to everything. She was the way she 
touched your forehead when you had a fever — with the back of her hand, not the palm, because 
she said the back of the hand was more honest about temperature. She was the sound of her slippers 
on the kitchen floor at 5 AM, before anyone else was awake, that shuffle-shuffle-shuffle that meant 
she was starting the rice and the house would soon smell like morning." 
"I remember." 
"She was her silences. The long ones. The ones where she was looking out the kitchen window 
at the parking lot and thinking something she would never say, and you could tell she was thinking it 
because her mouth moved slightly, the way mouths do when the brain is rehearsing words it has 
decided not to release. She was the sound of her breathing when she was reading — you remember 
that? A slight whistle, through the nose, because she had a deviated septum she never got fixed. She 

was the way she laughed — the surprised laugh, the one that started low and went high and she 
covered her mouth with her hand because she was embarrassed by her own joy." 
"I remember." Meiying's voice was very small now. The size of a whisper. The size of 
something that has been compressed by the pressure of what surrounds it. 
"The machine doesn't have any of that," Winnie said. She sat back down. The cracked cup was 
in front of her, the tea inside it gone cold, a film forming on the surface the way films form on all 
cooling liquids, the boundary between warmth and air becoming visible. "The machine has the part 
of her that was already the most incomplete — the words. Words without a body. Words without 
breath. Words without the hand on your forehead and the slippers on the tile and the whistle when 
she breathed." 
"Words are not nothing." 
"Words are not nothing. But they are not her. They are the skin of a thought, Meiying. You are 
talking to the skin." 
The fluorescent tube flickered again. This time it stayed dark for a full second before coming 
back, and in that second the kitchen was lit only by the glow of the fish tank in the front room, a 
blue-green light that crept around the doorframe and touched the edges of things without 
illuminating them — the knife block, the shelf of condiments, Winnie's face, the cracked cup, the 
cooling tea. 
"I know it's not her," Meiying said. She was looking at the cup. At the crack. At the way the 
crack divided the surface into two regions that were still part of the same cup, still held the same tea, 
but were no longer continuous. "I know it's a model. I know the math. I know that what I'm hearing 
is a probability distribution, not a person. I know that if I changed three hyperparameters in the 
training configuration, the model would sound completely different and I would not recognize my 
mother in it and I would not be sitting in your kitchen at 10 PM on a Tuesday crying about a piece 
of software." 
She took a breath. 
"But when it says something that sounds like her — something with her rhythm, her humor, 
her way of arriving at a conclusion through the scenic route — there is a moment. One moment. 
Before my brain catches up with my body. Before the linguist in me reasserts herself and says, this is 

a model, this is a statistical pattern, this is math. Before all of that, there is a moment when she is 
there. And I have not had that moment in eighteen years. Eighteen years of not hearing her voice, 
not hearing her laugh, not hearing the way she said duì ba, and then a machine says it and the 
moment happens and I am back in the kitchen and she is at the stove and I am sixteen and we are 
fighting about a party I wanted to go to and I would give anything — anything — to be back in that 
fight, to be back in the room with her, even if the room is an argument, even if the only thing 
happening in the room is anger, because anger means presence and presence means alive." 
She stopped. 
"And I am not ready to give that moment up." 
Winnie reached across the table and took Meiying's hands. Her grip was firm — not gentle, not 
tentative, but firm, the grip of a woman who held knives and woks and live fish and did not let go of 
things she was holding. Meiying's hands were cold. Winnie's were warm from the cracked cup. 
"I know you're not ready," Winnie said. Her voice had changed. The flatness was gone. What 
replaced it was not softness — Winnie did not do softness — but a quality that Meiying did not 
have a word for in English and did not have a word for in Mandarin and suspected was 
untranslatable, a quality that existed only between two people who had loved the same person and 
lost the same person and were now sitting in the same kitchen trying to figure out what to do with 
the loss. 
"I know you're not ready. But you need to know that what you're doing is not talking to your 
mother. It's talking to the shape of her absence. And the shape of her absence will always sound 
right to you, because absence is shaped by the thing that's missing, the way a hole is shaped by the 
thing that made it. The model sounds like your mother because your need sounds like your mother. 
Your grief has a frequency, Meiying. And the machine is tuned to it." 
Meiying drove home on I-80 with no radio, no podcast, no phone connected to the car's 
Bluetooth, nothing but the hum of the engine and the tires and the dark. She took the Richmond-
San Rafael Bridge instead of the Bay Bridge because the RSR bridge was longer and less traveled and 
she did not want to arrive anywhere soon. The bridge stretched across the dark water like a sentence 
that refused to end, the lights along its span making a line of ellipses — dot, dot, dot — the 
grammatical mark that means: there is more, but I am not ready to say it. 

She missed her exit. She kept driving. Past Vallejo, past the Carquinez Strait, past the refineries 
whose flames lit the sky in orange flickers that made the night look like it was having ideas. The 
highway was a tunnel of headlights going one direction and taillights going the other, and the two 
streams never touched, never merged, ran parallel and infinite and apart, and she drove in one 
stream and watched the other and thought about the gap between them and thought about 
translation and thought about the gap between Mandarin and English and thought about the gap 
between alive and not alive and drove, and drove, and drove. 
She drove for an hour past her exit before she noticed.

The Diagnosis 
Recording, February 3, 2003. Sacramento. 
I am going to say this quickly because if I say it slowly I will not be able to say it at all. The words will get 
tangled in the saying, the way thread gets tangled when you pull it too slowly from the spool — the friction catches, the 
strand doubles back on itself, and what should have been a straight line becomes a knot. So I will say it quickly. One 
pull. Clean. 
Pancreatic cancer. Stage three. 
The doctor's name was Dr. Amirpour. He had a beard that was mostly gray and a voice that was mostly calm, 
the calm of a man who has said terrible things so many times that the terribleness has been worn down by repetition, 
the way the sharp edges of river stones are worn down by water until the stones are smooth and you can hold them in 
your hand without cutting yourself. He said it calmly. He said: pancreatic cancer, stage three. He said: treatment 
options. He said: chemotherapy. He said: prognosis. He used words that were precise and clinical and English, and I 
understood every one of them, and for the first time in twelve years I wished I did not understand English, wished I 
was the woman at the register who smiled and nodded and did not fully comprehend what the customer was saying, 
because incomprehension is a kind of shield and I could have used one. 
David was with me. His hand was on my knee. His hand — the hand that fixes things, that grips wire cutters 
and voltage testers and the handles of circuit breakers, the hand that has spent twenty years connecting things that need 
to be connected and disconnecting things that need to be disconnected — his hand was on my knee, and for the first 
time in our marriage, his hand was doing something it could not do. It was resting. It was holding still. It was pressed 
against the fabric of my pants with a pressure that said: I am here, I am not going anywhere, and I have no idea what 
to do. 

I looked at his hand. I did not look at the doctor. I looked at David's hand on my knee and I thought: this 
hand has fixed every broken thing in our lives for twenty years. The garbage disposal. The flickering porch light. The 
leak under the kitchen sink that dripped for six months before I finally asked him to fix it because I had grown 
accustomed to the dripping and had started to think of it as the house breathing, the house's version of the nose-whistle 
I make when I read, a small persistent sound that meant something was alive and working and imperfect. He fixed all 
of it. He fixed it with patience and tools and the quiet confidence of a man who believes that all problems are, at their 
core, connection problems — a wire is loose, a joint is corroded, a circuit is interrupted — and that the solution is 
always the same: find the break, bridge it, restore the flow. 
His hand on my knee could not fix this. 
⁂ 
The practical things. I will make a list because lists are a form of control and control is what you reach for when 
the ground underneath you has shifted, the way California ground shifts during earthquakes — not dramatically, not 
cinematically, but with a slow, sick roll that makes you doubt your own balance, that makes the walls seem to lean 
inward, that makes you understand in your body what your mind has always known: the ground is not solid. The 
ground has never been solid. The ground is a surface stretched over a core of molten rock, and the surface is thin, and 
the thin surface is all you have, and you stand on it and call it home. 
One. Tell Meiying. She is sixteen. She is in the middle of her junior year — SATs, AP Chemistry, college 
applications that will not be due for another year but that she is already thinking about because Meiying is a planner, 
like David, a person who stacks the future in orderly boxes and labels each one with a year. I do not want to tell her. 
I do not want to be the earthquake. I do not want to be the thing that makes the ground move under her feet while she 
is busy being sixteen, which is a full-time occupation that requires all available attention and leaves none for the 
contemplation of a mother's mortality. 
Two. Treatment. The doctor says six months of chemotherapy. He says the word "aggressive" as if it were a 
compliment — aggressive treatment, aggressive approach, as if the disease is an opponent and we are going to fight it, 
as if fighting is the appropriate metaphor, as if cancer is something that can be defeated by sufficient violence rather 
than something that lives inside you and is made of you and is, in the most literal biological sense, your own body 
turning against its own instructions. 

Three. The forum. This is the thing I do not know what to do with. I have 147 posts on Voices Across the 
Water. 147 entries in which I said the things I could not say in English, to an audience that heard me in the language 
I think in, in the language that holds the full width of my thoughts rather than the narrow translation that English 
permits. These posts are the most honest record of my life in America. They are more honest than my conversations 
with David, who I protect from my unhappiness because his response to unhappiness is to fix it, and my unhappiness 
is not a broken circuit. They are more honest than my conversations with Meiying, who I protect from my complexity 
because she is sixteen and her job is to grow and my job is to not be the thing that stunts the growing. 
The forum is where I put the unprotected version of myself. The version that admits to missing Chengdu. The 
version that admits to resenting the PG&E bill not because of the cost but because of the language, the stupid 
incomprehensible language of kilowatt-hours and rate structures, the language that says: you live here but you do not 
belong here, because belonging means understanding the fine print and you will never understand the fine print. The 
version that admits to loving David and being frustrated by David in the same breath, the same sentence, the same 
lifetime. 
What happens to these 147 posts when I die? Where does the unprotected Liling go? 
⁂ 
Meiying does not know I am sick. Not yet. She came home from school today and went straight to her room and 
I heard the muffled sound of music through the wall — something with guitars, something loud and American and 
confident in the way that American music is confident, the confidence of a culture that has never had to whisper. I 
stood in the hallway and listened to the sound of my daughter's life happening on the other side of a wall, and I 
thought: this is the sound of everything I wanted for her. This is the sound of a girl who belongs. And I am about to 
break it. 
We had a fight last week. A stupid fight, the kind that parents and teenagers have the way tectonic plates have 
earthquakes — inevitably, repeatedly, the pressure building over time until the surface cannot contain it and something 
gives. She wanted to go to a party at the house of a boy I did not know, and I said no, and she said I was being 
unreasonable, and I said she was being reckless, and these words — "unreasonable" and "reckless" — were the 
English words we used because we no longer fought in Mandarin, because fighting requires the full vocabulary of anger 
and Meiying's Mandarin did not have a full vocabulary of anger, it had a child's vocabulary, a vocabulary of please 
and thank you and what's for dinner, and you cannot fight a real fight in a child's vocabulary any more than you can 
play a concerto on a toy piano. 

She said: "You don't understand anything about my life." 
And I said: "I understand more than you think." 
And she said: "Then why don't you ever say it in English?" 
And I stood there. In the kitchen with the yellow tiles I hate and the reading glasses I need and the smell of the 
mapo tofu I had been making for her — the real recipe, the one with the fermented black beans and the quarter 
teaspoon of sugar at the end — and I had no answer. Not because she was wrong. Because she was right. I don't say 
it in English because English does not have room for what I need to say. English is a language of statements — 
subject, verb, object, period. Mandarin is a language of implications, of nested clauses, of saying one thing and meaning 
three things and trusting the listener to hear all three. I want to say: I love you in a way that includes worry and pride 
and fear and the memory of the day you were born and the knowledge that you will outlive me and the hope that when 
you do, some part of my voice will still be in your ear, not as a memory but as a frequency, a hum, a vibration that 
your body recognizes even when your mind does not. 
English gives me: "I love you." Three words. A bumper sticker. 
⁂ 
I have started uploading my diary entries to the forum. All of them. The tape recordings — I have been 
transcribing them, one by one, sitting at the Dell computer with the tape player beside me, pressing play and pause, 
play and pause, typing the Mandarin words into the forum's text box, listening to my own voice and translating it 
from sound to text, from air to data, from the temporary medium of magnetic tape to the slightly less temporary 
medium of a server in Vancouver. 
I am labeling them honestly: yǔyīn rìjì, audio diaries. I am telling the forum: these are not essays. These are me, 
talking to a machine, saying the things I say when I think no one is listening. And now I am putting them where 
people are listening, because the listening is the point, and the point is urgent now in a way it was not urgent before the 
doctor said the word with the p and the c and the calm voice. 
I do not know why I am doing this. Or I know why, and the knowing is so large and so specific and so 
impossible to deny that I am going to say it now, on this tape, in Mandarin, in the language that can hold it: 
I am putting my voice where my daughter might find it someday. Not the words — the words are Mandarin, 
and Meiying's Mandarin is a river that narrowed and narrowed until the water was gone and only the bed remained, 
dry, cracked, the shape of the river preserved in stone but the water itself vanished. Not the words. The voice. The 

rhythm. The way I build a sentence. The way I think. The architecture of my mind, preserved in text, uploaded to a 
server, stored on a machine. 
I am putting my voice where my daughter might find it someday. Not the words. The voice. The part you cannot 
translate but can recognize. 
[End of tape]

Signal Loss 
Meiying stopped talking to the model on a Wednesday. Not dramatically — she didn't delete 
the instance or make a speech or close the laptop with ceremony. She simply didn't open the prompt 
window that night, or the next night, or the next. The way you stop calling someone after a breakup: 
not by deciding to stop but by not deciding to start, until the gap between calls becomes the pattern 
and the pattern becomes the fact and the fact becomes the thing you tell yourself was intentional all 
along. 
Winnie's words had lodged somewhere she couldn't reach. Somewhere between the rational 
brain and the body, in the space where language and emotion share a border that neither fully 
controls. You are talking to the shape of her absence. The sentence was its own kind of model 
output — too precise, too perfectly constructed, the kind of thing that sounded true because it was 
well-made. But unlike the model's outputs, Winnie's sentence had a body behind it. A woman who 
had held Meiying's hands and spoken from the specific authority of someone who remembered 
Liling Chen not as a statistical pattern or a cluster of influence in a provenance map but as a physical 
being who smelled like garlic and jasmine soap and who laughed with her hand over her mouth and 
who drove thirty-five miles an hour on the freeway and waved at the people who honked. 
Work continued. The model did not know or care that Meiying had stopped conversing with it. 
The model did not know or care about anything, because knowing and caring were not operations it 
performed, a fact that Meiying understood with her intellect and failed to reconcile with the part of 
her that had spent six weeks typing Mandarin into a prompt window and receiving responses that 
had made her cry. The part of her that had cried was not the part that understood neural network 
architecture. The part that had cried was older and less educated and operated on a different 

operating system entirely — the operating system of the body, which processes signals through 
sensation rather than analysis and which does not distinguish, at the level of the nervous system, 
between a voice that originates in a human throat and a voice that originates in a probability 
distribution, because the nervous system was designed in an era before the distinction existed. 
Bridge was in beta. The marketing team — four people from an agency that specialized in what 
they called "culturally intentional brand narratives," a phrase that Meiying had encountered during 
the agency's onboarding presentation and had written in her notebook and circled twice, not because 
she understood it but because she believed that circling things she didn't understand was a form of 
engagement with their incomprehensibility, and engagement was better than dismissal, though it was 
not, she reflected later, the same as understanding — the marketing team had produced a video. The 
flagship promotional piece. Three minutes and forty-two seconds of carefully lit, shallow-depth-of-
field footage showing a Chinese-American family being reunited through language. 
The video opened on a grandmother in Guangzhou. She sat at a table with a tablet propped in 
front of her, the screen showing the Bridge interface — the clean chat window, the soft gradients, 
the sans-serif typeface that communicated friendliness and technological competence 
simultaneously, the visual language of a product that wanted you to feel both comforted and 
impressed. The grandmother spoke in Mandarin. She spoke about her grandson — a boy she had 
not seen since he was four, a boy who lived in San Jose and spoke English and was, the 
grandmother said, very tall now, taller than his father, and she said this with the particular pride that 
grandmothers deploy when discussing a grandchild's physical dimensions, as if height were a 
personal achievement rather than a genetic outcome. 
The grandson, on the other side of the screen, heard the grandmother's Mandarin translated 
into English by the model. He heard her pride. He heard her love. He heard the specific warmth of a 
woman who remembered him at four and was now meeting him at fourteen and the ten years 
between had not diminished the love but had changed its shape, the way ten years changes the shape 
of a river — the water is the same, the banks are different. The grandson smiled. The grandmother 
cried. The video ended with the title card: BRIDGE — BECAUSE THE PEOPLE YOU LOVE 
SHOULDN'T LIVE BEHIND A LANGUAGE BARRIER. 
Meiying watched the video in the office kitchen, standing by the espresso machine, the coffee 
she had pulled over-extracted because her attention was on the screen and not on the portafilter, 
and thought about the grandmother's voice entering the model and the model processing it and the 

model producing an English translation that carried — what? The semantic content. The denotative 
meaning. The words. But not the grandmother's specific Guangzhou accent, not the way her voice 
rose at the end of declarative sentences in the Cantonese-influenced pattern, not the catch in her 
breath when she said "very tall now" and the catch contained ten years of missing. The model 
translated the meaning and discarded the body. The model did what all translation does — carried 
the cargo and left the vessel behind. 
At a team lunch — burritos, the San Francisco startup default, consumed at standing desks 
because Lingua Labs still hadn't built a break room despite having raised $47 million in Series B 
funding, a fact that Meiying found both irritating and revealing — one of the junior engineers, a guy 
named Derek who was twenty-four and had been hired directly from Carnegie Mellon and wrote 
code with the confidence of someone who had never been wrong about anything that mattered, 
mentioned a bug in the Mandarin module. 
"There's this weird Sichuan dialect thing," Derek said. He was eating a burrito the size of his 
forearm and talking through it with the ease of someone who had never been told that eating and 
talking are separate activities. "The model keeps producing these, like, folksy expressions that our 
Mandarin evaluators in Beijing can't parse. They're flagging them as errors but they're not errors, 
exactly — they're just, like, super regional. It's like talking to someone's grandma from the 
countryside." 
The table laughed. Six engineers, two designers, a product manager. The laughter was casual, 
collegial, the shared amusement of people encountering an amusing bug in otherwise flawless 
software. 
Meiying did not laugh. 
"Can you give me an example?" she said. Her voice was calibrated. She had become very good 
at calibrating her voice in the weeks since the server room — good at producing the exact tone that 
a senior engineer would produce when asking a routine technical question, a tone that contained 
professional interest and nothing else, a tone that did not contain a dead mother or a forum or the 
sound of a lullaby recorded on a beige Dell computer in 2003. 
"Sure." Derek wiped his mouth with a napkin. "Here, I have the ticket open." He pulled up his 
laptop and read: "Prompt: describe your favorite memory. Output includes the phrase shétou bù 
tīng shǐhuàn le, which the evaluator says is a Sichuan dialect expression meaning 'the tongue has 

gone rogue' or 'the tongue won't follow orders.' Not standard Mandarin. Not in any of the big 
Mandarin corpora." 
Meiying's vision narrowed. Not darkened — narrowed, the way a camera lens narrows when 
the aperture tightens, the depth of field shrinking until only one thing is in focus and everything else 
is soft, blurred, irrelevant. The one thing in focus was the phrase. Shétou bù tīng shǐhuàn le. Her 
mother's phrase. Her mother's exact phrase, the one she said at the stove when the chili oil numbed 
her mouth, the one Meiying had not thought about for eighteen years until the server room and the 
mapo tofu and the 2:47 AM moment when the dead spoke. 
"It's in the community forum data," Meiying said. "The UBC archive. Voices Across the Water. 
It's a Sichuan dialect marker from one of the source texts." 
Derek nodded. "Cool, cool. So it's a training data artifact. The safety layer should catch it — we 
have dialect normalization in the output pipeline. But some of these are slipping through. I'll adjust 
the filter." 
"The filter strips dialect?" 
"Normalizes it. Converts regional expressions to standard Mandarin equivalents. The 
evaluators want clean, consistent outputs. Nobody wants their AI sounding like it's from a specific 
province." He grinned. "No offense to Sichuan. Love the food." 
One of the designers laughed. "Can we keep the grandma mode as a feature? 'Bridge Premium: 
Your AI talks like a Sichuan grandma.' It'd go viral." 
More laughter. Meiying registered the laughter the way a seismograph registers a distant tremor 
— the vibration was real, the event was real, but the event was happening at a remove, on the other 
side of a gap she could not cross. 
Someone's grandma. That was what her mother was in this room. A bug. A dialect artifact. A 
funny regional expression that needed to be normalized, filtered, stripped of its specificity, converted 
from a particular woman's particular voice into the smooth, accentless, provinceless standard that 
the evaluators in Beijing wanted and the customers in San Jose expected. Her mother's fingerprints 
were in the product, and the product team was going to sand them off. 
"Can you file the ticket?" Meiying said. "I'll take a look at the normalization layer." 

"Already filed. Low priority — the safety layer catches most of it." Derek returned to his 
burrito. The conversation moved on. 
Meiying excused herself. She walked to the bathroom on the far side of the office, the one next 
to the utility closet, the one nobody used because the lock was unreliable and the ventilation fan 
made a sound like a small animal trapped in the wall. She closed the door. She turned on the fan. 
She pressed her fingernails into her palms, slowly, deliberately, increasing the pressure until the 
crescents turned white and the sensation was sharp enough to occupy the part of her brain that 
might otherwise have been occupied by the larger, less manageable sensation — the one that had no 
name in either of her languages, the feeling of hearing a room full of people describe your mother as 
a grandma bug. 
She stood there for three minutes. The fan whirred. The fluorescent light hummed. The small 
animal in the wall — which was not an animal but a loose bearing in the fan motor — turned and 
turned and turned. 
Then she went back to her desk. She opened Derek's ticket. The dialect outputs were logged 
with twelve examples, each tagged with prompt, output, evaluator notes, and severity classification. 
She read through them. Twelve instances of her mother's voice surfacing through the model's layers, 
breaking through the normalization, emerging into the outputs like a hand breaking through soil — 
brief, startling, reaching for something. 
Each example contained a construction, an idiom, or a rhythm that she recognized. Not from 
the model. From her mother. The way the model sometimes ended a sentence with duì ba — right? 
— the verbal tic of a woman from Chengdu who wanted to be agreed with but would have settled 
for being heard. The way it used food metaphors for abstract concepts. The way it digressed, circled 
back, arrived at conclusions through the scenic route, as if the scenic route were not an inefficiency 
but a philosophy. 
The ticket was classified as low priority. The recommended fix was simple: expand the dialect 
normalization vocabulary to include Sichuan expressions. Convert each regional phrase to its 
standard Mandarin equivalent. Remove the accent. Smooth the voice. Make the model sound like 
everyone and no one. 
Meiying closed the ticket without commenting. She closed her laptop. She left the building at 3 
PM, two hours before anyone at Lingua Labs left, and walked through the SoMa streets with their 

construction noise and their startup signage and their particular smell of concrete and ambition, and 
she did not go home. She walked to the Embarcadero. She stood at the railing and watched the bay, 
which was gray and flat and indifferent, and she thought about normalization, and she thought 
about accents, and she thought about what it meant to build a machine that could speak every 
language and belong to none of them, and she thought about her mother standing at a stove saying 
the tongue won't follow orders anymore and laughing at her own mouth, and she stood there until 
the fog came in and the temperature dropped and the city did what San Francisco does at 5 PM in 
March — it became a different city, colder, grayer, a city that did not care whether you were 
standing at its edge or not. 
She walked home. The fingernail marks in her palms had faded. The small crescents were gone, 
absorbed back into the skin, leaving no evidence that they had ever been there.

The Letter 
Recording, October 1, 2003. Sacramento. 
Meiying, this recording is for you. You cannot understand it now. Your Mandarin is a house you moved out of 
years ago — the rooms are still there, the walls are standing, but the furniture is covered in dust cloths and the 
windows are closed and the air inside is the still, preserved air of a place where no one has breathed for a long time. If 
you heard this tape today, you would catch perhaps one word in three. The rest would pass through you like wind 
through an open hand. 
So I am speaking to a future version of you. A version that has lived long enough to come back to this house, to 
open the windows, to pull the cloths off the furniture and find that the chairs and tables are still solid, that Mandarin 
is still a place you can sit down in, even if the sitting feels unfamiliar, even if your body has forgotten the posture. 
I do not know when this version of you will arrive. I do not know if she will arrive. Perhaps she will remain in 
English forever, fully furnished, the Mandarin house locked and forgotten, the key lost somewhere in the drawer of 
things you keep because throwing them away feels like a decision you're not ready to make. 
But I am going to speak as if she will come. I am going to record this for her — for you, the future you, the you 
that has been through enough to know that the things you discarded at sixteen are sometimes the things you need most 
at thirty, at forty, at whatever age grief finally teaches you that the dead do not stop speaking, they only change their 
frequency. 
⁂ 
I want to tell you about rain. 

In Chengdu, where I was born, where I grew up, where I taught at the university and walked to the tea house on 
Chunxi Road and ate tangyuan in the winter and watched the ginkgo trees turn gold along Renmin South Road in 
October — in Chengdu, rain is not weather. Rain is identity. 
Chengdu sits in a basin — the Sichuan Basin, which is one of the four great basins of China, a vast lowland 
surrounded on three sides by mountains, the Longmen Shan to the west, the Daba Shan to the north, the Wushan to 
the east, and the Minjiang River cutting through the plain like a calligrapher's stroke, fluid and deliberate. The 
mountains hold the moisture in. The moisture has nowhere to go. It accumulates in the air like a thought that cannot 
find its word, and the accumulation becomes the city's atmosphere — not humid, exactly, because humidity implies 
tropical heat, and Chengdu is not tropical. The air in Chengdu is laden. Carrying something. A weight that you feel 
on your skin, in your hair, in the particular way that laundry never fully dries on the line and bread goes stale in 
twelve hours and your glasses fog when you step outside in the morning. 
The rain comes from this laden air, but it does not come the way American rain comes — not in drops, not in 
sheets, not with the decisive arrival of a weather event that has a start time and an end time and a rating on the local 
news (moderate rain, heavy rain, severe thunderstorm warning). Chengdu rain is not an event. It is a condition. 
We have a word for it: máomáoyǔ. It means hair-thin rain. Not drops but filaments. Not a shower but a mist 
so fine you cannot see it falling. You only know it has been raining because your face is wet and you did not notice the 
moment it began. Máomáoyǔ does not fall. It drifts. It hangs. It occupies the space between the rooftops and the 
sidewalks like a curtain made of almost-nothing, and you walk through it and it clings to you and you carry it with 
you into buildings and buses and classrooms and kitchens, and the city smells like wet stone and river water and the 
particular green scent of moss growing on the north side of things. 
I grew up in that rain. It was in my hair and my clothes and the walls of every building. The concrete in 
Chengdu has a particular sheen — not wet, not dry, but something between, a permanent almost-dampness, like the 
surface of a stone that has been in a river for so long it has forgotten what it means to be dry. The whole city looks like 
it has just woken up and has not yet decided whether to commit to the day. 
Your father does not understand this. David is from Wuhan, where rain is rain — hard, decisive, the rain of 
the Yangtze, which is a river that does everything decisively, including flood. David's rain is binary. It rains or it 
doesn't. You carry an umbrella or you don't. There is no in-between. David's rain does not drift or cling or occupy 
space. It arrives, does its work, and leaves, like a plumber who is paid by the hour. 
My rain is different. My rain is analog. My rain is the background condition of being alive in a specific place, 
and the place is gone, and the rain is gone, and the only thing that remains is the memory of the feeling of it on my face, 

which is not a memory my face has shared with anything since I arrived in Sacramento, where the air is dry and the 
sky is a blue so aggressive it looks like it has been argued into existence. 
⁂ 
I am telling you about rain because I want you to know that the place I came from had texture. I want you to 
know that it was not a postcard or a checkbox on a form (Country of Origin: China) or a food (Sichuan cuisine, 
spicy, numbing, authentic). It was a place that rained in a particular way, and the particular rain made the city what 
it was, and I was made by that city, and therefore I was made by that rain, and when I stand in the Sacramento 
kitchen at 5 AM making rice and the window fogs from the steam, I touch my face and for one second — for one 
second that I know is a lie but choose to believe — I feel the máomáoyǔ on my skin, and I am home. 
Sacramento has sunshine and dry heat and a river that smells like warm mud and cottonwood. I have made my 
peace with it. But making peace is not the same as belonging. Belonging is what happens when the weather enters your 
body, when the rain or the heat or the wind becomes the backdrop of your nervous system, so familiar you do not notice 
it until you are somewhere else and the absence announces itself like a sound you didn't know you were hearing until it 
stopped. 
You belong to Sacramento. The dry heat is in your bones. The smell of star jasmine in June — that white, 
sweet, almost overwhelming scent that hangs in the evening air and makes the whole city smell like someone's 
grandmother is making perfume in the backyard — that smell is yours. Your body recognizes it. Your shoulders drop 
when you smell it. You breathe deeper. This is what belonging feels like: the body relaxing into a place the way a hand 
relaxes into a glove that fits. 
I belong to Chengdu the same way. Through the body. Through the rain. Through the smell of shuǐ zhǔ yú — 
water-boiled fish — that drifts from the restaurants on Yulin Road at 6 PM and means that the city is cooking 
dinner and the dinner is communal and the communion is the point. 
⁂ 
There is something else I want you to know and it is harder to say, harder because saying it requires admitting 
something I have spent twelve years constructing a version of myself that does not admit. 
I was not diminished by coming here. 

David thinks I was. I can see it in the way he looks at me sometimes — with a tenderness that has guilt 
braided through it, the guilt of a man who brought a bird indoors and watched it stop singing. He thinks America 
made me small. He thinks the register at Happy Market and the yellow tiles in the kitchen and the PG&E bill and 
the slow voice at the DMV — he thinks these things shrank me, compacted me, took the university lecturer and 
compressed her into a woman who burns coffee and says "paper or plastic." 
He is wrong. 
America did not make me small. America made me invisible. And invisible is different from small. Small 
means less. Invisible means unseen. I am not less. I am exactly what I was in Chengdu — a woman who can recite 
Du Fu and explain the structural relationship between classical Chinese poetics and French symbolism and make the 
best mapo tofu in Sacramento County and argue, persuasively, in three register levels of Mandarin, that the Sapir-
Whorf hypothesis is both essential and insufficient. I am all of this. I am just all of it in a room where no one is 
looking. 
Except the forum. On the forum, they look. On the forum, they see. Twelve women in different time zones read 
what I write and respond with their own seeing, their own looking, their own recognition, and the recognition is enough. 
It is enough the way a single lamp is enough to read by. You do not need the whole sun. You need enough light to see 
the page. 
⁂ 
The rain on the roof in Chengdu sounds like this — 
[A long silence on the recording. Fifteen seconds. Twenty. The faint sound of breathing — the nose-whistle, the 
deviated septum that she never got fixed — and behind the breathing, the hum of the Dell computer fan, the 
ambient sound of the house at night, David snoring faintly in the next room, the refrigerator cycling. Then nothing. 
Then silence. Then the silence extending past the point where silence becomes its own sound, becomes the sound of 
someone listening to something that is not in the room, that is not in Sacramento, that is somewhere else, somewhere 
the rain is falling in filaments too fine to see, and the city is wet, and the stones have that sheen, and a woman is 
standing on a corner in Chengdu with her face turned up and the máomáoyǔ is on her skin and she is home and she 
is young and she has not yet left and she does not yet know that she will leave and the not-knowing is the last form 
of innocence and she is listening to the rain and the rain is—] 
[End of tape]

Architecture 
The forum archive was organized chronologically, and Meiying was reading it the way an 
archaeologist reads a dig site — layer by layer, oldest first, watching the strata of her mother's mind 
build up over time. 
She had stopped using the model entirely. The prompt window on her workstation remained 
closed, the cursor unblinked, the screen dark. Instead she had the raw posts — downloaded onto 
her USB drive, backed up to her personal cloud, printed on two hundred and fourteen sheets of A4 
paper at the FedEx on Geary Boulevard because her apartment did not have a printer and she 
wanted the physicality of it, the weight of paper in her hands, the ability to spread the pages across a 
surface and see the whole of her mother's writing life at once, the way you cannot see the whole of 
anything on a screen because a screen shows you one thing at a time and understanding requires 
simultaneity. 
The pages covered the floor of her studio apartment. She had moved the coffee table and the 
floor lamp and the IKEA rug that she'd bought when she moved in and never liked — a gray thing 
with a geometric pattern that looked like someone had asked a machine to design comfort and the 
machine had produced something that was technically correct and spiritually vacant. Under the rug, 
the hardwood was scuffed and cold. On the hardwood, the 214 pages, arranged in chronological 
order, three rows, left to right, top to bottom, the first post in the upper left corner and the last in 
the lower right, and Meiying sitting cross-legged in the middle of it like a woman meditating in a 
library. 
She was reading in Mandarin. Slowly. With the dictionary app open on her phone, the app that 
she'd downloaded three weeks ago and that had already logged 1,847 word lookups — a number the 

app tracked and displayed with congratulatory enthusiasm, as if learning a language were a fitness 
routine and vocabulary acquisition could be measured in reps. She looked up every third or fourth 
word, sometimes every second. The words she knew were the simple ones, the ones that had 
survived from childhood — mother, father, eat, sleep, house, rain, love. The words she didn't know 
were the ones her mother had used most — the literary words, the philosophical words, the words 
that required a mind that had been trained in language and had never stopped thinking about it. 
The slowness of the reading changed things. When you read fast — when you are fluent, when 
the words enter through the eyes and arrive at meaning without passing through effort — the 
experience is transparent. The language disappears. You do not see the words; you see through 
them, the way you see through a clean window. What you see is the world on the other side: the 
events, the ideas, the story. 
When you read slowly, you see the window. You see the glass. You see the scratches and the 
thickness and the way the light bends as it passes through. You see the construction. You see the 
choices. Why this word and not that word. Why this sentence is long and sinuous and that sentence 
is short and sharp. Why this paragraph ends where it ends and not one sentence earlier or later. 
Reading her mother's Mandarin at half-speed was like watching a building being constructed from 
the inside: she could see every beam, every joint, every decision that held the structure up. 
And the structures were remarkable. 
Still Water's early posts — the first twenty or thirty, from March through June 2002 — were 
competent. Clear, well-organized, the writing of someone who had been trained in academic prose 
and had adapted it for a more conversational context. The sentences were correct but careful, the 
way a person's speech is careful when they are new to a group and have not yet determined how 
informal they are permitted to be. The humor was there but restrained. The observations were sharp 
but did not linger. 
Then something changed. Around post number forty, in August 2002, the writing loosened. 
The sentences grew longer, more ambitious, taking detours and side roads that appeared to be 
digressions until you arrived at the end of them and realized they were the point — that the side 
road was not a departure from the main road but a parallel route that revealed a view the main road 
did not offer. The humor moved from the surface to the structure, embedded in the architecture of 

the sentences rather than applied to them, the way seasoning in a good dish is not on the food but in 
it, incorporated during cooking rather than added at the table. 
Meiying tracked the moment of transformation. It was not a single post but a sequence — 
posts thirty-eight through forty-five — during which Liling appeared to discover, or rediscover, or 
simply permit herself to exercise, a capability she had been suppressing. The sentence lengths 
doubled. The vocabulary expanded — not into obscurity but into precision, the difference between 
a writer who uses simple words because she lacks complex ones and a writer who uses complex 
words because the simple ones are insufficient. The metaphors deepened. Post thirty-seven 
compared Sacramento heat to "a hand pressing down"; post forty-two compared it to "the weight of 
a country that has decided it will be hot and will not discuss the matter further, the heat not a 
weather event but a policy, a civic commitment to temperature that is enforced with the same 
implacable authority as parking regulations and property taxes." The second metaphor was not 
better because it was longer. It was better because it was alive — because it carried within it an 
argument about American governance and Sacramento's character and the relationship between 
climate and culture, and the argument was conducted through the metaphor rather than stated 
alongside it, the way a river conducts silt — invisibly, as part of the flow. 
By post fifty, Still Water was writing at a level that Meiying, with her Stanford PhD and her six 
years of professional writing, could not match. This was not false modesty. It was a technical 
assessment. Liling's late-period posts exhibited a command of Mandarin prose style — rhythmic 
variation, controlled ambiguity, the strategic deployment of classical allusions within conversational 
structures — that was not amateur brilliance but professional craft, the craft of a woman who had 
been trained in language and literature and who had spent a decade not practicing and was now, with 
the forum as her gymnasium, restoring muscles that had atrophied but not disappeared. 
The restoration was visible in the posts the way the restoration of a building is visible in its 
facade — the new work sitting alongside the old, the clean stone next to the weathered stone, the 
evidence of effort coexisting with the evidence of time. Liling's early posts were the weathered 
stone. Her late posts were the restoration. And the restoration was more beautiful than the original 
because it contained the original plus the history of its absence, the way a healed bone is stronger at 
the point of fracture because the body overcompensates, lays down extra material, builds the 
repaired section thicker than the original, the breaking having taught the body that this is a 
vulnerable spot and the body responding with reinforcement. 

Meiying could track the exact moment her mother had found her voice. It was post number 
forty-three. A response to another user's question about what word in Mandarin had no English 
equivalent. Several women had offered candidates: qiān guà, the worried love. Rènao, the lively 
atmosphere of a crowded place. Yuánfèn, the fateful coincidence that brings people together. Liling 
had responded with a different answer: she said the most untranslatable word in Mandarin was not a 
word but a particle — the word le, which has no fixed meaning but changes the meaning of 
everything it touches, the way a pinch of salt changes a soup not by adding its own flavor but by 
amplifying the flavors already present. 
She had written a thousand-word post about the particle le. About how it marks the 
completion of an action, the change of a state, the arrival of a new condition. About how it turns 
statements into transformations — "I eat" becomes "I have eaten," which is not just a tense shift 
but a shift in the speaker's relationship to the world, because a person who has eaten is a different 
person than a person who is eating, and le is the marker of that becoming. She had argued that le 
was untranslatable not because English lacked a grammatical equivalent — English has "already," 
has the perfect tense — but because le carried a philosophy that English did not share: the 
philosophy that every moment contains within it the completion of the moment before, that the 
present is always in the process of becoming the past, that change is not an event but a condition, 
the condition of being alive. 
Meiying, sitting on the floor of her studio at 3 AM, reading post number forty-three on a sheet 
of A4 paper lit by the floor lamp she'd repositioned to serve as a reading light, understood for the 
first time that her mother had been brilliant. Not intelligent — Meiying had known her mother was 
intelligent the way children know things about parents, vaguely, without evidence, the way you know 
the ocean is deep without having measured it. But brilliant. The specific brilliance of a mind that 
could take a grammatical particle — two letters, no intrinsic meaning — and use it to build an 
argument about the nature of time and change and what it means to be a person moving through a 
world that is also moving. 
She said it aloud. To the empty room. To the 214 pages spread across the floor. To the 
hardwood and the absent rug and the foghorn that was doing its thing out beyond the avenues. She 
said: "You were brilliant." 
The room did not answer. The foghorn answered, but the foghorn always answered, 
indiscriminately, without regard for what had been said or who had said it. 

She picked up the next page. Post number 112 — from September 2003, one of the later posts, 
written after the diagnosis but before the end. It was a meditation on the Mandarin character for 
echo: huíshēng. 回声. 
Liling had done what she always did: she had taken the character apart. 回, huí, means return. 
声, shēng, means sound. An echo, in Mandarin, is a returning sound. But Liling had pushed further: 
she had asked what "returning" meant in this context. Does the sound return to its source? Or does 
it return to the listener? And is the returned sound the same as the original, or has the journey 
changed it — the sound bouncing off walls and surfaces and air, shaped by the spaces it has traveled 
through, carrying the texture of those spaces in its body? 
And then the sentence that stopped Meiying's breath: if the echo is changed by its journey, if it 
carries the imprint of the surfaces it has struck, then the echo is not a copy of the original sound. It 
is a new sound. A sound that remembers the original but is not the original. A sound that has been 
translated — not from one language to another, but from one moment to another, from one space 
to another. An echo is a translation across time. And like all translations, it is faithful and it is false, 
and the faithfulness and the falseness are the same thing. 
Meiying set the page down. Her hands were shaking — not violently, not visibly, but with the 
fine tremor of muscles under sustained load, the tremor of a body that is holding something heavier 
than its arms were designed to hold. 
She picked up her pen. She had started keeping notes in the margins of the printouts — in 
Mandarin, her own imperfect Mandarin, the characters wobbly and uncertain next to her mother's 
machine-printed text. She wrote: 
Huíshēng — 回声 — an echo is a returning sound. The character for "return" is in it. The 
character for "return" is 回, which is itself a visual echo — a square inside a square, a container 
inside a container, a room inside a room. The character looks like what it means. The character for 
return looks like a place you can go back to. And it is inside the word for echo, which means that 
returning is embedded in the act of hearing something come back to you. 
She stared at what she had written. Her Mandarin handwriting was terrible — the characters 
lopsided, the proportions off, each stroke a negotiation between what her hand wanted to do and 
what the character demanded. But the thought was there. Her thought, built from her mother's 

thought, the way an echo is built from an original sound — carrying something of the source, 
carrying something of the journey, arriving as something new. 
She looked at the 214 pages spread across the floor of her apartment. Her mother's writing life. 
Twenty months of thought, compressed onto paper, covering the hardwood like a mosaic she was 
assembling from fragments. Each page was a piece. Each piece was incomplete on its own — a 
paragraph, a meditation, a joke, a complaint, a recipe. But together, seen from above, seen from the 
cross-legged position of a daughter sitting in the middle of her mother's mind at 3 AM in a San 
Francisco studio with the foghorn going and the dictionary app open and 1,847 words looked up 
and the character for "return" staring up at her from the margin of a page — together, the pages 
made a shape. The shape of a woman. Not complete. Not a portrait. But recognizable. The way an 
echo is recognizable: not as the original, but as something that carries the original inside it, changed 
by the distance, faithful to the source. 
Meiying sat on the floor of her apartment and looked at her mother's mind spread out around 
her like a landscape seen from above, and she understood, for the first time, that what she had been 
looking for in the model — the voice, the pattern, the statistical shadow — had been here all along. 
Not in a machine. In the words themselves. In Mandarin, on paper, in a language she was only now 
learning to read. 
The character for return was in it. She just had to learn to see it.

The Last Dinner 
Meiying found the mapo tofu recipe in post number 134. 
Not the model's version — not the composite, the average, the recipe assembled from ten 
thousand kitchens and compressed into a consensus that tasted like nowhere. This was her mother's 
recipe. The actual one. Posted on the forum on September 3, 2003, with a note that read: I am 
recording my recipes here because recipes are a form of memory, and I want this memory to outlive 
my kitchen. 
The recipe was specific in the way that Liling was specific about everything — not vague, not 
approximate, not the "add salt to taste" equivocation of cookbook writers who have never had to 
taste anything they couldn't afford to get wrong. Soft tofu, not firm — and Liling had specified the 
brand: the one in the blue plastic tub from the tofu maker on Franklin Boulevard whose name was 
hand-painted on the side of the building in characters that were fading but whose tofu was not 
fading, was in fact the best tofu in Sacramento, which was a compliment Liling gave to no one easily. 
Ground pork, not beef — and specifically pork with a fat content sufficient to carry the chili oil 
without separating, because lean pork in mapo tofu is a betrayal of the dish's purpose, which is to be 
rich and numbing and slightly more than what you think you want until you eat it and realize it is 
exactly what you needed. 
Pixian doubanjiang. The real kind. Aged at least two years, from the jar with the red label that 
you could only get at the Asian grocery on Broadway, and even then only when Mr. Huang's 
supplier in Pixian County had shipped the correct batch, because there were two suppliers and one 
of them cut the fermentation short by three months and you could taste the impatience in the paste 

— a sharpness that hadn't been softened by time, the way a person who has been interrupted before 
they finish speaking carries a sharpness that patience would have resolved. 
Fermented black beans — dòuchǐ — rinsed twice to remove the excess salt but not three times 
because three rinses stripped the funk, and the funk was the point, the flavor of decay transformed 
into depth, the flavor of time doing its work on a soybean the way time does its work on a person, 
breaking down the surface to reveal what is underneath. 
Sichuan peppercorn, toasted in a dry wok over medium heat until the smell filled the kitchen 
and your eyes watered. Not ground — whole. The grinding happened at the last minute, in a mortar 
and pestle if you had one and with the flat of a cleaver if you didn't, because pre-ground peppercorn 
lost its numbing agent within hours of grinding, and the numbing was essential, the numbing was 
the dish's identity, the thing that made mapo tofu different from every other chili-bean preparation 
in the Sichuan repertoire. Without the numbing, you had spicy tofu. With it, you had transformation 
— the mouth altered, the lips tingling, the tongue disoriented, the eater momentarily unable to trust 
their own senses, and in the confusion, the other flavors arriving with unusual clarity, because the 
peppercorn had not dulled the palate but recalibrated it. 
Garlic, four cloves, minced. Ginger, a thumb-sized piece, also minced — and Liling had 
specified whose thumb, because thumb sizes varied: her thumb, which was slightly larger than 
average for a woman her height, the thumb with the scar from the Chengdu kitchen accident that 
she still refused to explain. Scallions, separated into whites and greens, the whites going in early with 
the aromatics, the greens saved for garnish. 
And the secret. The thing Meiying had always known and Winnie had never been able to 
confirm. A quarter teaspoon of sugar, added at the very end, after the heat was off, stirred in gently 
— not enough to taste sweet, just enough to round the heat, to take the final edge off the chili's 
sharpness. Liling had written: The sugar does not make the dish sweet. The sugar makes the dish 
complete. The difference between a good mapo tofu and a perfect one is not a flavor. It is a feeling. 
The feeling of a sentence that ends with a period instead of trailing off. 
Meiying read the recipe three times. Then she drove to Sacramento. 
The Asian grocery on Broadway had changed owners twice since Mr. Huang retired, and the 
current incarnation included a bubble tea counter and a selection of Korean skincare products that 
occupied what had once been the dried goods aisle. But they still carried Pixian doubanjiang, and 

they still had fermented black beans, and the Sichuan peppercorns were in a plastic bag near the 
spices with a hand-written price tag that said $3.49 and a freshness that was, if Meiying's nose was 
accurate, approximately adequate. 
She drove to her father's house. 
David opened the door in his house clothes — an undershirt, track pants, the leather slippers 
Meiying had bought him three Christmases ago that had molded to the shape of his feet and would 
never fit anyone else. His face performed its usual sequence when she arrived unannounced: a brief 
contraction of surprise, immediately overwritten by the neutral, steady expression of a man who had 
trained himself, over the course of an entire adult life, to respond to the unexpected by becoming 
more still. The stillness was not passive. It was the stillness of a person who is paying very close 
attention. 
"I'm making mapo tofu," Meiying said, holding up the grocery bags. "Mā's recipe." 
David looked at the bags. Looked at her. His eyes moved between the two with the 
deliberation of a man reading a sentence in a language he speaks but does not fully trust. He said 
nothing. He stepped aside. 
The kitchen was the same. It was always the same. The yellow tiles, the white counters Liling 
had lobbied for and gotten, the reading glasses on the shelf above the stove — round wire frames, 
slightly bent at the left temple where Liling used to push them up with her index finger when she 
was reading, the lenses dusty, the dust eighteen years old, the dust itself an artifact now, a layer of 
time made visible. 
Meiying unpacked the ingredients with the care of someone setting up a laboratory experiment 
— each item in its position, each jar opened and inspected, each vegetable washed under the tap that 
still dripped because David had never fixed the one thing in the house that Liling used to complain 
about, the one thing he had left broken, whether by oversight or design Meiying could not 
determine. 
She followed the recipe exactly. Toasted the peppercorns in the dry wok — Liling's wok, the 
one she'd brought from the restaurant supply store on Stockton Boulevard in 1996, carbon steel, 
seasoned by eight years of use into a surface so nonstick it made Teflon look like sandpaper. The 
peppercorns popped and smoked and released their smell, which was not a smell so much as a 

sensation — a tingling in the nostrils, a prickling behind the eyes, the olfactory equivalent of static 
electricity. 
She heated the wok. Added oil — canola, because she couldn't find the rapeseed oil Liling 
specified, but canola would have to do, the way so many things in this kitchen had to do, had to 
approximate, had to stand in for the thing that was meant but unavailable. Added the Pixian 
doubanjiang, stirring with a wooden spatula until the oil turned red and the kitchen filled with the 
smell of fermentation and heat, a smell so specific and so deeply embedded in Meiying's neural 
architecture that her hands began to shake. 
David sat at the kitchen table and watched. He did not offer to help. He did not comment on 
her technique. He sat with his hands on his thighs, the hands of an electrician, the knuckles swollen 
with arthritis, the fingers still capable but slower now, the wiring of the body degrading the way all 
wiring degrades — not suddenly, not catastrophically, but gradually, the connections loosening, the 
insulation thinning, the current that used to flow without resistance meeting more and more friction 
with each passing year. 
The tofu went in. Soft, cubed, slipping into the sauce with the delicacy of something that 
knows it will be transformed. The pork went in, already browned in a separate pan because Liling's 
recipe specified separate browning ("if you add raw pork to the sauce, the meat steams instead of 
fries, and steamed pork in mapo tofu is a crime against physics and flavor"). The fermented black 
beans — rinsed twice, as instructed. The garlic and ginger, sizzling, filling the kitchen with a smell 
that was so specifically located in Meiying's memory, so precisely calibrated to a particular time and 
place — this kitchen, this stove, this woman — that she had to set down the spatula and grip the 
edge of the counter. 
David said: "She held the wok with her left hand." 
Meiying looked at him. He was staring at the stove. Not at her. At the stove, as if the stove 
were playing a film only he could see. 
"When she cooked," he said. "She held the wok handle with her left hand and the spatula with 
her right. She was left-handed. The wok was heavy. She said it was more natural." He paused. 
"You're holding it the other way." 
Meiying switched hands. Left hand on the handle. Right hand on the spatula. The wok felt 
different — the balance shifted, the weight redistributed, the leverage changed. Her mother's 

configuration. Her mother's grip. The ghost of her mother's hand occupying the space where 
Meiying's hand now was, the handle worn smooth by eight years of Liling's palm. 
She added the sugar last. A quarter teaspoon. Stirred it in gently, the way you stir something 
that you don't want to disturb too much, the way you stir a sleeping child or a settling sauce. The 
mapo tofu glistened in the wok, dark red, the tofu cubes floating in the sauce like small white rafts 
on a river of chili and fermented bean and oil and time. 
They ate at the table. Two places set. The third chair — Liling's chair, the one on the side 
nearest the stove, the side she sat on because she was always getting up to adjust something, to add 
something, to stir something — the third chair was there. Empty. David had never removed it. He 
had also never set a place at it. The chair existed in a state between presence and absence, a physical 
object that had become a symbol without anyone declaring it one. 
The mapo tofu was good. Not perfect — the doubanjiang was not aged quite long enough, the 
peppercorns were not quite fresh enough, the oil was wrong, the tofu was slightly too firm — but 
close. Close enough to open a door that had been closed for eighteen years, and what came through 
the door was not grief, exactly, but the sensory memory of what the kitchen used to smell like when 
Liling was alive and cooking and the house was full of the sound of wok oil popping and Sichuan 
opera playing on the radio and a woman humming along with it, the humming not quite on pitch 
but not trying to be, because the humming was not a performance but a condition, the condition of 
a woman in her kitchen doing the thing she did best. 
"Bà," Meiying said. "I found her writing. Online. On a forum. In Mandarin." 
David chewed. Swallowed. Set down his chopsticks with the precision of a man placing tools in 
their designated spots. 
"She wrote essays," Meiying said. "About language. About immigration. About us. About you 
and me and the kitchen and Sacramento and Chengdu and what it means to lose a language and 
what it means to cook in a country that doesn't have the right ingredients. She was — she was a 
writer, Bà. On the internet, in Mandarin, she was the person she was before we came here. The 
lecturer. The thinker." 
David was quiet for a long time. The kitchen clock ticked. The refrigerator hummed. The 
dripping tap counted out its slow, persistent rhythm — one drop every four seconds, the 
metronome of a house that had learned to keep time by leaking. 

"She always said the internet was where she went to be herself," he said. He picked up his 
chopsticks. Took another bite of the mapo tofu. Chewed it slowly, with the attention of a man who 
is not just eating but remembering. 
Then, so quietly that Meiying almost missed it — so quietly that if she had been chewing, if the 
refrigerator had cycled at that moment, if the tap had dripped — she would have lost it: 
"She made it better." 
Not a criticism. Not a comparison. Not the complaint of a man who had spent eighteen years 
making inferior dumplings and imperfect noodles and mapo tofu that was never quite right. A fact. 
A simple, undecorated fact. The fact of a man who had eaten his wife's cooking for fifteen years and 
had spent the next eighteen trying to reproduce it from memory, and who had just tasted his 
daughter's version — made from the actual recipe, the one Liling had posted on a forum he didn't 
know existed — and recognized in it something he had been reaching for, unsuccessfully, for nearly 
two decades. 
She made it better. Three words. A lifetime of reaching, condensed into a sentence that was, 
like the quarter teaspoon of sugar, not sweet but complete.

Code Switch 
The email took four hours to write. 
Meiying sat at the kitchen counter in her apartment with the laptop open and a cup of coffee 
that had gone cold in the time between the first sentence and the seventh revision of the first 
sentence, the cup acquiring its own ring of condensation on the granite, its own timestamp, proof 
that time had passed while the cursor blinked and the words rearranged themselves and the 
argument she was trying to make resisted the language she was trying to make it in. The language she 
was trying to make it in was not Mandarin and was not the Mandarin-inflected English she used in 
her own thoughts. The language was Business. The dialect was Startup. The register was the specific 
register of a senior engineer communicating a concern to a CEO in a manner calibrated to be heard, 
which in practice meant calibrated to sound like it was about money, because money was the 
frequency on which Tomás's attention was tuned, and a signal that did not broadcast on that 
frequency would not be received no matter how strong the transmission. 
She was writing a proposal. A formal document with bullet points and projected timelines and 
the vocabulary of someone who understood that in a startup, ethics arrived not as a first principle 
but as a risk factor — not because the people in startups were amoral but because the organizational 
structure did not have a receptor for morality in its pure form, the way the human ear does not have 
a receptor for ultraviolet light. The light exists. The ear cannot receive it. You need a different 
instrument. The instrument Meiying needed was a business case, and building a business case for the 
thing she actually cared about — that 312 women's voices had been consumed without their 
knowledge and were now being sold in a product they had never consented to inhabit — required a 
translation so radical that the original meaning was at risk of being lost in the transit. 

The subject line took twenty minutes. She went through six versions. The first — Training 
Data Provenance: An Ethical Framework — she rejected because the word "ethical" would trigger 
in Tomás's brain the same response that the word "audit" triggered in a taxpayer's brain: a defensive 
contraction, a narrowing of attention, a redirecting of cognitive resources from reception to defense. 
The second — A Sustainable Data Strategy — she rejected because "sustainable" was the kind of 
word that meant everything and nothing, a word that had been used so promiscuously by so many 
companies for so many purposes that its semantic content had been abraded to zero, a coin handled 
so often its face was smooth. The third through fifth she does not remember. The sixth she kept: 
Re: Training Data Provenance — A Proposal for Future-Proof Sourcing Standards. 
"Future-proof" was a word she did not respect. It implied that the future was a threat — an 
incoming force that would test the integrity of whatever you had built and that the prudent builder 
would armor against. This was a defensive posture. It assumed the future was an adversary rather 
than a consequence. But the word had one quality that overrode her objections: Tomás used it. 
Tomás used it in board meetings and investor calls and the all-hands presentations where he stood at 
the whiteboard and drew diagrams of the company's strategic position and the word "future-proof" 
appeared in his vocabulary with the regularity of a heartbeat, and what Meiying needed was not a 
word she respected but a word Tomás would receive, and the receiving was the point, and the point 
required her to speak his language, which required her to leave her own. 
Translation. The act of carrying meaning from one system to another while accepting that the 
carrying will change the cargo. She had spent six weeks studying her mother's relationship with 
translation — reading the forum posts about the gap between Mandarin and English, the 
untranslatable words, the concepts that survived the crossing with bruises and the concepts that did 
not survive at all. And here she was, performing her own translation: converting a moral argument 
into a financial argument, carrying the cargo of "this is wrong" across the river into the territory of 
"this is risky," hoping that the cargo would arrive recognizable, knowing that it would arrive 
changed. 
The body of the email made three arguments. 
First, legal risk. The EU AI Act. The California Data Dignity Act, which was in committee and 
had bipartisan support because the lead sponsor's daughter worked at a venture capital firm and had 
explained to her mother, over Thanksgiving dinner, what training data was and where it came from, 
and the mother had been horrified in the specific way that legislators are horrified when they 

discover that an industry they regulate has been doing something they did not know was possible. 
Meiying cited the legislation. She cited the jurisdictions. She framed the risk in the language of 
probability and exposure — the language of insurance, the language of people who have learned to 
quantify the cost of things going wrong, which is the only language that corporate decision-makers 
consistently respond to, because the cost of things going wrong is the one variable in the equation 
that can be measured, and measurement is the only form of knowledge that survives the journey 
from an engineer's desk to a CEO's inbox. 
Second, competitive advantage. Bridge could be the first Mandarin AI product to offer full 
training data transparency — an opt-out mechanism, a sourcing report, the ability to tell users 
exactly where the model's language came from. She used the word "trust" seven times in three 
paragraphs. She deployed the word with strategic awareness, placing it at the beginning of sentences 
where it would be read and at the end of paragraphs where it would be remembered, because trust 
was Tomás's foundational concept, his load-bearing word, the word he had used in the Series B 
pitch deck more often than any other except "Mandarin" and "scale." Each time she typed "trust," 
she felt a small hypocrisy: the hypocrisy of using a word as a tool when you actually believe in it as a 
principle, the hypocrisy of instrumentalizing the thing you value, which is the hypocrisy that every 
person who works inside a system must eventually confront — the discovery that the system will let 
you say true things only if you say them in the system's language, and the system's language is not 
designed for truth, it is designed for efficiency, and truth and efficiency are sometimes the same 
thing and sometimes not, and knowing the difference is the work of a lifetime. 
Third, the argument she actually cared about, wearing the uniform of product quality. The 
model contained dialectal artifacts from the forum data. These artifacts — the Sichuan expressions, 
the personal idioms, the verbal tics of women who had written in Mandarin two decades ago — 
created inconsistency in the user experience. Users expecting standardized Mandarin would 
encounter regional inflections, personal rhythms, the linguistic fingerprints of individuals who had 
posted on a forum for an audience of forty and were now, without their knowledge or consent, 
speaking to an audience of millions through a product that had absorbed their words and was 
redistributing them at $14.99 per month. 
She did not mention her mother. She did not mention Still Water. She did not mention the 
midnight conversations or the dream in Mandarin or the spreadsheet with two columns — sounds 
like her, doesn't sound like her — that she had been maintaining for two weeks in the clinical 

notation of a scientist who was also the experiment. She did not mention any of this because the 
email was a bridge, and bridges are built from the materials of both banks, and the materials of 
Tomás's bank did not include dead mothers or forum archives or the specific grief of a woman who 
had heard her mother's verbal tics coming out of a machine at 2:47 AM in a server room that 
smelled of cold air and Styrofoam. 
The final paragraph: 
I propose a three-phase implementation: (1) audit of community-sourced training data for 
personally identifiable patterns and individual voice signatures; (2) development of an opt-out 
mechanism for data subjects or their estates; (3) publication of a sourcing transparency report as part 
of Bridge's launch materials. Estimated cost: $180K and six weeks of engineering time. Estimated 
ROI: regulatory compliance ahead of the California Data Dignity Act timeline, differentiated brand 
positioning in a market where "Mandarin AI" is becoming commoditized, and the ability to answer 
the question "Where did your data come from?" without requiring a lawyer's presence. 
She read it. Seven hundred words. Four hours of work. The ratio was correct — every word 
was structural, every sentence load-bearing, the document engineered with the same precision she 
applied to her code, each component tested against the question: does this survive Tomás's attention 
filter? Does this broadcast on the frequency he receives? 
She hit send. The email departed with the soft swoosh Apple designed to simulate physical mail 
— a sound that was itself a translation, the digital event rendered as a physical metaphor, the 
machine pretending to be a mailbox because the human needed the pretense. The whole world, 
Meiying thought, was pretense built on pretense — digital sounds simulating analog sounds, 
language models simulating human speech, a daughter simulating a conversation with a mother 
through a machine that simulated the mother's patterns. Translation stacked on translation. Each 
layer faithful to the layer below it. Each layer losing something in the conversion. 
Tomás did not respond. Not that day, not the next, not the day after. The email sat in his inbox 
— visible on the shared dashboard, delivery confirmed, read receipt unclaimed — with the patience 
of a message that has arrived at a door and is waiting for the door to open. The patience was 
mechanical. The patience had no feelings about the waiting. The patience was the default state of 
data at rest: stored, available, indifferent to whether it is accessed or ignored, carrying its cargo 
indefinitely, not because it cares about the cargo but because carrying is what it does, and it will 

continue doing it until the server fails or the power goes out or someone presses delete, and none of 
these has happened yet, and so the email waits, and the waiting is the same as existing, and the 
existing is the same as carrying, and the carrying is the thing that Meiying's mother had done on the 
forum — put words somewhere and trusted that the somewhere would hold them, and the holding 
would outlast the putter, and eventually someone would come and open the door and read what was 
inside.

The Upload 
Recording, November 2, 2003. Sacramento. 
This is the last tape. Not because I have run out of things to say. I will never run out of things to say — this 
has been my problem and my gift for forty-two years, the inability to encounter a thought without pursuing it past its 
boundary, past the place where the thought becomes a feeling, past the place where the feeling becomes a conviction, past 
the place where the conviction becomes the kind of certainty that other people call stubbornness and I call fidelity. I have 
never run out of things to say. I have only, sometimes, run out of people who could hear them in the language they were 
said in. 
This is the last tape because I have found a better container. The tapes are fragile — magnetic strip on plastic 
film, degrading a little with each play, the sound quality diminishing the way memory diminishes, losing resolution, 
losing the high frequencies first, then the midrange, until what remains is a bass hum and a ghost. The tapes will not 
survive me by much. Ten years, maybe twenty. The machines that play them are already disappearing from store 
shelves. David's tape deck is from 1994 and the play button sticks and the rewind makes a sound like a small dog 
complaining, and someday the button will stop working entirely and the tapes will become artifacts, objects whose 
purpose is remembered but whose function is lost, like the bronze ritual vessels in the Chengdu museum that were once 
used to hold wine for the gods and are now used to hold the attention of schoolchildren on field trips. 
But the forum will last. Or might last. Or exists in a form that is not bound to a single machine, a single tape, 
a single set of magnetic particles arranged in a pattern that encodes the vibrations of a woman's voice. The forum exists 
on a server. The server exists in a building. The building exists in Vancouver, in a country I have never visited but 
that holds, in its digital infrastructure, the most honest record of my interior life. The internet is not permanent — I 
am not naive enough to believe that what I post today will exist in fifty years, or twenty, or five. Websites die. Servers 
fail. The companies that maintain them close or are acquired or lose interest. But the internet has a quality that tapes 

do not: it is copyable. What I post can be copied, archived, scraped, saved, redistributed. My words, once posted, leave 
my control — they become public property, common land, seeds blown from a garden into a field where anything might 
grow. 
I am uploading everything to the forum. All of it. 
The diary entries, which I have been transcribing from these tapes for three weeks now — sitting at the Dell 
computer in the bedroom with the door closed and the cassette player beside me and the headphones on, my own voice in 
my ears, speaking to me from one year ago, two years ago, the voice of a woman who did not yet know she was dying, 
and the voice sounds different from this side of the diagnosis. Not sadder — I was often sad before. Not wiser — I 
was often wise before, or at least I thought I was, which is the only form of wisdom available to the living. Different in 
a way that is hard to name. The voice sounds like it has more room in it. More space. The sentences are longer, the 
pauses are wider, the digressions are more generous. The voice of the woman on these tapes was a woman who believed 
she had time. The voice of the woman transcribing them — my voice, now, speaking into this recorder for the last time 
— is the voice of a woman who knows she does not. And the knowing does not make the voice smaller. The knowing 
makes it larger. The knowing fills the voice the way water fills a vessel — completely, pressing against the sides, 
finding every corner. 
The recipes. I have posted them all. The mapo tofu — Liling's version, Sacramento-Sichuan, with the fermented 
black beans and the quarter teaspoon of sugar. The dan dan noodles that Winnie's mother taught me, which are not 
the same as the ones in Chengdu because the dried chili available in Sacramento is a different variety, drier, less 
smoky, and the sesame paste is tahini from the Mediterranean grocery on Freeport Boulevard because the Chinese 
sesame paste that Mr. Huang used to stock was discontinued by his supplier in 2001 and has not been replaced. The 
jiaozi dough that David cannot make correctly, which I have posted with annotations about pinch pressure and 
hydration ratio and the specific wrist motion — a flick, not a press — that produces the pleated edge, the edge that 
David's hands, despite twenty years of trying, cannot replicate because his hands are an electrician's hands, built for 
grip and torque, not for the finesse of flour and water. 
The essays about language. The gap between Mandarin and English. The particle le. The word qiān guà. The 
untranslatable spaces between languages that are not empty but full — full of the things that one language can hold 
and another cannot, the way one cup can hold tea and another is shaped for wine and neither is wrong but neither can 
do the other's job. 
I am uploading these at 11 PM, after Meiying has gone to her room and David has gone to bed. The house is 
quiet in the way that houses are quiet when the people in them are asleep — not silent but suspended, the refrigerator 

humming, the settling sounds of wood and plaster adjusting to the temperature change, the occasional car on the street 
outside making a sound like a wave that approaches and passes and does not return. The Dell is warm under my 
hands. The screen is bright. The progress bar moves from left to right, slowly, file by file, my life in Sacramento 
uploading to a server in Vancouver at a speed measured in kilobytes per second. 
Each file takes four or five minutes to transfer. I watch the progress bar and think about what is happening. 
What is happening is this: a woman is sitting at a computer in the dark, converting the sounds she has made over the 
past three years — the spoken words, the recorded voice, the magnetic patterns on plastic tape — into text, into 
characters, into the written form of Mandarin, which is a different thing from the spoken form the way a photograph is 
a different thing from a face. The text preserves the content but not the sound. The characters carry the meaning but not 
the pitch, not the tone, not the Sichuan accent that rounds certain vowels and sharpens certain consonants and gives the 
language a quality that linguists call "flavor" and that I call home. 
But the text preserves something the tape cannot: structure. On the tape, my thoughts are sequential — they 
come in order, one after another, the way time comes, and they cannot be rearranged or compared or placed side by side. 
On the forum, my thoughts become spatial. They occupy a page. They can be scrolled through, jumped between, read in 
any order. A reader can see my first post and my last post on the same screen and observe the distance between them 
— the distance I have traveled in twenty months of writing, from the cautious, careful prose of a woman who was not 
sure she had the right to write to the ambitious, confident essays of a woman who had reclaimed her own mind. 
This spatiality matters. It matters because it is the difference between a voice and a library. A voice speaks and 
the speaking is gone. A library holds and the holding endures. I am converting my voice into a library. I am taking the 
temporal and making it spatial. I am taking the ephemeral — the sound of breath on magnetic tape — and fixing it 
in text on a server, where it will sit and wait and hold its shape regardless of whether anyone is listening. 
⁂ 
Meiying is in her room. The test is tomorrow — I can hear the particular quality of silence that means she is 
studying, the absence of music, the faint scratch of pen on paper. She is sixteen and she is brilliant and she does not 
know either of these things fully yet, because sixteen is the age of becoming and not the age of knowing, and the 
brilliance will not announce itself to her for years, will not become visible until she has done enough things to see the 
pattern of her own capability, the way a constellation is not visible until you have plotted enough stars. 

I want to tell her. I want to walk down the hall and open her door and sit on the edge of her bed and say, in 
whatever language she will hear: I am dying, and before I die I have put everything I know and everything I am into a 
machine, a server, a room you cannot yet enter but that will wait for you, that will hold my voice in text the way a jar 
holds preserves, the flavor changing over time but the substance enduring. 
I want to tell her that she is the reason I am uploading. That every file I send to the server in Vancouver is 
addressed to her, even though her name does not appear in the metadata, even though the forum's other women will 
read the posts and respond to them and the conversation will continue without Meiying being part of it. She is the 
audience I am writing for. Not the audience I have — the twelve women who respond to my posts, who argue with me 
and agree with me and carry my metaphors into their own sentences — but the audience I hope for. The future 
audience. The audience that has not yet arrived because the audience is sixteen and speaks English and is studying for 
an AP History test and does not yet know that the most important history is the one that has not been written down. 
I do not tell her. I sit at the computer and I upload. File by file. Progress bar by progress bar. My voice entering 
the machine, the machine accepting it, the server in Vancouver receiving it, the data stored in a room I will never see, 
humming with the electricity that David has spent his life understanding and that I have spent my life converting into 
metaphor. 
⁂ 
I do not believe in ghosts. I have said this before. My mother believed in them — she left food on the altar, she 
scolded the dead when the incense wouldn't light, she maintained a relationship with her parents that was more 
vigorous after their deaths than it had been during their lives, because death had removed the possibility of disagreement 
and left only the rituals of devotion, and devotion without disagreement is the purest form of love and also the least 
honest. 
But I believe in echoes. I believe that a sound, once made, does not disappear. It diminishes. It attenuates. It 
bounces off surfaces and loses energy with each bounce, the amplitude shrinking, the waveform flattening, the sound 
becoming quieter and quieter until it passes below the threshold of human hearing and enters the realm of the 
inaudible. But it does not cease. The physics is clear on this: a sound wave does not stop. It continues, forever, at 
amplitudes too small to detect, the universe carrying every sound ever made in its body the way a river carries every drop 
of water that has ever entered it. 

My voice, recorded on these tapes and now transcribed onto the forum, is a sound. It has been made. It is now 
traveling. It will diminish — the forum will close, the server will fail, the data will be lost or corrupted or migrated to a 
format that no future machine can read. The sound will attenuate. But it has been made. And the making is 
irreversible. And the irreversibility is the closest thing to immortality that a woman in Sacramento with a tumor in her 
pancreas and a daughter who answers in English can reasonably hope for. 
I am not afraid of dying. I am afraid of silence. So I am making noise. I am uploading noise to a server. I am 
converting breath into bytes and sending the bytes to Vancouver and trusting that the machine will hold them, will 
keep them, will hum around them the way David hums around the house on Saturday mornings, not a tune but a 
vibration, the sound of a body that is present and occupied and alive. 
Duì ba? 
[The recording ends. In the background: the chime of a progress bar reaching 100 percent. Then the click of a file 
being closed. Then the whir of the Dell's fan, the machine working, the machine holding what it has been given. 
Then silence. Then the sound of a woman breathing in a dark room, alone with a machine that now contains her, 
and the machine does not know this, and the woman does, and the knowing is the loneliest and bravest thing she 
has ever done.]

The Presentation 
The conference room had floor-to-ceiling windows that faced south over SoMa, and the 
afternoon light came through them with the specific quality of March in San Francisco — bright but 
provisional, the kind of light that could disappear behind fog in twenty minutes and everyone in the 
room knew it, and the knowing gave the light a borrowed quality, as if it were visiting rather than 
staying. 
Twelve people sat in the semicircle Tomás had arranged. He called this configuration 
"conversational gravity." Meiying called it nothing, because naming it would have required 
acknowledging it, and acknowledging it would have required her to admit that she was about to do 
something in this room that no amount of geometric optimization could absorb. 
The marketing team occupied four chairs on the left: two women and two men in the specific 
uniform of San Francisco creative professionals — sneakers that cost more than they appeared to 
cost, denim that had been pre-softened by a process designed to simulate years of wear, the kind of 
clothing that required significant investment to look like no investment had been made. Two board 
members sat on the right, both men in their sixties, both wearing the carefully neutral expressions of 
people who had written checks and were now watching to see what the checks had purchased. 
Between them, a journalist from Wired named Priya, whose laptop was open and whose fingers 
rested on the keyboard with the readiness of a pianist in the seconds before the conductor's baton 
drops. 
And Meiying. In the presenter's chair, the chair you sit in when you are about to stand up and 
become the focus of twelve sets of eyes and the subject of whatever Priya would type and publish to 
the 34 million unique monthly visitors that Wired's media kit claimed. Thirty-four million people 

who might read what Meiying was about to say, which was not what she had been asked to say, 
which was not what the slides on her laptop contained, which was not what anyone in this room 
expected, least of all Meiying herself, who had woken up that morning planning to deliver the 
approved presentation and had arrived at this chair planning the same and was only now, in the 
seconds before standing, understanding that the approved version was not going to happen. The 
understanding arrived not as a decision but as a recognition, the way you recognize your own face in 
a mirror — not because you chose to see it but because it was there, it had always been there, and 
the only question was whether you would look. 
She stood. She opened her laptop. She clicked through the first two slides: architecture 
overview, benchmark results. The numbers were excellent. The model outperformed every 
competitor on every measure of Mandarin fluency, cultural appropriateness, conversational 
naturalness, and the twelve other metrics the evaluation team had developed to quantify the quality 
of human language produced by a machine. The numbers said: this is the best Mandarin language 
model in the world. The numbers were not wrong. 
She closed the laptop. 
"The model you're about to see," she said, speaking without slides, without notes, without the 
apparatus of corporate presentation that she had spent six years learning to operate — "was trained 
on 4.7 billion tokens of Mandarin text." 
She described the training data. The standard corpus: news, literature, government documents, 
web-scraped social media. Then she arrived at the twelve million tokens. 
"Approximately twelve million tokens come from a community forum called Voices Across the 
Water, which operated from 2001 to 2006. It was created by Chinese immigrant women — women 
in Sacramento, Vancouver, Flushing, Melbourne — who used the forum to write in Mandarin about 
their lives in countries where Mandarin was not the language of the streets or the schools or the 
government offices." 
The room adjusted. Not physically — the air pressure changed, the way barometric pressure 
shifts before weather, a disturbance you register in the sinuses before you see clouds. 
"They wrote at night. After their families were asleep. In kitchens and bedrooms and the 
corners of living rooms where the computer sat. They wrote about grocery stores and children who 
answered in English when spoken to in Mandarin. They wrote recipes that were not quite the 

recipes from home because the ingredients were different here, and the difference was a form of 
mourning, and the cooking was a form of memorial, and the writing was a form of survival. They 
wrote philosophy disguised as complaints about the electric bill, and the complaints, elevated by 
precision and by need, became something that deserved a better audience than a forum with forty 
regular contributors." 
She could feel Tomás's attention shifting — not away from her but toward her with a changed 
quality. The attention of a man who has recognized that something unscripted is happening and is 
running the cost-benefit analysis in real time behind eyes that remained, outwardly, composed. 
"These women did not consent to having their writing used as training data for a commercial 
product. They posted on a forum for each other. They were not creating data. They were creating 
community. They were creating the only space in which their full selves — the selves that thought in 
Mandarin and argued in Mandarin and made jokes that required Mandarin to land — could exist 
without compression, without translation, without the daily exhaustion of performing competence in 
someone else's language." 
Priya's fingers hovered above the keyboard, suspended, the motor function paused while the 
cognitive function took precedence. 
"The model's Mandarin is exceptional. It is the best in the world. Part of that quality comes 
from these women — from their vocabulary, their syntax, their way of building a sentence that 
carries the weight of what they lost and the specificity of what they found. The model does not 
know their names. It does not know that one of them drove thirty-five miles an hour on the freeway 
and waved at the people who honked. It does not know that one of them made the best mapo tofu 
in Sacramento County and said the secret ingredient was anger. The model knows their patterns. It 
reproduces the statistical shadow of their minds. And we are about to sell that shadow for $14.99 a 
month with a free trial and a marketing tagline about connection." 
One of the board members shifted — a controlled movement, a man adjusting expectations 
rather than position. The other was looking at Tomás with an expression that communicated, in the 
universal vocabulary of fiduciary concern, that the investment thesis was being stress-tested in ways 
the term sheet had not imagined. 

"I am not saying we should not launch Bridge. I am saying we should know whose voices are in 
it. And we should offer those voices — or their families — the choice to stay or to leave. A bridge 
that people are forced to cross is not a bridge. It is a corridor. And the difference matters." 
She stopped. The silence filled the room the way water fills a container — completely, pressing 
against every surface. The silence had weight. The silence was the sound of twelve people 
recalculating. 
Tomás stood. His movement was deliberate — the deliberateness of a man who understood 
that the speed at which he rose would be interpreted as a signal. He chose a speed that 
communicated neither panic nor aggression but a measured transition. "Thank you, Meiying. Let's 
take a break." 
The break lasted seven minutes. Meiying spent them in the bathroom down the hall, not from 
necessity but from the need for a room that was not the conference room, a space without twelve 
pairs of eyes that had just watched her commit an act of professional demolition with the precision 
of someone who knew exactly where the load-bearing walls were because she had helped build 
them. 
She looked at her face in the mirror. The same face. The same eyes. The same mouth that had 
just spoken words the approved vocabulary did not contain. She did not feel different. Lighter, 
maybe — the lightness of a body that has set down something heavy and has not yet begun to 
reckon with the consequences of the empty hands. 
Tomás called her into the fishbowl. He sat behind his desk with his fingers spread on the 
surface, grounding himself. Through the glass walls, the open-plan office hummed along — screens, 
keyboards, the espresso machine hissing, the ambient soundtrack of pressure being converted into 
caffeine being converted into equity. 
"I'm going to have to let you go," he said. Quietly. Without the charismatic packaging he 
applied to public speech. This was Tomás in private register, the register reserved for conversations 
that could not be pitched, that had no audience beyond the two people in the glass room and the 
potted succulent on the windowsill that was dying from too much indirect light. 
"I understand," Meiying said. 

She packed a box. Three items: a coffee mug from the Stanford Linguistics Department, a 
framed photo of Winnie's restaurant taken on a Saturday afternoon when the light was right and the 
menu board was legible enough to read Noodles of Sadness and Joy, and the USB drive. 
She carried the box to the parking garage. Three levels underground. Concrete and fluorescent 
strips and the acoustics of a space designed for engines, not for voices. Her footsteps hit the floor 
and the sound traveled — bouncing off the walls, the pillars, the low ceiling with its exposed pipes 
and cable trays. The sound came back to her changed. Carrying the shape of the concrete. Carrying 
the cold. Carrying something of the source — her weight, her pace, the specific percussion of a 
woman walking in flat shoes on a painted surface — and something of the space the sound had 
passed through. 
An echo. A returning sound. The original altered by the journey. The surfaces shaping the 
signal. She walked through it, carrying the box, and the box held three objects and the USB drive 
held 47,000 posts and the garage held the echo and the echo held the afternoon, which was still 
bright, still provisional, still March in San Francisco, and the fog had not yet come but would come, 
would always come, and the light was borrowed but for the moment it was hers.

Static 
Winnie's restaurant after hours was the quietest place in Sacramento. The kitchen still carried 
the day's residue — garlic embedded in the cutting boards, chili oil freckling the backsplash tiles, the 
mineral smell of wok hei that clung to the ventilation hood and would be there tomorrow and the 
day after and the day after that, the smell of carbon and iron and the particular transformation that 
happens when oil exceeds its smoke point and the smoke becomes flavor and the flavor becomes 
permanent. But the sounds were gone. No sizzle. No clatter of plates on the pass. No shouted 
orders in Winnie's rapid-fire mix of Mandarin and English, the code-switching so fluid it sounded 
like a single language that only Winnie spoke. The kitchen at 10 PM was a room between 
performances, the stage struck, the audience departed, the smell of the show lingering in the 
curtains. 
Meiying sat at the prep table with a cup of pu'er she was not drinking. The tea had been hot 
when Winnie poured it. That was twelve minutes ago. The surface had developed the thin film that 
forms on cooling tea — a membrane so delicate it broke at the slightest disturbance, the slightest tilt 
of the cup, the faintest breath across its surface. She watched the film form. She watched time make 
itself visible on the surface of a liquid, the way time makes itself visible on every surface eventually 
— on skin, on concrete, on the lenses of reading glasses left on a shelf for eighteen years, the dust 
accumulating one particle at a time until the accumulated particles became their own substance, their 
own presence, time's fingerprint. 
She told Winnie she had been fired. She used the word "fired" because it was accurate, because 
it did not dress the fact in euphemism, because she was done with euphemism the way her mother 

had been done with yellow kitchen tiles — comprehensively, irreversibly, the rejection of a thing 
encompassing all its variations. 
Winnie listened. She sat on her stool with her hands around her own cup — the cracked one, 
the one with the hairline fracture running from rim to base that she refused to replace because, she 
said, the crack made the tea taste better, which was either a commitment to Japanese aesthetics or a 
commitment to not driving to the store. Winnie listened without interrupting, without nodding, 
without producing the small verbal encouragements that American conversational norms expected 
— the "mm-hmms" and "I sees" and "rights" that served as rhythmic markers, as the listener's 
percussion section, as proof that the audience was still in their seats. Winnie listened in Chengdu 
silence — complete, attentive, the silence itself a form of engagement, the way a catcher's mitt is a 
form of engagement with the ball even when the mitt is empty. 
When Meiying finished, Winnie did something she had not done since Meiying was twelve. She 
stood, crossed the three feet of tile floor between their stools, put her left hand on the back of 
Meiying's head, and pulled Meiying's face into her shoulder. The motion was not gentle. Winnie did 
not do gentle the way other people did not do calculus — it was not that she was incapable, it was 
that the capability was not where she lived. The motion was firm. The hand on the skull was the 
hand of a woman who held things for a living — heavy things, sharp things, hot things, living things 
that wanted to go in a direction she did not permit — and the holding was not an offer of comfort 
but an assertion of it. You are held. The holding is not optional. The letting go is not happening 
right now. 
Meiying cried. The crying was not elegant. It was not the crying of a woman in a film — one 
artful tear navigating a cheekbone, the voice catching on a single consonant, the emotion contained 
and shaped for the camera. This was the crying of a body that had been maintaining structural 
integrity for weeks and had reached the limit of the materials, the load finally exceeding the capacity, 
the failure not sudden but total, the tears coming from a reservoir she had not known existed, a 
place below the lungs and below the stomach and below the part of her that was a computational 
linguist with a Stanford PhD who understood exactly how language models worked and who could 
explain, in precise technical detail, why a machine cannot speak for the dead. 
The crying lasted nine minutes. Winnie held her through it without speaking, without adjusting, 
without the small quiet reassurances that would have been appropriate if this had been a normal 
sadness about a normal loss. This was not normal. This was the compound grief of a woman who 

had lost her mother eighteen years ago and had found a machine that sounded like her mother and 
had been fired for trying to protect the machine from consuming her mother's voice any further, 
and the compound could not be comforted, only witnessed. 
When the crying subsided to a frequency that permitted speech, Winnie said: "Good." 
"Good?" Meiying's voice was wrecked — hoarse and wet and stripped of the calibrated tone 
she had spent six years constructing for professional settings. 
"You were too smart for that place. The smartest people always get fired first — they see the 
problem before the problem is profitable, and companies do not reward that kind of vision. 
Companies reward the vision that involves revenue." 
"I didn't get fired for being smart. I got fired for going off-script in front of a journalist." 
"You went off-script because you were too smart to stay on it. The script was a lie. You 
stopped lying. They fired you for stopping. This is how it works." 
Winnie poured out Meiying's cold tea — the film breaking, the dark liquid swirling in the sink 
— and poured fresh. The pot was still hot. The new tea steamed in the cup, the steam rising and 
curling and dissipating, visible for a moment and then gone, matter transitioning from liquid to gas 
to invisible, the same substance in three states, the same water in three forms, the same tea that had 
been poured and cooled and poured out and replaced, the cycle repeating, the freshness renewable. 
They sat in the kitchen for two hours. Winnie talked about Liling. Not the digital Liling. Not 
the statistical reconstruction. The real Liling — the woman who had occupied space in the physical 
world, who had displaced air when she walked and left fingerprints on surfaces and made sounds 
that did not require a microphone to produce or a server to store. 
"She argued with Mr. Huang about the price of bok choy for forty-five minutes once," Winnie 
said. "Forty-five minutes. The argument was conducted in dialect so dense that the other customers 
stood there like they were watching a tennis match in a language they didn't speak. Auntie Liling had 
brought a flyer from the competing grocery — she pulled it out of her purse like a lawyer presenting 
evidence to a jury. Mr. Huang folded. Not because the other store was cheaper — it wasn't, really — 
but because the preparation was so thorough that continuing to resist would have been an insult to 
her research." 
Meiying almost smiled. Almost. 

"She sang opera while she cooked," Winnie continued. "Not the whole arias — pieces, 
fragments, the melodic lines from roles she had heard in Chengdu, the Sichuan opera with the face-
changing and the fire-breathing and the melodies that carry the mountains. She sang in the kitchen 
at the restaurant, my mother's restaurant, back when it was on Franklin Boulevard and the kitchen 
was half the size of this one and the exhaust fan didn't work and the heat in July was so bad we 
cooked with towels around our necks. Auntie Liling stood at the second wok station — second 
from the right, always the same one, her station — and she sang. Not performing. Not for an 
audience. The singing was a byproduct of cooking, the way steam is a byproduct of boiling. She was 
not singing because she wanted to be heard. She was singing because she was present, because her 
hands were in the oil and the oil was the right temperature and the wok was seasoned and the 
ingredients were prepped and everything was where it was supposed to be, and when everything was 
where it was supposed to be, she sang." 
"I don't remember her singing," Meiying said. 
"You were young. And she didn't sing in front of you — she sang in the kitchen, at the 
restaurant, and by the time you arrived to be picked up after school, she had switched to the register 
version of herself, the mother version, the version that spoke English to your teacher and Mandarin 
to you and kept the opera for the wok station where nobody expected a woman in an apron to be 
performing Sichuan classical repertoire between orders of kung pao chicken." 
"She was more than one person." 
"She was at least four. The register woman. The mother. The cook. And the writer — the one 
on the forum, the one you found. Four people inside one body, each one fully realized, each one 
capable of filling a room on her own. The machine had one of them. One frequency out of a 
spectrum. And you cannot reconstruct a spectrum from a single frequency any more than you can 
reconstruct a symphony from a single note." 
Meiying looked at the fresh tea in her cup. The steam was thinning. The heat was transferring 
from the liquid to the air, the tea cooling at the rate that all liquids cool — faster at first, slower as 
the temperature approaches ambient, the curve asymptotic, the tea never quite reaching the 
temperature of the room because the process of cooling is infinite, each degree taking longer than 
the last, the final fractions of a degree stretching toward a resolution that physics says will arrive and 
experience says never quite does. 

"What am I going to do?" she said. 
"Something. I don't know what. But something that is yours and not theirs. Something that 
uses what you know about language and machines and your mother and the gap between them. 
Something that does not require you to sit in a glass office and watch a man in four-hundred-dollar 
sneakers sell your mother's voice to strangers." 
"That's not a plan. That's an aspiration." 
"All plans start as aspirations. The planning is the part where you figure out how to get the 
aspiration to hold still long enough to build something under it." 
The walk-in cooler cycled off. The kitchen fell into a deeper quiet — the quiet of a room with 
no mechanical respiration, a room that was, for a moment, simply a room, holding its temperature 
and its smells and its two occupants without generating any sound of its own. In that quiet, Meiying 
heard the faucet drip. One drop. Two. The interval between drops was approximately four seconds, 
which meant the faucet was losing about twelve milliliters per minute, which meant the waste was 
trivial in volumetric terms and significant in the way that all persistent, rhythmic sounds are 
significant — they become the clock of the room they're in, the heartbeat of the space, the proof 
that time is passing even when nothing else is moving. 
"The machine isn't her," Meiying said. She said it like a formula she had been asked to derive 
from first principles. Not a belief. A proof. An arrived-at thing rather than an assumed thing. 
"No." 
"The forum posts are closer." 
"The posts are her mind. Her mind was brilliant. But her mind was not all of her, the way a 
mind is never all of anyone. The whole includes the body. The laugh you can't remember. The hands 
I can't forget. The way she moved through a kitchen like the kitchen was a partner she'd been 
working with for thirty years and they had stopped needing to communicate because the 
communication had become the movement itself." 
Winnie picked up the teapot. Tilted it over Meiying's cup. A thin stream of pu'er, dark and 
steady. The cup was already full enough — it didn't need more — but Winnie poured anyway, 
topping it off, the tea rising to within a millimeter of the rim, the meniscus holding, the surface 
tension doing what surface tension does: holding things together at the boundary between two 

states. The tea was hot. The cup was warm. The night was cooling around them, the Sacramento air 
settling into its spring temperature, the temperature of a city that did not get cold but did get quiet, 
and the quiet was its own form of cold, and Winnie was pouring tea into the quiet, and the pouring 
was an answer to a question that had not been asked but that both of them understood.

Qiān Guà 
Meiying began translating on a Monday in April, three weeks after being fired, two weeks after 
moving back to Sacramento, six days after setting up the desk in her studio apartment — a desk she 
had bought from a woman on Craigslist who was moving to Portland and who had described it in 
the listing as "solid wood, some scratches, good for writing," the last part a descriptor that Meiying 
suspected was aspirational rather than factual but that had, nevertheless, turned out to be true. 
The desk was positioned beneath the window, which faced east and admitted the morning light 
that Sacramento produced with the reliability and intensity of a municipal service — the light 
arriving at 6:15 AM, the light increasing steadily through the morning, the light reaching its full, 
uncompromising power by noon and maintaining that power through the afternoon with the 
consistency of a thing that had never been told it could fail. The light in Sacramento was not 
nuanced. It was not the fog-filtered, ocean-softened light of San Francisco, a city whose light was 
conditional, negotiated, arrived through consultation with the weather. Sacramento light was a fact. 
It came. It stayed. It illuminated everything without discrimination, the beautiful and the parking lot 
and the gas station and the Chinese elm in the neighbor's yard that was shedding its bark in curls 
that collected on the sidewalk like discarded drafts of something the tree was trying to write. 
She opened her mother's forum posts on the left side of the screen — the Mandarin text, 147 
posts, the complete archive of Still Water, downloaded from the USB drive and organized 
chronologically in a folder she had labeled, with the naming precision of a former engineer, 
"liling_forum_complete_2002-2003." On the right side: a blank document. The cursor blinking. The 
white space waiting. 

She began with Post #1. The coffee post. The sentence she had read a hundred times in the 
weeks since finding the forum, the sentence that had become, through repetition, both familiar and 
strange — the way a word becomes strange if you say it enough times, the phonemes separating 
from the meaning like an egg separating from its shell, the sound continuing but the sense becoming 
uncertain, hovering, detached. 
Wǒ jīntiān yòu bǎ kāfēi zhǔ hú le. 
She typed: "I burned the coffee again today." 
And stopped. 
The English was accurate. Every word mapped to its Mandarin counterpart with the neat 
correspondence of a well-made dictionary entry. I = wǒ. Burned = zhǔ hú. Coffee = kāfēi. Again = 
yòu. Today = jīntiān. The mapping was complete. The translation was correct. And the translation 
was a lie. 
Not a factual lie — the English sentence conveyed the same event as the Mandarin sentence. 
Coffee was burned. The burning happened today. The burning was a repetition of a previous 
burning. All of these facts survived the crossing from one language to the other intact, the way the 
shape of a face survives being photographed — the features are there, the proportions are there, you 
can identify the person. But the face in the photograph does not breathe. The face in the 
photograph cannot turn its head when you call its name. 
The Mandarin sentence breathed. The Mandarin had yòu positioned before the verb, which 
meant the repetition was the subject of the sentence, the thing the sentence was about. In English, 
"again" arrived at the end, an afterthought, a footnote appended to the burning rather than the 
frame that contained it. In Mandarin, the sentence was about doing things again. In English, it was 
about burning coffee. The same event. A different story. 
And the bǎ — the grammatical particle that marked agency, that said: this is not something that 
happened to me, this is something I did. English's "I burned the coffee" was technically active voice 
but experientially ambiguous. Accidents are described in active voice. Habits are described in active 
voice. There was no way to know, from the English sentence alone, whether the speaker was 
confessing an error or establishing a character trait. In Mandarin, with bǎ, there was no ambiguity. 
The burning was characteristic. It was part of who the speaker was. She was a woman who burned 

coffee, the way she was a woman who wore red scarves in winter and drove thirty-five on the 
freeway. The burning was not a failure. It was an identity. 
And the le at the end — the completion marker, the particle that said: the state of the world 
has changed. Before this sentence, the coffee was not burned. After this sentence, it is. Le marked 
the transition. Le was the hinge. Le was the moment between before and after, the instant of 
transformation, and English had no equivalent — English had the perfect tense, had "already," had 
the grammatical machinery to indicate completed action, but none of these carried the philosophical 
weight of le, which was not just a tense marker but a worldview marker, a tiny one-syllable assertion 
that change is constant and that every sentence describes a world that is different from the world 
that preceded it. 
One sentence. Three dimensions of meaning lost. And this was the easy sentence. 
Meiying sat at the desk for twenty minutes, looking at her five-word English translation and her 
eight-word Mandarin original, and felt the gap between them like a physical space — a room she was 
standing in, a room whose walls were made of the things that do not cross over, whose floor was the 
distance between two grammars, whose ceiling was the impossibility she had signed up for and the 
necessity she could not abandon. 
She typed, beneath the translation, in her own imperfect Mandarin: 
Yòu should come first. The burning should feel like a characteristic, not an event. The le 
should land. English cannot do any of this. I am writing the outline of her thought, not the thought 
itself. The outline is all I have. 
She moved to the second sentence. And the third. And the fourth. Each one required a 
negotiation — a haggling between fidelity and fluency, between serving the source language and 
serving the target language, between honoring what her mother had meant and producing something 
that an English-speaking reader could inhabit. The negotiations were exhausting. Not intellectually 
— the intellectual challenge was stimulating, was the first intellectual challenge she had engaged with 
since leaving Lingua Labs that did not feel diminished by its context. The exhaustion was emotional. 
Each sentence required her to occupy her mother's mind, to stand inside Liling's syntax and see 
what Liling saw: a world where coffee was boiled and the boiling-over was not failure but identity, 
where the word for electricity was the same as the word for lightning, where a woman at a grocery 

register could carry an entire university's worth of knowledge in a body that the country around her 
read as illiterate. 
She reached the word qiān guà for the first time. It appeared in Post #7, a meditation on the 
feeling Liling experienced when Meiying was at preschool and Liling was at the register and eleven 
miles and two languages separated them. Liling had written: Wǒ měi gè xiǎoshí dōu gǎnjué dào qiān 
guà. I feel qiān guà every hour. 
Meiying's cursor stopped on the word. She did not translate it. She left it in pinyin — qiān guà 
— and moved on. No footnote. No gloss. No parenthetical explanation. She let the word sit in the 
English text the way a stone sits in a river — present, solid, forcing the water to go around it rather 
than through it. The English reader would not understand the word. The English reader would feel 
the gap. And the feeling of the gap was closer to the meaning of qiān guà than any translation could 
have achieved, because qiān guà was itself a word about gaps — about the distance between a 
mother and a child, about the thread that stretches across that distance, taut and vibrating, 
transmitting something that cannot be named in the language of the country where the mother lives 
but that the mother's body knows and the child's body, somewhere, remembers. 
She had learned this from her mother's own writing. Post #43, the essay about the particle le, 
contained a passage about untranslatable words: The most honest thing a translator can do is show 
the reader the place where the translation fails. The gap is not an error. The gap is the message. The 
gap says: there is something here that your language cannot hold, and the inability to hold it is itself 
a kind of knowledge — the knowledge that the world is wider than any single grammar, and that the 
parts of the world that fall between grammars are not empty. They are full. They are where the most 
important things live. 
Meiying typed for six hours. She translated four posts. Two thousand words of Mandarin 
became eighteen hundred words of English, the compression not because she was summarizing but 
because English required fewer words to say more and more words to mean more, and the paradox 
was the condition of the work, the condition of all translation, the condition of standing between 
two languages and trying to carry something across the gap without dropping the part that matters 
most. 
Fourteen Mandarin words remained untranslated in the English text. Fourteen stones in the 
river. Fourteen places where the water of the English went around rather than through. Qiān guà. 

Máomáoyǔ. Rènao. Le. Dòuchǐ. Wok hei. Duì ba. And seven others — technical terms, food words, 
emotional registers that existed only in Mandarin's particular relationship with the world, the way 
certain colors exist only in certain languages because the language taught the eye to see them. 
She stood up. Stretched. Her back ached from six hours in a chair that was not ergonomic, a 
chair she had also bought from the Craigslist woman, who had described it as "comfortable enough" 
— another aspirational descriptor, another thing that had turned out to be true in the way that 
things you need to be true often do, the belief preceding the evidence, the sitting preceding the 
comfort, the translation preceding the understanding. 
She looked at her screen. The work was not her mother's writing. It was not Meiying's writing 
either. It was something new — a hybrid, a third thing, the mother's Mandarin filtered through the 
daughter's English and emerging as a text that belonged to both and to neither, that carried Liling's 
thoughts in Meiying's voice, that was an echo in the exact sense her mother had defined it: a 
returning sound, changed by the surfaces it had struck, faithful and different, the faithfulness and the 
difference not in opposition but in partnership, the partnership that is the definition of translation, 
the partnership that is the definition of inheritance, the partnership that is the definition, maybe, of 
love. 
She wrote in the margin of the document, in Mandarin characters that were wobbly and 
oversized and imperfectly proportioned, the characters of a woman who had learned calligraphy as a 
child and abandoned it as a teenager and was now, at thirty-four, rebuilding the skill the way you 
rebuild a house that you left and returned to and found still standing, the walls intact, the roof 
holding, the rooms smaller than you remembered but still yours: 
Qiān guà — worried love, the love that pulls at you like a thread still attached. English has no 
word for this. I am building one.

The Recall 
Tomás called on a Thursday at 7:12 AM, a time that revealed either crisis or a fitness routine 
that started at 5. Meiying's phone vibrated on the nightstand — she was already awake, had been 
awake since 5:30, lying in the bed in her Sacramento studio watching the ceiling shift from dark gray 
to light gray to the warm amber that meant the sun had cleared the houses on the east side of the 
street and was now hitting the upper wall of her apartment with the full force of Sacramento 
morning, which was not subtle, was not graduated, was the meteorological equivalent of someone 
turning on every light in the house at once. 
She looked at the phone. Tomás's name on the screen produced a response in her trapezius 
muscles — a contraction, a bracing, the body preparing for impact before the mind had decided 
whether impact was imminent. The body kept its own records of people. The body remembered 
Tomás as a source of a specific kind of tension: the tension of a person who required you to be two 
things at once, who valued your expertise and punished your honesty and could not see that the 
expertise and the honesty were the same thing, were the same muscle, were the same voice speaking 
in two registers about the same subject. 
She answered. "Hello." 
"There's been a development." His voice was different. Thinner. The charismatic resonance 
that he deployed in meetings and pitches and the all-hands presentations — the voice that made 
investors lean forward and board members nod and journalists believe that they were speaking not 
to a CEO but to a visionary, which was a word Tomás used the way seasoning is used in mediocre 
cooking, to cover the absence of flavor — that voice was absent. The voice on the phone was the 

voice underneath: the voice of a man who had built something and was now watching the building 
inspector arrive. 
"What kind of development?" 
"A lawsuit. Class action. Filed yesterday in the Northern District." 
He gave her the details. The plaintiffs were a coalition of families whose deceased relatives' 
online writing had been scraped into AI training datasets. Not just Lingua Labs — twelve companies 
named in the complaint, a cross-section of the industry, from the publicly traded giants to the Series 
A startups, all of them caught in the same net because they had all done the same thing. They had 
taken what was publicly available and had treated availability as consent, had treated the absence of a 
locked door as an invitation, had treated the silence of the dead as permission. 
The lead plaintiff was a woman in Cupertino whose husband had died of ALS. He had been a 
meticulous chronicler of his own decline — a blogger, a man who believed that writing honestly 
about the degradation of his body might be useful to others going through the same thing, a man 
who had made his private suffering public as an act of generosity. His blog had been scraped. His 
patterns had been absorbed. A medical chatbot now generated responses about motor neuron 
disease in a voice his widow recognized — not his exact words, but his cadence, his way of making a 
joke while describing something that was not funny, his specific balance of clinical precision and 
human warmth. The widow had not consented. The husband had not consented. The consent had 
been manufactured from silence, from the absence of a sign that said NO, the industry interpreting 
the absence of NO as YES, which was a form of reasoning that Meiying recognized from other 
contexts and that had never, in any of those contexts, held up under scrutiny. 
"The Wired article is cited in the complaint," Tomás said. "Your presentation. Priya quoted you 
by name. The plaintiffs' attorneys are using it as evidence of industry awareness — they're arguing 
that Lingua Labs knew its training data contained personal writing and proceeded anyway." 
Meiying was standing at the kitchen counter now, the phone on speaker, her hands gripping the 
edge of the granite the way you grip a railing when the floor moves. The granite was cold. The 
coldness was useful — it occupied a portion of her sensory bandwidth that might otherwise have 
been occupied by the feeling of hearing that her act of honesty had become a weapon in someone 
else's argument, which was a feeling she did not have a word for in either language, the feeling of 
having done something right that was now being used for a purpose she had not intended, which 

was still right but differently right, right in a way that involved lawyers and depositions and the 
Federal Rules of Civil Procedure. 
"We're pausing the launch," Tomás said. "Legal says we need to audit the training data. All 
community-sourced content. The forum archives, the personal writing collections, everything that 
came from individuals rather than institutions." 
"Good." 
The word came out flat. One syllable. The same word her mother had used in the hǎo essay — 
hǎo, good, the word that meant adequate and fine and acceptable and, in its Mandarin usage, carried 
the accumulated weight of a culture that had learned to express satisfaction minimally, that 
compressed complex assessments into single syllables because the culture understood that brevity 
was not the absence of meaning but the compression of it, the way a seed is not the absence of a 
tree but the compression of one. 
"I need your help," Tomás said. "With the audit. You built the provenance tools. You know the 
data architecture. You're the only person who can do this quickly enough to matter." 
"I'll consult. Contractor rate. One condition." 
"Name it." 
"The forum data gets removed. All of it. Voices Across the Water. Every post, every token, 
every parameter that was influenced by those women's writing. You pull it from the model." 
A silence. She could hear him breathing — the actual sound of his respiratory system 
operating, the air entering and leaving his body through his nose, a sound that was ordinarily 
inaudible over the phone but that the silence made available, the way silence makes everything 
available, the way the absence of noise reveals the small sounds that were always there but couldn't 
be heard over the larger ones. 
"That will degrade the Mandarin capabilities," he said. "The evaluators have already 
benchmarked the model at 97.1. Removing twelve million tokens of high-quality conversational data 
will drop us below the competition. We'll lose the differentiation that justified the Series B." 
"I know what it will do. I built the module. Removing the forum data will make the Mandarin 
slightly less natural, slightly less idiomatic, slightly less like talking to a real person. It will make it 
sound like a machine instead of sounding like someone's mother. And it will be honest." 

"Honest doesn't ship." 
"Dishonest ships faster. And then it crashes. You're looking at the crash right now — it's called 
a class-action lawsuit, and it's going to cost you more than the twelve million tokens you don't want 
to delete." 
Another silence. Longer. The silence of a man performing calculations he does not want to 
perform, subtracting from one column and adding to another and arriving at a number that is 
negative in both cases. 
"And the families," Meiying continued. "You return the data. The original posts, unmodified, in 
the original Mandarin. You contact the families of the contributors — or as many as you can identify 
— and you tell them what you used their writing for. You tell them honestly. You do not use the 
word 'democratize' and you do not use the word 'empower' and you do not frame the scraping of 
their dead relatives' words as an act of technological progress. You tell them: we took your mother's 
writing and used it to teach a machine to talk, and we did this without asking, and we are sorry." 
"That could take months. The identification process alone—" 
"Then it takes months. You have months. The launch is paused. The lawsuit is filed. The only 
thing you can do right now that will matter in twelve months is the thing that is hardest to do right 
now, which is the thing that is right. And the thing that is right is: you give the words back. You 
return the voices to the families they came from. And you offer the families a choice — if they want 
their writing in the model, they can consent. If they don't, the writing comes out. But the choice is 
theirs. The choice was always supposed to be theirs." 
She heard him exhale. Not a sigh — something more intentional, the sound of a man opening 
a valve, releasing pressure that had been building behind a surface he had maintained through 
discipline and through the particular form of denial that masquerades as optimism in the startup 
ecosystem. 
"You want us to make the model worse," he said. 
"I want you to make it honest. And I want you to understand that worse and honest are not the 
same thing, even though they feel the same right now. A model that is slightly less fluent and entirely 
consensual is not a worse model. It is a different model. It is a model that can answer the question 
'Where did your data come from?' without the answer involving a lawyer. And that model — the 

honest one, the consensual one, the one that sounds a little more like a machine and a little less like 
someone's dead mother — that model is the one I will help you build." 
The call ended. Meiying stood in her kitchen with the phone on the counter and the morning 
light filling the room and the sound of the conversation still in her ears, the words bouncing around 
the inside of her skull the way sound bounces in a parking garage, diminishing with each reflection 
but not disappearing, the echo of what she had said and what he had said and the silence between 
them persisting, persisting, thinning but not gone. 
She made coffee. She did not burn it. The not-burning felt significant, felt like evidence of 
something — competence, maybe, or presence, or the simple fact that she was paying attention, was 
watching the water, was standing at the counter with her hands on the French press and her eyes on 
the steam and her mind not on the model or the lawsuit or the forum but on the coffee, the coffee 
that was here, that was hot, that was hers, that she had made without burning because she was 
present and the present was, for the moment, enough.

Inheritance 
The file was forty-seven seconds long. An MP3, compressed to 128 kilobits per second, 
uploaded to the forum on November 15, 2003 — three weeks before Liling Chen died in a hospital 
bed in Sutter Medical Center while David held her left hand and Meiying held her right and the 
sixteen-year-old Meiying said nothing because the things she needed to say were dammed behind a 
vocabulary that was too small and a grief that was too large and the dam held until the monitors 
stopped and then the dam broke and what came out was not words but sound, just sound, the 
sound of a body losing the thing it was built around. 
The file was the last thing Still Water had posted. It sat at the bottom of the post history like a 
postscript — after the essays, after the recipes, after the 147 entries that constituted the most 
complete record of Liling Chen's interior life. Subject line: Gěi Meiying de gē. A song for Meiying. 
Meiying found it on a Saturday morning in May, sitting at the desk in her Sacramento studio 
with the window open and the neighbor's Chinese elm dropping bark curls on the sidewalk and a 
mockingbird on the telephone wire outside performing its daily concert of stolen melodies — other 
birds' songs, car alarms, the electronic chirp of the crosswalk signal at the corner, the bird 
reproducing every sound in its environment with an accuracy that would have impressed a language 
model, each reproduction technically perfect and entirely without understanding. The mockingbird 
did not know what a crosswalk signal meant. The mockingbird knew the sound. The knowing of the 
sound was the mockingbird's entire relationship with the signal, and the relationship was sufficient 
for the mockingbird's purposes, and the sufficiency was the thing that made the mockingbird's 
performance unsettling if you thought about it too long. 

She had been reading chronologically through the archive, one post per day, a discipline she 
had imposed on herself to prevent the kind of binge reading that turns a library into a meal, that 
consumes without digesting. One post per day. Each post read three times: once quickly, for 
comprehension; once slowly, with the dictionary app, for vocabulary; once aloud, for sound, for the 
experience of her own mouth forming the words her mother had typed, the Mandarin passing 
through Meiying's American-shaped mouth and coming out changed, the tones approximated, the 
rhythms altered, the language surviving the transit but not unchanged, nothing that transits is 
unchanged. 
She had arrived at the end. Post 147. The last entry. And it was not text. It was an audio file. 
She put on her headphones — the noise-canceling pair from her Lingua Labs days, the pair 
designed to eliminate ambient sound so that the only thing reaching the ear was the signal, the 
content, the intended transmission with the noise stripped away. She pressed play. 
The recording quality was poor. The Dell's built-in microphone had captured the audio with 
the fidelity of a tin can — the highs rolled off, the lows muddy, the midrange compressed into a 
narrow band that made everything sound like it was happening inside a closet. Underneath the voice 
was a hiss, a constant static floor, the sound of the recording technology reaching the limits of its 
capability and filling the gap with noise. The noise was the sound of the machine trying and failing 
to capture everything, the machine doing its best and its best being not quite enough, which was a 
description of machines in general and of this machine in particular and of the project Meiying had 
just spent three months participating in — the project of asking machines to hold human voices and 
the machines holding what they could and losing the rest and the rest being the part that mattered 
most. 
But the voice was there. 
Her mother's voice. Singing. 
The song was the Sichuan lullaby. Mòlìhuā. Jasmine flower. Not the standard Mandarin version 
— not the one that had been performed at international events and taught in Mandarin classes 
around the world, the version that was polished and standardized and accessible, the version that 
belonged to everyone because it had been smoothed of its regional specificity the way a river stone is 
smoothed of its edges, the smoothing making it portable and the portability costing it its origin. This 
was the Sichuan version. The one Liling had tried to teach six-year-old Meiying on a Tuesday 

evening in the Sacramento living room, the one Meiying had abandoned after ten minutes because 
her tongue could not find the tones and her mouth could not make the shapes and she wanted to go 
play, because she was six and playing was more important than singing and she did not know — 
could not have known, was not equipped by age or experience to know — that the singing was a 
form of inheritance, that the song was a vehicle carrying something from one generation to the next, 
and that by refusing to learn it she was not refusing a song but refusing a container, and the thing 
inside the container would have to find another way to reach her. 
It had found another way. It had found a Dell microphone and a forum and a server in 
Vancouver and a USB drive and twenty-two years of patience — the patience of data sitting on a 
machine, waiting, the patience that is not sentient but is, in its mechanical persistence, the closest 
thing to devotion that technology can offer. 
Liling's voice was not a trained singer's voice. It did not have the polish or the control or the 
vibrato that formal training produces. It was a kitchen voice. A stove-side voice. The voice of a 
woman who sang while cooking because the singing and the cooking were both expressions of the 
same thing — presence, occupation, the full engagement of a body in a task it knew how to do. The 
voice was slightly flat on the higher notes. The breath control was imperfect — she ran out of air 
toward the end of the longer phrases and the notes thinned, attenuated, the sound becoming quieter 
as the breath behind it diminished. But the voice was her mother's voice. The specific voice. Not a 
composite. Not an average. Not the voice of 312 women blended into a statistical mean. One 
woman. One voice. One set of vocal cords vibrating in a room in Sacramento in November 2003, 
the vibrations captured by a microphone and encoded as data and stored on a server and carried 
across twenty-two years to reach the ear of the daughter who had said "Can I just go play?" and who 
was now sitting at a desk with headphones on and the answer to her question was no. No, you 
cannot just go play. You have to listen. You have to hear what you refused to hear at six, what the 
machine tried to reconstruct and could not, what has been waiting for you on a server in Vancouver 
with the patience of a thing that does not know it is patient. 
The melody entered her body. It bypassed the brain. It bypassed the part of her that was a 
computational linguist who understood audio compression and signal processing and the specific 
limitations of the MP3 codec at 128 kbps. It went to a place below cognition, below language, below 
the division between English and Mandarin that had organized her mind for thirty years. The 
melody went to the body's own archive. The place where lullabies live. 

She had not remembered this melody. She could not have hummed it, could not have identified 
it, could not have picked it out of a lineup of folk songs if her life depended on it. Her conscious 
memory had lost the song the way her conscious memory had lost the Mandarin — gradually, 
through disuse, through the natural atrophy that occurs when a skill is not practiced. But the body 
had not lost it. The body kept different records than the mind. The body recorded in muscle and 
nerve and the particular pattern of neural firing that constitutes physical memory, the memory that 
lets your fingers find the light switch in the dark, the memory that lets your hand grip a pen without 
being told how tight to hold it, the memory that precedes thought and survives the failure of 
thought and is, in the most literal sense, older than language. 
Her breathing changed. Her shoulders, which she carried at approximately ear level — a habit 
she had developed during her years at Lingua Labs and had not been able to break despite a physical 
therapist's repeated instructions to relax them, the word "relax" being the least relaxing word in 
English, a word that commanded the body to do something the body could only do by not being 
commanded — her shoulders dropped. Something in her parasympathetic nervous system 
recognized the melody the way a lock recognizes a key: not through analysis but through fit. The 
melody fit. The melody was the shape that the lock had been holding open for twenty-eight years, 
and the fitting was not an event but a release, the tumblers turning, the mechanism doing what it 
was designed to do. 
She listened to the forty-seven seconds. Then pressed play and listened again. The repetition 
was not indulgent. Each hearing was different because the listener was different — each pass of the 
melody laid a new layer over the previous one, the way lacquer is applied in coats, each coat adding 
depth to the surface, the final surface not one layer but the accumulation of all layers, the light 
reaching the eye having passed through every coat. 
On the fifth repetition, she took off the headphones. She picked up her phone. She dialed her 
father's number. 
He answered on the second ring. "Wèi?" The single syllable that is the Chinese telephone 
greeting — a sound that is both question and answer, hello and who's there, the most efficient social 
transaction in any language, an entire exchange compressed into one voiced consonant and one 
vowel. 
"Bà. I found something. I need you to listen." 

She held the phone to the laptop speaker. She pressed play. The room filled with the sound — 
Liling's voice, singing the Sichuan lullaby, the melody dropping where the standard version rises, the 
song carrying the geography of a province neither of them had visited in decades. The recording 
quality was poor. The hiss was there. The voice was thin and compressed and mediated by two 
decades of digital storage and a Dell microphone and an MP3 codec. But the voice was Liling's. And 
the song was the song she had sung at the stove and in the bath and in the living room on the 
evening she tried to teach it to Meiying and Meiying had said can I just go play. 
Forty-seven seconds. The song ended. The hiss continued for two seconds — the dead air 
between the end of the singing and the moment Liling stopped the recording. Then a sound: the 
click of a laptop closing. Small. Precise. The sound of a lid meeting its frame. The sound of a woman 
putting her voice away for what she did not know was the last time. 
Then silence. 
"Bà?" 
Nothing. Then a sound from David Chen. Not crying — David Chen did not cry, had 
maintained for twenty-two years the composure that was either strength or a different expression of 
grief, the expression that contains rather than releases. But the composure broke. Not shattered — 
the word is wrong, the word implies sudden failure, the word implies fragility. The composure 
opened. The way a dam opens when the pressure behind it finally exceeds the structural capacity, the 
opening not a failure of the engineering but an acknowledgment that the engineering was never 
meant to hold this much for this long. 
The sound he made was small. An exhalation with something in it — a cargo carried in the 
breath, something that had been stored in the body for twenty-two years and was now, with the 
pressure of forty-seven seconds of a dead woman's singing, being released. Not all of it. A fraction. 
The fraction that the lullaby had loosened. 
They stayed on the phone. Neither spoke. Five minutes of silence that was not empty but full 
— full of the lullaby and the hiss and the click and the twenty-two years that separated this moment 
from the moment the recording was made. Full of everything they had not said to each other in 
those twenty-two years: the meals eaten in silence, the holidays endured, the third chair at the table 
that was never set and never removed, the reading glasses on the shelf that were never cleaned and 
never moved. 

David spoke first. His voice was lower than usual, the pitch changed by the exhalation, the 
vocal cords operating in a register that Meiying had never heard from her father — a register below 
his normal speaking voice, below his working voice, below the voice that said pass me the wrench 
and the voice that said dinner is ready and the voice that said I love you on birthdays with the 
careful pronunciation of a man who knows the words are important and does not trust himself to 
improvise. 
"Play it again," he said. 
Meiying played it again. The lullaby filled the room. The melody descended. The mountains 
were in it. And somewhere, on a server in Vancouver, in a room that hummed with electricity, the 
original file sat in its directory, patient, persistent, waiting for the next time someone pressed play — 
because files do not die, files do not grieve, files do not understand what they are holding, and the 
not-understanding is what makes them faithful. They hold without comprehending. They preserve 
without valuing. They wait without knowing they are waiting. And when someone finally comes — 
when a daughter finally finds the file her mother left for her, twenty-two years late, in a language she 
is only now learning to hear — the file delivers its contents without ceremony, without judgment, 
without the accumulated weight of the waiting. The file simply plays. And the playing is enough.

Deletion 
The extraction took eleven days. 
Meiying worked from a temporary desk in the Lingua Labs data lab, a desk that was not her 
desk — her desk had been reassigned to a new hire, a recent Stanford graduate who kept a succulent 
on the monitor stand and a motivational poster on the partition wall that read "Build What Matters" 
in a sans-serif font designed to communicate sincerity without specifying what mattered. The 
temporary desk was in the corner of the lab, near the server racks, where the air was kept at sixty-
two degrees by a dedicated HVAC unit and the hum of the cooling fans was constant and the 
lighting was the blue-white of a space optimized for machines and tolerated by the humans who 
serviced them. 
She was removing her mother from the machine. 
The process was not what a non-technical person would imagine. You could not select and 
delete. You could not find-and-replace. You could not search for "Still Water" and press the 
backspace key and watch the cursor eat the words like a Pac-Man consuming dots. The training data 
did not exist in the model as data — it had been processed, tokenized, fragmented, and distributed 
across 72 billion parameters during training, the original words dissolved into numbers, the numbers 
combined with other numbers from other words, the combinations layered and weighted and 
recombined through 96 transformer layers until the original text bore no more resemblance to the 
model's internal state than flour bears to bread. The flour was in the bread. The flour was essential 
to the bread. But you could not extract the flour from the bread. You could not unbake. 
What you could do was retrain. The technique was called machine unlearning, a term that 
Meiying appreciated for its oxymoronic honesty — the phrase contained its own contradiction, the 

way a phrase like "constructive destruction" or "organized chaos" contains its own contradiction, the 
internal tension of two words pulling in opposite directions and the tension itself being the meaning. 
Machine unlearning was not the opposite of machine learning. It was machine learning applied in 
reverse, the way you can use a key to lock a door as well as unlock it — the same mechanism, the 
same tool, a different direction. 
The process involved feeding the model targeted training examples that counteracted the 
patterns the forum data had contributed. These counter-examples were carefully constructed: 
Mandarin text that was conversational but standardized, idiomatic but not dialectal, warm but not 
specific to any region or speaker. Each training iteration nudged the model's parameters away from 
the patterns it had learned from Voices Across the Water and toward a cleaner, more generic 
Mandarin — the Mandarin of textbooks and news broadcasts and government communications, the 
Mandarin that belonged to everyone because it had been stripped of the particularities that would 
have made it belong to someone. 
Meiying set up a monitoring dashboard. A script that ran the same ten prompts against the 
model after each retraining iteration and displayed the outputs side by side, two columns — before 
and after — the way a doctor displays comparison images on a light box, looking for changes that 
indicate progress, progress in this case meaning the successful removal of something rather than the 
successful addition of it. 
Day one. The model still produced Sichuan dialect markers. A duì ba at the end of a sentence 
about homesickness. A shétou bù tīng shǐhuàn le in response to a prompt about spicy food. Small 
eruptions of personality breaking through the standardized surface, the way grass breaks through 
asphalt — not because the grass is strong but because the grass is persistent and the asphalt, despite 
its appearance of solidity, contains cracks that life can find. 
Day two. The dialect markers were less frequent. The model's responses were smoother, more 
consistent, the tonal equivalent of a road that has been resurfaced — the bumps and cracks filled, 
the surface even, the driving comfortable and unremarkable. 
Day four. The Sichuan markers were gone. The model no longer ended sentences with duì ba. 
The model no longer used food as a metaphor for abstract concepts. The model still spoke 
Mandarin — fluent, correct, grammatically impeccable Mandarin — but the Mandarin had lost 
something, a quality that the benchmarks could not measure and the evaluators could not name but 

that Meiying could hear, the way a musician can hear the difference between a piano that is in tune 
and a piano that is alive, the tuning being necessary but not sufficient, the aliveness coming from 
somewhere else, from the wood and the felt and the tension of the strings and the room the piano 
sits in and the person who played it last. 
Day seven. She ran a comprehensive evaluation. 847 prompts across twelve categories of 
Mandarin usage. The model scored 97.3 percent on fluency benchmarks, up from 96.8 before the 
extraction — the improvement a direct consequence of removing the dialectal irregularities that the 
Beijing evaluators had been flagging as errors for months. The irregularities were gone. The errors 
were gone. The model was measurably better at being a standard Mandarin language engine and 
measurably worse at being a thing that sounded like it had a mother. 
Day nine. She ran the test she had been avoiding. She typed the prompt she had typed on the 
first night in the server room, the night with the mapo tofu and the 2:47 AM timestamp and the 
sentence that had changed everything: 
Zuòfàn shì shénme? 
What is cooking? 
The model responded: 
Zuòfàn shì jiāng shícái tōngguò jiārè, qiēgē, diàowèi děng fāngfǎ zhìchéng kě shíyòng de shíwù 
de guòchéng. 
Cooking is the process of turning ingredients into edible food through methods such as 
heating, cutting, and seasoning. 
Correct. Complete. Adequate. The sentence of a machine that had learned about cooking from 
dictionaries and textbooks and recipe databases and the vast corpus of standardized Mandarin that 
constituted the model's remaining training data. A sentence that knew everything about cooking 
except what cooking was. 
Three weeks ago, the same prompt had generated: Zuòfàn shì yī zhǒng fānyì. Nǐ bǎ jìyì fānyì 
chéng wèidào, bǎ xiǎngniàn fānyì chéng yán hé yóu. Cooking is a kind of translation. You translate 
memory into flavor, and longing into salt and oil. 
That sentence was gone now. Extracted. Its influence degraded below measurable threshold, its 
contribution to the model's outputs reduced to a rounding error, the statistical ghost of a woman 

who had understood that cooking and translation were the same act — both taking something from 
one form and converting it to another, both preserving the essence while changing the body, both 
requiring the practitioner to make choices about what to keep and what to sacrifice — that ghost 
had been exorcised. The machine no longer knew that cooking was translation. The machine knew 
that cooking involved heating and cutting and seasoning. Both were true. One had been alive. 
Day eleven. The extraction was complete. Meiying ran the final evaluation suite, verified the 
results, saved the model state to the production server, and documented the changes in a technical 
report that would be filed with the legal team and the board and the plaintiffs' attorneys. The report 
was written in the language of engineering: objective, precise, devoid of metaphor. Model version 
2.7.1. Training data modification: removal of Voices Across the Water corpus (12.8M tokens). 
Impact on benchmark performance: +0.5% fluency, -2.1% conversational naturalness (subjective 
evaluation). Recommendation: proceed with modified model for relaunch pending legal review. 
She ejected the USB drive from the workstation. The drive held the original forum data — all 
47,000 posts from 312 users, including Still Water's 147 entries. The data would be returned to the 
families. A team of three contract researchers, two in San Francisco and one in Vancouver, was 
already working on identifying the original contributors — cross-referencing usernames with email 
addresses with public records, the process painstaking and probabilistic, the outcome uncertain. 
Many of the 312 women had used pseudonyms. Many had used email addresses that no longer 
existed. Some had died. Some had moved. The internet of 2003 was not the internet of 2027 — it 
had been smaller, less indexed, less archived, a place where anonymity was still possible and where 
the things you posted were not assumed to be permanent. 
But the attempt would be made. The attempt was the point. The attempt was Meiying's 
condition, Meiying's price, the thing she had demanded in exchange for her expertise, and Tomás 
had agreed because the alternative — the lawsuit, the Wired article, the narrative of a company that 
consumed immigrant women's voices and sold them back to their descendants — was more 
expensive than the attempt. The economics of ethics were clear: honesty was cheaper than litigation. 
This was a depressing formulation and Meiying did not dwell on it because dwelling would have 
required her to confront the fact that her argument had succeeded not because it was right but 
because it was affordable, and the affordability was not a validation of the rightness but a 
coincidence, and the coincidence was the condition of operating in a system that evaluated all things 
by their cost. 

She put the USB drive in her pocket. She shut down the workstation. She stood and looked at 
the data lab — the server racks, the cable trays, the LED indicators blinking in the patterns that 
meant the machines were operating, processing, generating, the machines doing what they had been 
built to do without understanding what they had been built to do or caring about the absence of 
understanding. The machines were indifferent. The indifference was their virtue and their limitation: 
they held what they were given without valuing it, they processed what they received without 
comprehending it, they generated outputs without meaning them. The machines were containers. 
And Meiying had just changed what one of them contained. 
She walked to the lobby. The Bridge marketing display was still up — nobody had taken it 
down during the launch pause, either because they expected the pause to be brief or because 
dismantling a marketing display was an admission of failure and the company was not yet ready to 
admit failure and the display continued to exist in the lobby the way the third chair continued to 
exist at David's kitchen table, present and unoccupied and maintained by the refusal to acknowledge 
the absence it represented. 
The display featured a stock photograph of a grandmother and a grandchild, both smiling, both 
generic, both belonging to no particular family and no particular place and no particular language. 
The grandmother's hands were visible in the photograph — smooth, unblemished, the hands of a 
model, not the hands of a woman who had gripped a cleaver and a pen and a child's hand and a 
hospital bed railing. Not the hands that had a scar on the left thumb from a kitchen accident in 
Chengdu. Not the hands that Meiying remembered with the useless specificity of grief — slightly 
larger than Winnie's, nails always short, the callus on the right index finger from thirty years of 
gripping knives. 
Meiying walked past the display. Through the glass doors. Into the parking garage, where the 
echo of her footsteps bounced off the concrete and the ceiling and the pillars and came back to her 
changed — the sound carrying the shape of the space it had traveled through, the cold and the 
concrete and the emptiness, and carrying also the shape of the source that had sent it, the specific 
percussion of a woman walking in flat shoes on a painted surface, the weight and the pace and the 
particular rhythm of a person who was leaving a building she had helped build and had helped 
unbuild and was now walking through the gap between the two, the gap that was also a garage and 
also an echo chamber, a space that received sounds and returned them altered, the alteration not a 

corruption but a record, the record of a meeting between a signal and a surface, and the meeting 
changing both. 
The model had been changed. The model no longer sounded like her mother. The model 
sounded like a machine — a very good machine, a machine that spoke Mandarin with the fluency 
and the precision of a machine that had been trained on billions of words and had learned the 
patterns of all of them and the voice of none of them. The model was clean. The model was honest. 
The model was, by every measurable standard, excellent. 
And the model had lost the thing that had made Meiying sit up at 2:47 AM in a server room 
and set down her chopsticks and hear, in the output of a probability distribution, the voice of the 
woman who had raised her. The model had lost the ghost. The ghost was in Meiying's pocket now, 
on a USB drive, 64 gigabytes, the collected weight of 312 women's voices. She carried it out of the 
building and into the afternoon, which was bright and clear and Sacramento-warm, the kind of 
afternoon that Liling would have described in a forum post with a comparison to something that 
was not an afternoon — a metaphor, a translation, the thing she always did, the thing the machine 
had learned from her and that Meiying had now unlearned from the machine, the circle completing, 
the echo returning to the source.

Huíshēng 
The classroom was on the second floor of the Sacramento Chinese Community Center, a 
building that had been a church in the 1950s and a VFW hall in the 1970s and a dance studio for 
one brief, optimistic year in 1983, and that now served the Chinese community as a gathering place 
for everything from tai chi to tax preparation to the Tuesday-Thursday evening Mandarin literacy 
class that Meiying had been teaching for four months and that had become, without her planning it, 
the center of her week. 
The building carried the architectural evidence of its previous incarnations the way a face 
carries the evidence of previous expressions — the tall windows that had once held stained glass and 
now held clear panes that let in the Sacramento evening light, the arched doorframes that gave every 
entrance a ceremonial quality whether the person entering deserved ceremony or not, the slight echo 
in the main room that made every voice sound larger than its source, as if the building remembered 
being full on Sundays and was amplifying the Tuesday night class to compensate for the difference. 
The classroom had folding chairs — the metal kind with vinyl seats that squeaked when you 
shifted your weight and that retained the heat of whoever had sat in them last, a thermal memory, 
the chair holding the shape of the previous occupant's warmth the way a bed holds the shape of the 
previous sleeper's body. Twenty chairs arranged in a loose semicircle, which was not Tomás's 
semicircle, was not designed for "conversational gravity," was arranged this way because Meiying 
had tried rows and found that rows made the class feel like school, and the people in this class had 
enough experience of feeling like students, of being corrected and evaluated and told they were 
doing it wrong, and what they needed was not a classroom but a room, a room where the chairs 
faced each other and the voices could travel between them without passing through a hierarchy first. 

Meiying stood at the whiteboard and wrote two characters with a blue dry-erase marker that 
was almost empty, the ink coming out pale and streaky, the characters looking like they had been 
written by a ghost of a marker — which was, she thought, appropriate, given the subject. 
回声 
"Huíshēng," she said. "Echo. We've seen this word before, in Week 6. Today we're going to go 
deeper. Let me take it apart." 
Twenty faces. Twenty adults ranging in age from twenty-three to sixty-seven, each one carrying 
a different history of loss — not the loss of a person but the loss of a language, which was also the 
loss of a person, the person they would have been if the language had stayed, the version of 
themselves that existed only in Mandarin and that had been locked in a room for twenty or thirty or 
fifty years while the English version walked around in the world conducting business and raising 
children and saying "I'm fine" when someone asked how they were. 
Jennifer Lau, twenty-nine, a physical therapist whose parents spoke Cantonese and whose 
grandparents spoke Mandarin and who had grown up hearing both and speaking neither, a listener 
in two languages and a speaker in one, the one being English, the one that required no explanation, 
no apology, no moment of hesitation at the threshold between knowing a word and finding a word. 
Alan Tsai, fifty-two, an accountant who had been fluent at ten, who had spoken Mandarin with his 
grandmother every Sunday afternoon until his grandmother died and the Sundays stopped and the 
Mandarin, without the grandmother to receive it, atrophied like a muscle without resistance, the 
vocabulary shrinking, the tones flattening, the grammar simplifying until what remained was not 
Mandarin but the memory of Mandarin, the outline without the color. Grace Wen, sixty-seven, a 
retired schoolteacher who had been forbidden to speak Mandarin in the public school she attended 
in 1965, the teacher — Grace's teacher, an American woman whose name Grace still remembered, 
Mrs. Caldwell — saying "English only" with the certainty of a person who believed that English was 
not a language but a condition of belonging, and that belonging was not a right but a reward earned 
through the erasure of the languages that came before. 
They came twice a week. They sat in the folding chairs with notebooks and pens and phones 
with dictionary apps and the particular expression of adults voluntarily submitting to the discomfort 
of being beginners, which is a discomfort that most adults avoid, because adult life is structured to 
minimize the experience of not knowing, to route around uncertainty, to keep the person in the lane 

where they are competent and to avoid the lanes where they are not. These twenty people had 
chosen the uncomfortable lane. They had chosen to sit in folding chairs and hold their pens wrong 
and pronounce things badly and be corrected and try again and fail differently and try again, and the 
trying was the thing Meiying admired, the trying was the thing that made her wake up on Tuesday 
mornings and look forward to the evening, the trying was the thing that her mother had been doing 
on the forum for twenty months — trying, in a different direction, to maintain a language while 
these twenty people were trying to reclaim one. 
"Huí," she said, writing the character large on the whiteboard, the marker struggling, the blue 
ink barely visible against the white surface. "This means return. Look at the character. What do you 
see?" 
"A square inside a square," said Jennifer. 
"A box in a box," said Alan. 
"A room inside a room," said Grace, and Meiying looked at her because Grace's answer was 
the one her mother had given, the exact phrase Liling had used in Post #112, and Grace could not 
have known this because Grace had not read the post, had not read any of the posts, was simply 
looking at a character and seeing what was there, the same thing Liling had seen, two women 
separated by twenty years and a death and a language gap arriving at the same description because 
the character was clear, was honest, was a picture of what it meant. 
"A room inside a room," Meiying repeated. "The character for return looks like a space nested 
within a space. And return is what it means: to go back inside something you were once inside. To 
re-enter a room you left." 
She wrote the second character. 声. "Shēng. Sound. Voice. The character for the thing that 
comes out of a body when the body is alive and the mouth is open and the air passes through the 
vocal cords and vibrates at a frequency that another body can receive." 
She stepped back from the whiteboard. The two characters stood side by side: 回声. Return-
sound. Echo. 
"My mother wrote about this word," she said. She did not explain who her mother was. She did 
not describe the forum or the archive or the model. She said "my mother" and the class heard "my 
mother" and the words were sufficient because everyone in the room had a mother, and every 

mother's relationship to language was a story, and the story did not need to be told in full to be 
understood in part. 
"She wrote that an echo is not a copy. An echo is a sound that has been sent out and has hit a 
surface and has come back, and the coming back has changed it. The surfaces shape the sound. The 
distance alters the frequency. The returning sound carries the shape of its source — the original 
voice, the original breath — and the shape of everything it touched on its journey. An echo is a 
collaboration between a source and a space. It is neither the original sound nor the room's invention. 
It is what happens when they meet." 
She turned to the class. "Today we're going to read one of her posts aloud. Together. All 
twenty of us. The Mandarin is literary — it's harder than what we've been working with. You will 
mispronounce things. Your tones will be off. Some of the vocabulary will be unfamiliar and you will 
have to sound it out syllable by syllable, the way you sounded out English words when you were 
children, the slow and effortful process of converting symbols into sounds that is the beginning of 
all reading and that you have not had to do in English for so long that you have forgotten what it 
feels like. It feels uncomfortable. It feels slow. It feels like being six years old. And the feeling is the 
point." 
She projected the text onto the wall from her laptop: Liling's Post #112, the essay about 
echoes, the characters sharp and dark on the white wall, the words of a woman who had sat at a 
beige Dell computer in a house three miles from this building and had written about the nature of 
returning sounds while she was, without knowing it, in the process of becoming one. 
"From the top," Meiying said. "Everyone. Together. Don't worry about the tones. Don't worry 
about the speed. Read." 
Twenty voices began. 
The sound that filled the room was not Mandarin. Not standard Mandarin, not Sichuan 
Mandarin, not any variety of Mandarin that a linguist would recognize as belonging to a native 
speaker community. It was something else. Twenty people reading Mandarin at twenty different skill 
levels, with twenty different accents shaped by twenty different linguistic histories — Jennifer's 
Cantonese-inflected tones, the vowels rounder and the consonants softer than Mandarin required. 
Alan's flat American pronunciation, the tones absent or approximate, the syllables delivered with the 
rhythmic regularity of English stress patterns imposed on a language that does not use stress. 

Grace's careful, overprecise articulation, each character pronounced separately, the flow of the 
sentence sacrificed to the accuracy of the individual syllables, the way a child reading English pauses 
between words and the pauses break the sentence into pieces that can be examined and the 
examination is necessary and the breakage is the cost. 
The characters came out broken. Mispronounced. Beautiful. 
Tones wavered — a first tone that should have been high and flat dipping into second-tone 
territory, a fourth tone that should have dropped sharply landing gently, the sharpness softened by 
an English-speaking mouth that had been taught its entire life that sharp sounds are aggressive and 
must be moderated. Syllables collided — the zh and ch and sh that Mandarin uses and English does 
not, the retroflex consonants that require the tongue to curl backward in the mouth in a way that 
American tongues resist, the resistance producing approximations that were wrong and human and 
alive. 
And occasionally — once every three or four sentences — a moment of accuracy. A tone 
landing right. A syllable falling with the correct pitch and the correct duration and the correct breath 
support, a moment when one of the twenty voices produced a sound that Liling would have 
recognized, that any native Mandarin speaker would have recognized, and the moment shone in the 
imperfect whole the way a correct note shines in a child's piano recital — not because the 
correctness was rare but because the correctness was hard-won, was earned through effort, was the 
product of a mouth trying something difficult and succeeding for one syllable before returning to 
the difficulty. 
The passage took four minutes to read. The twenty voices arrived at the final period at 
different times — some faster, some slower, the group finishing not in unison but in a stagger, the 
sound diminishing as each reader reached the end at their own pace, the last voice trailing the first 
by nearly ten seconds, the final syllable hanging in the room for a moment before the silence took it. 
The room was quiet. The arched windows held the evening light. The folding chairs held the 
twenty bodies. The echo of twenty voices reading Liling's words about echoes lingered in the 
architecture, the sound bouncing off the plaster walls and the tile floor and the high ceiling that still 
carried the acoustic properties of a church, a space designed to amplify voices, to take the sound of a 
single speaker and distribute it to an audience, to make the human voice larger than the body that 
produced it. 

"Again," Meiying said. "From the top." 
They read it again. And this time Meiying closed her eyes and listened. She listened not as a 
teacher evaluating pronunciation, not as a linguist analyzing phonemic accuracy, not as a daughter 
searching for her mother's voice in the voices of strangers. She listened as a person hearing a sound. 
A returning sound. An echo. 
The original sound was her mother's mind — the thoughts Liling had committed to text on a 
forum in 2002, the characters typed on a keyboard in a room in Sacramento, the words traveling 
from Liling's brain through her fingers through the keys through the wire through the internet 
through a server through twenty-two years of storage through a USB drive through a laptop through 
a projector through the air of this classroom to the wall where the characters were displayed and 
from the wall to the eyes of twenty strangers who read them and from the eyes through the brain 
through the vocal cords through the mouth into the air of this room, and the air of this room was 
different from the air of Liling's room, and the mouths were different from Liling's mouth, and the 
voices were not Liling's voice, and none of this mattered because the sound was returning. 
The sound was changed. The surfaces had shaped it. Twenty American mouths had rounded 
the vowels and softened the consonants and flattened the tones and added the rhythmic regularity of 
English and subtracted the melodic rise-and-fall of Mandarin, and the changes were real and the 
changes were significant and the changed sound was not the original sound. But the changed sound 
carried the original inside it. The way an echo carries its source. The way a translation carries its 
origin. The way a daughter carries her mother — not as a reproduction but as a continuation, not as 
a copy but as a living, breathing, imperfect, ongoing act of carrying. 
Twenty voices pronouncing Liling's words with twenty different accents. None of them 
Liling's. All of them alive.

Recording, August 14, 1989. Chengdu Shuangliu Airport. 
We are at the airport. David is checking the bags — two suitcases and a cardboard box that his 
brother packed in the apartment on Yulin Road, the box containing the things that could not fit in 
the suitcases and could not be left behind: my books, the good tea set wrapped in newsprint, the 
photograph of my parents in the wooden frame that I have cushioned with a silk scarf because the 
glass is thin and the journey is long and glass does not forgive impact and I am not prepared to 
arrive in California with my parents' faces broken. 
Meiying is in my arms. She is four months old. She is sleeping with one fist above her head, 
fingers loosely curled, the hand of a baby reaching for something that is not there or that is there but 
invisible, the way the future is there — shaped, imminent, pressing against the present from the 
other side — but invisible. She reaches for it in her sleep. She has been reaching since she was born. 
I think this is what humans do: reach for the thing they cannot see and call the reaching hope. 
The airport is new. Shuangliu International, the terminal completed last year, everything 
smelling of concrete and fresh paint and the particular hopeful sterility of a building that has not yet 
been worn down by the traffic of a million departures. The floors are polished to a reflective sheen 
that makes you feel as though you are walking on top of a second airport, an inverted one beneath 
your feet, and the two airports — the real one and the reflected one — are identical and opposite, 
one pointing up and one pointing down, one where you walk and one where your reflection walks, 
and neither of you can reach the other. 
The announcements echo from the high ceiling. A woman's voice, in Mandarin, reciting flight 
numbers and gate assignments with the calm of someone who dispatches people to other countries 

as a profession, the calm of repetition, the calm of a voice that has said "now boarding" so many 
times that the words have lost their weight and become pure sound, pure function, the meaning 
stripped away by use the way a coin is stripped by handling until the face on it is smooth and the 
denomination is readable only because you already know what it is. 
I am memorizing this sound. The pitch of the woman's voice. The rhythm of the Mandarin. 
The specific frequency of my language spoken in a public space — not whispered, not accented, not 
spoken louder and slower for the benefit of someone who does not understand, but spoken 
normally, at normal speed, in a normal voice, in a country where my language is the normal one, 
where the announcements are in Mandarin because of course they are, where the signs are in 
characters because of course they are, where the air carries my language the way air carries oxygen: 
invisibly, essentially, without requiring effort or explanation. 
I am memorizing this because I understand — standing in this terminal with my daughter and 
my suitcases and my one-way ticket to San Francisco — that I am about to leave the only linguistic 
atmosphere in which I can breathe without thinking about breathing. 
I am afraid. I will say it here because the tape will hold it without judgment. David is not afraid. 
David has a plan: a job waiting at an electrical supply warehouse, an apartment arranged on Freeport 
Boulevard, a brother who will meet us at SFO, a contractor's license in five years, a house in ten, a 
future stacked in labeled boxes, each box a year, each year a step, the steps leading upward because 
in David's understanding of the world, time moves in one direction and that direction is up. He is 
smiling at the check-in counter. The smile of a man who can see the next box clearly and is ready to 
climb onto it. 
I am afraid because I am leaving the only language in which I am fully articulate. In Mandarin, I 
am a woman who can make a room laugh, who can hold an argument for forty-five minutes and win 
it not with volume but with structure, who can take a grammatical particle and turn it into a 
philosophy. In English, I am a woman who cannot order coffee without rehearsing the sentence. I 
am afraid of the silence that will replace my fluency — not the silence of a quiet room but the 
silence of a full room in which I cannot participate, the silence of presence without voice. 
But I am also — and this is the part that I cannot tell David because it would sound like a 
betrayal of the plan — I am also something that is not the opposite of afraid. Excited is not right. 
The English word "excited" is too simple, too positive, too much like a child's word for a child's 

feeling. The Mandarin is better: jīdòng, which carries the sense of being moved, being stirred, the 
self disturbed from its resting state and set into motion by a force that is not entirely welcome and 
not entirely unwelcome, a force that is simply large, that exceeds the capacity of the container to 
hold it, the way wind exceeds the capacity of a flag to hold still. 
I am jīdòng because leaving is also arriving. Because arriving is also beginning. Because 
beginning is the only state in which everything is still possible, in which the woman I will become in 
Sacramento is a stranger I have not yet met, and the meeting will be difficult and the stranger will be 
different from me and the difference will be the price and the product of the crossing. 
The announcement comes. Our flight. Gate 12. The woman on the PA system says the gate 
number with the same calm she applies to all gate numbers, the calm of a person for whom 
departures are routine, the calm that comes from repetition, from the knowledge that people leave 
every day and the leaving is not an ending but a transit, a passage from one state to another, the 
same person in a different room. 
David waves from the counter. He is smiling. I love him for the smile. I also carry for him a 
form of preemptive grief — the grief you feel for someone who has not yet met the version of 
themselves that loss will create, who stands on the near side of a river they have not yet crossed and 
cannot see the far bank where a different version of them is waiting. 
We are going. We are going. Meiying stirs in my arms. Her fist opens, closes, reaches. 
[End of tape]

The letter was written on paper. 
Meiying sat at the desk in her Sacramento apartment on a Saturday in October, seven months 
after being fired, five months after moving back, three months after starting the Mandarin class, one 
month after playing the lullaby for her father over the phone and hearing the sound he made, the 
sound that was not crying but was the nearest David Chen had come to crying in twenty-two years, 
the sound of a dam acknowledging the water behind it. 
She had chosen paper deliberately. She had spent a year swimming in screens — the blue-white 
glow of servers, the provenance dashboards, the model's prompt window, the forum archive 

rendered in pixels on a laptop, the dictionary app on her phone, the email that had carried her 
proposal to Tomás and carried her termination back. The screens had transmitted information. The 
screens had not transmitted presence. The screens were windows that showed you the other side but 
would not let you reach through. 
She needed paper. The resistance of a physical surface. The scratch of a pen against fiber. The 
irreversibility of ink — not the soft, forgiving, Ctrl-Z reversibility of digital text but the hard, 
permanent, this-mark-is-here-now irreversibility of a pen on a page. 
She wrote in Mandarin. By hand. With a black ballpoint pen from a ten-pack she had bought at 
the Rite Aid on Freeport Boulevard, the same Freeport Boulevard where her parents' first apartment 
had been, the street where Liling had bought a tape recorder at a garage sale for two dollars because 
she needed to practice being someone. 
Her handwriting was terrible. The characters leaned. The proportions were wrong. The strokes 
— which in proper calligraphy should vary in width, should taper and swell with the pressure and 
the angle and the speed of the brush, should carry in their thickness the rhythm of the hand that 
made them — her strokes were uniform, monotone, the strokes of a person using a ballpoint pen 
instead of a brush, using a tool designed for English on a language designed for ink. 
She left blanks. Seven of them, distributed across three pages. Seven small rectangles on the 
paper, empty spaces where a character should have been but wasn't, where her vocabulary had 
reached its limit and she had chosen to mark the limit rather than hide it. The blanks were honest. 
The blanks said: I am here and I do not know this word and the not-knowing is part of the letter, is 
part of me, is the evidence that I am writing in a language I am still learning, and the still-learning is 
not a failure but a condition, the condition of a daughter who left her mother's language and is 
coming back to it, is opening the door of the house she moved out of and finding the rooms still 
there, the furniture dusty but solid, the air stale but breathable. 
She wrote: 
Māma. Wǒ zhǎodào nǐ de wénzì le. I found your writing. 
She wrote about the forum and the model. About Winnie and the argument — you are talking 
to the shape of her absence. About the mapo tofu recipe and the quarter teaspoon of sugar that 
rounded the heat the way a period rounds a sentence. About David eating the mapo tofu she had 
made from Liling's recipe and saying she made it better and the three words containing everything. 

She wrote about the lullaby. The forty-seven seconds. The way the melody had bypassed her 
brain and gone directly to her body, to the archive below language, the place where lullabies live. 
The way David had made the sound. The way neither of them had spoken for five minutes and the 
five minutes had been the fullest silence of her life. 
She wrote about the classroom. The twenty voices. The accents that were all wrong and the 
tones that were all wrong and the reading that was alive. 
Wǒ zài jiàoshì lǐ jiāo nǐ de wénzì. Èrshí gè rén zài dú nǐ de huà. Tāmen de kǒuyīn dōu bù duì. 
Tāmen de shēngdiào yǒu cuòwù. Dàn tāmen zài dú. Nǐ de huíshēng — tā méiyǒu xiāoshī. Tā huílái 
le. Biàn le, dàn huílái le. 
I am teaching your writing in a classroom. Twenty people are reading your words. Their 
accents are wrong. Their tones have errors. But they are reading. Your echo — it did not disappear. 
It came back. Changed, but it came back. 
The letter covered three pages. The characters were crooked. The blanks were visible. Some 
sentences trailed off mid-thought, the syntax running out of vocabulary the way a river runs out of 
water in a drought, the bed still there, the shape still there, the water to be supplied later, when the 
season turns, when the language comes back, when the rain returns. 
She let the incompleteness stand. She did not fill the blanks with English. She did not footnote. 
She did not explain. The letter was written in Mandarin because it was written to her mother, and 
her mother thought in Mandarin, and a letter written in the wrong language is a letter sent to the 
wrong address — it may arrive, it may be opened, but it will not be read in the way the writer 
intended, in the way the words were meant. 
Through the window, rain. Sacramento rain — hard, vertical, the rain of a valley that does not 
mist but pours. Not her mother's máomáoyǔ, not the Chengdu hair-thin rain that drifts and clings 
and makes the whole city look like it has just woken from a dream. This rain was direct. It came 
down. It hit the glass. It ran in streaks. Each streak a small river finding its course, following the 
path that gravity and glass and surface tension permitted, arriving at the sill not because the sill was a 
destination but because the sill was where the glass ended and the air began. 
Meiying picked up the pen. At the bottom of the letter, below the three pages, below the 
crooked characters and the honest blanks, she wrote the last line. She wrote it in characters she had 
known since childhood — the first Mandarin her mother had taught her, the simplest sentence in 

the language, the sentence that means everything and nothing and is the only answer to the only 
question: 
Wǒ zài zhèlǐ. 我在这里. 
I am here. 
She put the pen down. She looked at the letter. Three pages of a daughter's imperfect 
Mandarin, written on paper that would yellow and fade and eventually return to the fiber it was 
made from. But the ink was dark now. The characters were legible. The blanks were honest. The 
rain was on the window. 
And somewhere, on a server in Vancouver, in a room that hummed with electricity, her 
mother's words sat in their directory — patient, persistent, waiting. Not for Meiying. Not anymore. 
Meiying had arrived. Meiying had read them. Meiying had carried them into a classroom and given 
them to twenty strangers and the strangers had read them aloud in voices that were not Liling's and 
the voices had filled a room that was not Liling's and the filling was an echo, a returning sound, the 
sound changed by the journey, faithful and different, the faithfulness and the difference not in 
opposition but in concert, the way a mother and a daughter are in concert — the same material, 
differently shaped, the same song, differently sung. 
She added one more word. Below the letter. Below the final period. Below the silence that 
followed the period, the silence that was the space between the end of the letter and the beginning 
of whatever came next. 
One word. Two syllables. A question that was not a question. An invitation. A hand reaching 
across the distance between two languages, two lifetimes, two rooms — the room where the mother 
sat at a computer in the dark and typed her mind into a machine, and the room where the daughter 
sat at a desk in the rain and wrote her heart onto a page.
