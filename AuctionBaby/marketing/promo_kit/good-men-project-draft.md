**I Built a Dating App Where Men Bid on the Date — Not the Woman**

Every argument about "who pays on the first date" is an argument about information, not money. Nobody says the number out loud. He doesn't know if she expects dinner or coffee. She doesn't know if he planned a $40 night or a $300 one. Both sides walk in with a number in their head and a prayer that it matches.

I built a dating app — Auction Baby — on a simple thesis: state the number first, and half the fight disappears.

Here's the mechanic. A man posts a bid: the date itself, and what he'll spend making it happen. A taco-crawl night for $60. A jazz bar for $120. A tasting menu for $350. A woman browses the bids — the *plans*, not the prices — and picks the one she wants to go on. She never sees the highest bidder first. She sees the best plan first. She picks the person she likes, whether that's the $80 picnic or the $350 tasting menu.

And here's the sentence I have to say more often than any other: **no money changes hands in the app, ever.** A bid is a promise, not a payment. Nobody is buying anybody. What's being committed is the *date* — the effort, the plan, the evening itself.

The reaction I didn't expect: women telling me the budget isn't even the interesting part. The interesting part is that a plan *exists* before the first message. No six weeks of "hey beautiful." No bait-and-switch from "dinner and cocktails" to "come over, I'll cook." If the bid says gallery night and tacos, the date is gallery night and tacos. Commitment is the artifact, and it's visible before anyone leaves the house.

The men I expected to hate it don't. Pricing the date isn't pricing the woman — it's the opposite of negotiation at the table. He decides what the evening is worth *before* anyone is seated, and states it plainly. Some bid $40 and mean every dollar of effort; some bid $400 and mean it. She picks. The check never becomes a scene, because the scene was never scheduled.

I'm not naïve about the objections, and hear them all: Isn't this just escorting with extra steps? (No — nothing is ever paid to a person; there's no transfer, no invoice, no claim.) Doesn't the highest bid win? (No — that's the whole point. She picks; the bid she likes wins.) Isn't it anti-feminist to have women choose among suitors? (My women users say the choice architecture is the most power they've had on any app: they set nothing, risk nothing, and pick from real plans instead of photos.)

Is it for everyone? No. Swipe apps will always own the infinite-carousel market, and some people want exactly that. Auction Baby is for the people who are tired of the carousel — who'd rather have one good Tuesday night than four hundred matches.

The app is free, it's a web app (works via Add to Home Screen), and there are no ads. I built it because a check arriving at a table shouldn't be the most stressful moment of a first date. State the number. Pick the plan. Enjoy the evening.

A bid is a promise, not a payment.
